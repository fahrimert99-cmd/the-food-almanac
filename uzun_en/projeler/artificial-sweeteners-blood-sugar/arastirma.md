# Research notes: Do artificial sweeteners raise your blood sugar?

Slug: `artificial-sweeteners-blood-sugar`. Researched 8 November 2026.

Access note: NCBI/PMC/PubMed and Nature pages blocked direct fetching in this environment (browser-check pages). Abstract-level facts below were obtained through Europe PMC records (title, authors, journal, summary), WHO's own news page, the ADA press release, and search-result summaries of the papers. Where I could not see an exact figure (for example hazard ratios, glucose-curve AUCs), it is **not used in the script** and is marked "not verified".

## 1. Suez et al., Cell 2022 — sucralose, saccharin, aspartame, stevia and glucose tolerance

- **Citation:** Suez J, Cohen Y, Valdés-Mas R, ... Segal E, Elinav E. Personalized microbiome-driven effects of non-nutritive sweeteners on human glucose tolerance. *Cell* 2022;185(18):3307-3328.e19. DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2022.07.016. PMID 35987213. Europe PMC record: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35987213 (PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35987213/)
- **Design:** randomized controlled trial. 120 healthy adults, selected from more than 1,300 screened people who strictly avoided non-nutritive sweeteners. Six groups: four took sachets of saccharin, sucralose, aspartame or stevia for 2 weeks at doses below the acceptable daily intake; two were control groups (no sweetener). (Cell Press release/Weizmann copy describe "two controls and four" groups.)
- **Outcome:** glycemic response during glucose tolerance tests, measured with continuous glucose monitors.
- **Result:** each sweetener altered stool and oral microbiome and plasma metabolome. Saccharin and sucralose **significantly impaired glycemic responses** at group level ("small but statistically significant elevated glycemic response" vs controls). Aspartame and stevia did not. Responses were person-specific. Germ-free mice given microbiomes from top responders developed glucose changes resembling their human donors; microbiomes from bottom responders largely did not.
- **Not verified:** exact AUC values and between-group differences. Not used.
- **Limitations:** 2 weeks only; healthy adults (not people with diabetes; age distribution not verified, so no claim about older adults); clinical meaning "unknown, merits long-term studies" (authors); mouse part is animal evidence; competing-interests statement: two authors are scientific co-founders of DayTwo (personalized nutrition company).

## 2. Nichol, Holle, An, Eur J Clin Nutr 2018 — meta-analysis of RCTs

- **Citation:** Nichol AD, Holle MJ, An R. Glycemic impact of non-nutritive sweeteners: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Eur J Clin Nutr* 2018;72(6):796-804. DOI 10.1038/s41430-018-0170-6. PMID 29760482. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29760482/
- **Design:** 29 RCTs, 741 participants; aspartame, saccharin, stevioside, sucralose.
- **Result:** "NNS consumption was not found to elevate blood glucose level"; levels declined gradually over the observation period relative to baseline. Effect did not differ by NNS type but varied somewhat with age, body weight and diabetic status.
- **Limitations:** authors call for studies of frequent chronic use and mechanisms; pooled heterogeneous short trials. Exact effect sizes not verified (not used).

## 3. Greyling, Appleton, Raben, Mela, Am J Clin Nutr 2020 — acute effects

- **Citation:** Greyling A, Appleton KM, Raben A, Mela DJ. Acute glycemic and insulinemic effects of low-energy sweeteners: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Am J Clin Nutr* 2020;112(4):1002-1014. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqaa167. PMID 32672338. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32672338/
- **Design:** systematic review/meta-analysis of 26 RCTs (PROSPERO CRD42018099608) of postprandial glucose (PPG) and insulin (PPI) responses.
- **Result:** no statistically significant difference vs control interventions in PPG and PPI overall; exception: in type 2 diabetes, PPG response smaller by mean difference −0.3 mmol/L (95% CI −0.53 to −0.07). In US units, 0.3 mmol/L ≈ 5.4 mg/dL (conversion factor 18; my calculation, narrated as "roughly 5").
- **Limitations:** acute (hours), not long-term; the T2D subgroup is small.

## 4. Stevia trials

- **Iranian RCT (2020):** "Effects of stevia on glycemic and lipid profile of type 2 diabetic patients: A randomized controlled trial." *Avicenna J Phytomed* 2020. PMC7103435, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7103435 . Adults with type 2 diabetes, stevia-sweetened vs sucralose-sweetened tea, 8 weeks, small sample (roughly 34–39 per search summary; exact n and author names not verified). No significant differences in fasting/2-hour glucose, insulin, A1C or lipids between groups (per search-result summary of the abstract). Limitation: very small, comparator was sucralose, not no sweetener.
- A 24-week Egyptian controlled (not double-blind RCT) study reported improvements with stevioside; weaker design; **not used in script** other than the general statement that small stevia trials are mixed.
- Suez 2022 (above): stevia did not change glycemic responses in healthy adults.
- A 2024 review (26 studies, "low certainty") appeared in a search summary only; not opened, not used.

## 5. WHO guideline 2023

- **Citation:** World Health Organization. Use of non-sugar sweeteners: WHO guideline. Geneva; 2023. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK592245/ ; news release 15 May 2023: https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2023-who-advises-not-to-use-non-sugar-sweeteners-for-weight-control-in-newly-released-guideline
- **Recommendation (conditional):** recommends against use of non-sugar sweeteners to control body weight or reduce risk of noncommunicable diseases. Applies to all people **except those with pre-existing diabetes**. Covers synthetic and natural/modified sweeteners (acesulfame K, advantame, aspartame, cyclamates, neotame, saccharin, stevia and derivatives, sucralose). **Does not cover low-calorie sugars and sugar alcohols (polyols)**, which includes erythritol.
- **Evidence summarized by WHO:** no long-term benefit in reducing body fat in adults or children; observational data suggest possible increased risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and mortality with long-term use in adults.
- **Limitations (WHO's own words, paraphrased):** links may be confounded by participants' baseline characteristics and complex patterns of use; recommendation is conditional. Critics (e.g., International Sweeteners Association; Prof Nita Forouhi quoted by Science Media Centre) note most RCTs did not directly compare replacing sugar with sweeteners.

## 6. American Diabetes Association Standards of Care 2025

- Press release: https://diabetes.org/newsroom/press-releases/american-diabetes-association-releases-standards-care-diabetes-2025 . Emphasis on water intake over nutritive and nonnutritive sweetened beverages; use of nonnutritive sweeteners over sugar-sweetened products "in moderation and for the short term" to reduce overall calorie and carbohydrate intake. (Press-release wording; the Standards text itself was not opened.)

## 7. Erythritol and cardiovascular events

- **Citation:** Witkowski M, Nemet I, Alamri H, Wilcox J, Gupta N, Nimer N, Haghikia A, Li XS, Wu Y, Saha PP, Demuth I, König M, Steinhagen-Thiessen E, Cajka T, Fiehn O, Landmesser U, Tang WHW, Hazen SL. The artificial sweetener erythritol and cardiovascular event risk. *Nat Med* 2023;29(3):710-718. DOI 10.1038/s41591-023-02223-9. PMC10275890. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36849732
- **Design/numbers:** discovery cohort of 1,157 patients undergoing cardiac risk assessment (22% with diabetes), 3-year follow-up for major adverse cardiovascular events (death, heart attack, stroke); erythritol most strongly associated. Validated in US cohort n = 2,149 and European cohort n = 833 (stable patients undergoing elective cardiac evaluation). In vitro, erythritol at physiological levels increased platelet reactivity; in animal models it increased thrombosis. Small pilot in healthy volunteers: one 30 g dose raised plasma erythritol for more than 48 hours above levels that enhanced platelet reactivity in vitro (pilot size reported as 8 in a search summary; not verified, so the script says "a small pilot").
- **Hazard ratios:** not verified, not used.
- **Limitations:** observational association in high-risk patients; reverse causality possible (metabolically unwell people may choose sugar-free products or clear erythritol differently); measurement not standardized; in vitro conditions artificial; mouse clotting may not translate; authors call for long-term safety studies. Commentary: Pafili K, Roden M. Signal Transduct Target Ther 2023, doi 10.1038/s41392-023-01504-6.
- **Follow-up:** Witkowski M, et al. Ingestion of the non-nutritive sweetener erythritol, but not glucose, enhances platelet reactivity and thrombosis potential in healthy volunteers. *Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol* 2024. DOI 10.1161/ATVBAHA.124.321014 (DOI as reported in a search summary; confirm). About 20 healthy adults (10 per group per secondary reports), 30 g erythritol vs 30 g glucose in water; platelet reactivity increased after erythritol, not glucose. Short-term lab markers only; no long-term data. Details come from secondary summaries (Healio, News-Medical), not the full paper.
- The institution (Cleveland Clinic) is not stated in the narration to avoid an unverified claim.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- In pooled short-term trials (29 RCTs/741 people; 26 RCTs acute), sweeteners did not raise blood sugar or insulin after a dose; in type 2 diabetes the post-meal rise was slightly smaller (−0.3 mmol/L).
- WHO's 2023 conditional advice against using sweeteners for weight control or disease prevention, in people without diabetes; no long-term body fat benefit.
- ADA: water preferred; sweeteners acceptable as a moderate, short-term swap for sugary products.

**Uncertain**
- Whether saccharin and sucralose worsen glucose handling over longer periods, and in whom (one 2-week trial in 120 healthy adults; person-specific; microbiome mechanism from mice; clinical meaning unknown).
- Stevia in diabetes (small, mixed trials; neutral in the one healthy-adult trial).
- Observational links between sweeteners and diabetes, heart disease and death (confounding, reverse causality).
- Erythritol and heart events/clotting: consistent association plus mechanistic lab data and small short human tests, but no outcome trial.

**False or unsupported**
- "Diet soda spikes blood sugar like sugar does": not supported by the pooled trials.
- "Artificial sweeteners are proven to cause diabetes or heart attacks": not shown; associations only.
- "Sweeteners are a proven weight-loss tool": WHO found no long-term body fat benefit.
- "A diet drink can treat low blood sugar": it contains no sugar (general knowledge, logical consequence).
