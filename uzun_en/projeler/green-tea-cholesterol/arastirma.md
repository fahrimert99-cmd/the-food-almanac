# Research notes: Does green tea really lower cholesterol?

Slug: `green-tea-cholesterol` · researched 10 November 2026

How the sources were read: PubMed and PMC pages blocked automated access, so abstracts were read through the Europe PMC record of the same PMID (identical abstract text). Where only the abstract or only the title was seen, it says so below.

---

## 1. Main meta-analysis: Xu et al. 2020 (31 RCTs)

**Citation.** Xu R, Yang K, Li S, Dai M, Chen G. Effect of green tea consumption on blood lipids: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Nutrition Journal. 2020;19(1):48. doi:10.1186/s12937-020-00557-5. PMID 32434539. PMC7240975.
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7240975/ (full text read) · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32434539/

**Design and population.** Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, searched to September 2019. 31 trials; the abstract says 3,321 subjects, the full text says 3,216 (script says "more than 3,000"). Adults only, mean ages roughly 21 to 69; Western and Asian countries; healthy normal-weight and overweight/obese participants. Trial length 3 weeks to 12 months, median about 11 weeks. Catechin dose 80 to 2,488.7 mg/day, median 630.9 mg/day. 9 comparisons used green tea beverages, 24 used green tea extract capsules.

**Figures (weighted mean difference vs control, random effects).**
- Total cholesterol: −4.66 mg/dL (95% CI −6.36 to −2.96; P < 0.0001)
- LDL cholesterol: −4.55 mg/dL (95% CI −6.31 to −2.80; P < 0.0001)
- HDL cholesterol: no significant effect
- Triglycerides: −3.77 mg/dL (95% CI −8.90 to 1.37; P = 0.15), not significant

**Subgroups (Table 2).**
- Beverage: TC −2.80 (−5.43, −0.16), 9 comparisons; LDL −2.61 (−5.49, 0.27), 8 comparisons. The LDL interval crosses zero, so not statistically significant.
- Capsule/extract: TC −5.41 (−7.46, −3.37), 22 comparisons; LDL −5.28 (−7.44, −3.12), 21 comparisons.
- Western LDL −5.07; Asian LDL −3.98; 12 weeks or longer LDL −4.04; under 12 weeks LDL −4.91.

**Limitations.** Only 9 of 31 trials rated low risk of bias (20 high, 2 unclear). Short follow-up; authors say benefits "cannot be extrapolated beyond the duration of these studies" and that they "could not determine the optimal dosage". Wide variation in dose, product and population. English-language articles only. Outcome is a blood marker, not heart attacks or strokes.

**Mechanisms proposed in the Discussion (not tested by the authors).** Regulation of the SREBP-1 pathway and LDL receptor (from HepG2 human liver cells in culture); catechins as antioxidants limiting LDL oxidation (in vitro); "Green tea might also inhibit intestinal lipid absorption by interfering with micelle formation"; animal studies in high-fat-fed rats. So the mechanism evidence is cell and animal work, and the script says so.

**Own arithmetic used in the script (not from the paper).**
- 4.55 mg/dL out of an LDL of 130 mg/dL = 3.5 percent ("about three and a half percent").
- 9 beverage comparisons out of 33 = 27 percent ("about a quarter").

## 2. Earlier meta-analysis: Zheng et al. 2011 (14 RCTs)

**Citation.** Zheng XX, Xu YL, Li SH, Liu XX, Hui R, Huang XH. Green tea intake lowers fasting serum total and LDL cholesterol in adults: a meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. 2011;94(2):601-610. doi:10.3945/ajcn.110.010926. PMID 21715508.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21715508/ (abstract read)

**Figures.** 14 RCTs, 1,136 adults; green tea beverages or extracts. Total cholesterol −7.20 mg/dL (95% CI −8.19 to −6.21); LDL −2.19 mg/dL (95% CI −3.16 to −1.21); HDL no significant change.

**Limitations.** Abstract only. Mixed beverages and extracts; short trials.

## 3. Largest single trial: Minnesota Green Tea Trial, lipids (Samavat et al. 2016)

**Citation.** Samavat H, Newman AR, Wang R, Yuan JM, Wu AH, Kurzer MS. Effects of green tea catechin extract on serum lipids in postmenopausal women: a randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. 2016;104(6):1671-1682. doi:10.3945/ajcn.116.137075. PMID 27806972. PMC5118731.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27806972/ (abstract read)

**Design and population.** Ancillary analysis of a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. 1,075 healthy postmenopausal women randomized (538 green tea extract, 537 placebo); 936 completed the lipid substudy. Dose: 1,315 mg catechins a day including 843 mg EGCG, in capsules, for 12 months.

**Figures (12-month change, extract vs placebo).**
- Total cholesterol: −2.1% vs +0.7% (P = 0.0004)
- LDL cholesterol: −4.1% vs −0.9% (P < 0.0001)
- Non-HDL cholesterol: −3.1% vs +0.4% (P = 0.0032)
- HDL: no change
- Triglycerides: +3.6% vs −2.5% (P = 0.046), mainly in women with obesity and statin users
- The total cholesterol reduction was significant only in women with baseline TC of 200 mg/dL or higher (P-interaction = 0.01)

**Limitations.** Women only, all postmenopausal; secondary analysis of a breast cancer biomarker trial; capsule extract at a high dose, not brewed tea. Exact age range was not visible in the abstract, so the script says only "postmenopausal women".

## 4. Liver safety in the same trial

**4a. Dostal et al. 2015.** Dostal AM, Samavat H, Bedell S, Torkelson C, Wang R, Swenson K, Le C, Wu AH, Ursin G, Yuan JM, Kurzer MS. The safety of green tea extract supplementation in postmenopausal women at risk for breast cancer: results of the Minnesota Green Tea Trial. Food and Chemical Toxicology. 2015;83:26-35. doi:10.1016/j.fct.2015.05.019. PMID 26051348. PMC4540665.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26051348/ (abstract read)
- 1,075 postmenopausal women, 843 mg EGCG a day or placebo for one year.
- ALT elevation: 36 women (6.7%) on extract vs 4 women (0.7%) on placebo (P < 0.001). Own arithmetic: 6.7% is about 1 in 15.
- Any adverse event 75.6% vs 72.8% (no significant difference); serious adverse events 2.2% vs 1.5%; ALT-related serious adverse events in 1.3% of the extract group. More nausea on extract.
- Authors: extract "is generally well tolerated"; adverse events "mainly mild and transient".

**4b. Yu et al. 2017.** Yu Z, Samavat H, Dostal AM, Wang R, Torkelson CJ, Yang CS, Butler LM, Kensler TW, Wu AH, Kurzer MS, Yuan JM. Effect of green tea supplements on liver enzyme elevation: results from a randomized intervention study in the United States. Cancer Prevention Research. 2017;10(10):571-579. doi:10.1158/1940-6207.CAPR-17-0160. PMID 28765194. PMC9257888.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28765194/ (abstract read)
- 1,021 women with normal baseline liver enzymes (513 extract, 508 placebo).
- ALT rose by 5.4 U/L and AST by 3.8 U/L on extract (both P < 0.001 vs placebo).
- Moderate or more severe liver abnormalities: 26 women (5.1%) on extract; odds ratio 7.0 (95% CI 2.4 to 20.3) vs placebo. Own arithmetic: 5.1% is about 1 in 20.
- "ALT returned to normal after dechallenge and increased again after one or more rechallenges."

**Limitations.** One product, one high dose, women only. Enzyme elevations are a laboratory signal; most were mild and reversible.

## 5. Agency review: EFSA 2018

**Citation.** EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS), Younes M, Aggett P, Aguilar F, et al. Scientific opinion on the safety of green tea catechins. EFSA Journal. 2018;16(4):e05239. doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2018.5239. PMID 32625874. PMC7009618.
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009618/ (abstract and first part of full text read) · news summary https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/180418

**Figures and statements.**
- Green tea is made from the leaves of Camellia sinensis without fermentation; most of its polyphenols are catechins.
- USDA data quoted in the opinion for brewed green tea (1 g leaves per 100 mL water): mean EGCG 70.2 mg/100 g (range 2.3 to 203); EGC 29.2, ECG 17.9, EC 8.3, C 4.5 mg/100 g.
- Mean EGCG intake from green tea infusions: 90 to 300 mg/day; high consumers up to 866 mg/day.
- Supplements provide 5 to 1,000 mg EGCG a day.
- Doses at or above 800 mg EGCG/day from supplements were linked with a statistically significant rise in serum transaminases (liver enzymes), usually in under 10% of people.
- No evidence of raised ALT with infusions at 5 or more cups a day (about 700 mg EGCG/day).
- Infusions "in general considered safe"; rare liver injury cases after infusions are "most probably due to an idiosyncratic reaction".
- No safe dose of extract could be identified (one product caused liver injury at 375 mg EGCG/day).
- Taking extract while fasting raises plasma EGCG compared with taking it with food; other authorities (for example the USP) require a "take with a meal" instruction.

**Own arithmetic used in the script.**
- 70.2 mg/100 g × about 237 g in an 8-ounce cup = about 166 mg EGCG per cup ("roughly 165").
- Sum of the five catechins = 130.1 mg/100 g, about 308 mg per 8-ounce cup. The median trial dose in Xu 2020 (631 mg catechins/day) is therefore about two average cups. Because the measured range is so wide (2.3 to 203 mg/100 g), the script says "about two cups, and more if your tea is weak".
- 843 mg EGCG ÷ 166 mg per cup = about 5 cups (consistent with EFSA's own "5 or more cups, about 700 mg").

**Limitations.** European intake data; the USDA values are averages of very variable brews.

## 6. NIH NCCIH: Green Tea fact page

**Citation.** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NIH). Green Tea: Usefulness and Safety. Last updated February 2025.
URL: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/green-tea (page read)

**Statements.**
- Green tea reduced total and LDL cholesterol "to a small extent"; no effect on HDL or triglycerides.
- Green tea consumption has been linked to lower coronary heart disease risk "in Asian populations but not in Western populations".
- "No safety concerns have been reported for green tea consumed as a beverage by adults."
- Liver injury is uncommon and has been reported "primarily [with] green tea extracts in tablet or capsule form".
- "Green tea at high doses has been shown to reduce blood levels and, therefore, the effectiveness of the drug nadolol."
- "Green tea extract can reduce blood levels of the cholesterol-lowering drug atorvastatin."
- Green tea contains caffeine; advises talking to a health care provider before using green tea supplements with any medicine.

## 7. Cochrane review: Hartley et al. 2013

**Citation.** Hartley L, Flowers N, Holmes J, Clarke A, Stranges S, Hooper L, Rees K. Green and black tea for the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. 2013;(6):CD009934. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD009934.pub2. PMID 23780706.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23780706/ (abstract read)

**Figures.** 11 RCTs, 821 participants (7 green tea, 4 black tea). No trial reported cardiovascular events. Green tea: total cholesterol −0.62 mmol/L, LDL −0.64 mmol/L, but results "not stable in sensitivity analysis" and based on very few small trials. These larger figures are not used in the script because the later, bigger meta-analysis (Xu 2020) found much smaller effects.

**Use in script.** Only the fact that no trial measured heart attacks or strokes.

## 8. Observational evidence: Ohsaki study (Kuriyama et al. 2006)

**Citation.** Kuriyama S, Shimazu T, Ohmori K, Kikuchi N, Nakaya N, Nishino Y, Tsubono Y, Tsuji I. Green tea consumption and mortality due to cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all causes in Japan: the Ohsaki study. JAMA. 2006;296(10):1255-1265. doi:10.1001/jama.296.10.1255. PMID 16968850.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16968850/ (abstract read)

**Figures.** Prospective cohort, 40,530 Japanese adults aged 40 to 79, free of stroke, heart disease and cancer at baseline. Up to 11 years of follow-up for all-cause mortality, up to 7 years for cause-specific mortality. Five or more cups a day vs less than one: all-cause mortality HR 0.88 in men (0.79 to 0.98) and 0.77 in women (0.67 to 0.89); cardiovascular mortality in women HR 0.69 (0.53 to 0.90), which is a 31% lower risk.

**Limitations.** Association, not cause; Japanese population and Japanese tea habits; residual confounding likely. NCCIH notes the link is not seen in Western populations.

## 9. Warfarin

**9a. Case report.** Taylor JR, Wilt VM. Probable antagonism of warfarin by green tea. Annals of Pharmacotherapy. 1999;33(4):426-428. doi:10.1345/aph.18238. PMID 10332534.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10332534/ (abstract read)
- One 44-year-old man on warfarin. INR 3.79 at clinic entry, 1.37 twenty-two days later; he had begun drinking one-half to one gallon of green tea a day about a week before. After stopping green tea, INR rose to 2.55.
- Limitations: a single case; very large volume; authors attribute it to vitamin K.

**9b. Vitamin K in brewed tea.** Booth SL, Madabushi HT, Davidson KW, Sadowski JA. Tea and coffee brews are not dietary sources of vitamin K-1 (phylloquinone). Journal of the American Dietetic Association. 1995;95(1):82-83. doi:10.1016/S0002-8223(95)00018-6. PMID 7798585.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7798585/
- Only the title and indexing were available (no abstract in the record). The script uses only the title's finding: brewed tea is not a meaningful dietary source of vitamin K. No numbers are quoted.

**Script position.** Keep green tea intake steady and tell the anticoagulation clinic before a large change (standard consistency advice for warfarin). No evidence was found for the newer blood thinners, so the script only says "ask your pharmacist".

## 10. Nadolol

**Citation.** Misaka S, Yatabe J, Müller F, Takano K, Kawabe K, Glaeser H, Yatabe MS, Onoue S, Werba JP, Watanabe H, Yamada S, Fromm MF, Kimura J. Green tea ingestion greatly reduces plasma concentrations of nadolol in healthy subjects. Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics. 2014;95(4):432-438. doi:10.1038/clpt.2013.241. PMID 24419562.
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24419562/ (abstract read)

**Figures.** 10 healthy volunteers; 700 mL green tea a day (about 24 fluid ounces, about three 8-ounce cups, own conversion) or water for 14 days, then a single 30 mg nadolol dose. Peak concentration fell 85.3% and total exposure (AUC) 85.0% (P < 0.01); the blood-pressure-lowering effect was reduced.

**Limitations.** Ten healthy volunteers (not older patients), single dose, one drug.

## 11. Caffeine: FDA

**Citation.** US Food and Drug Administration. Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much? Content current as of 28 August 2024.
URL: https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much (page read)

**Figures.** 400 mg a day is "an amount not generally associated with negative effects" for most healthy adults. Per 12 fluid ounces: green tea 37 mg, black tea 71 mg, regular brewed coffee 113 to 247 mg. Sensitivity varies widely; some conditions and medications increase sensitivity. Decaffeinated teas and coffees still contain some caffeine.

## 12. LDL background: MedlinePlus (NIH National Library of Medicine)

**Citation.** MedlinePlus. LDL: The "Bad" Cholesterol. Last updated 22 March 2024.
URL: https://medlineplus.gov/ldlthebadcholesterol.html (page read)

**Statements.** LDL is called "bad" cholesterol "because a high LDL level leads to a buildup of cholesterol in your arteries". For healthy adults: under 100 mg/dL optimal; 100 to 129 near optimal; 130 to 159 borderline high; 160 to 189 high; 190 and above very high. Lowering LDL: heart-healthy eating plan, weight management, physical activity, and medicines including statins.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Green tea catechins lower LDL cholesterol a little in randomized trials: about 4.5 mg/dL across 31 trials (Xu 2020), about 2.2 mg/dL in an earlier pooled analysis (Zheng 2011), and about 3 percentage points more than placebo over a year in 1,075 postmenopausal women (Samavat 2016). HDL does not change.
- High-dose green tea extract (around 800 mg EGCG a day or more) raises liver enzymes in a minority of users: 6.7% vs 0.7% in the Minnesota trial; confirmed by EFSA's review. The changes were mostly mild and reversed on stopping.
- Brewed green tea as a drink is regarded as safe for adults by NIH NCCIH and EFSA.
- Green tea sharply reduced nadolol levels in a small human study.

**Uncertain**
- Whether brewed tea alone, at realistic cups per day, lowers LDL: the beverage-only subgroup showed −2.6 mg/dL and was not statistically significant.
- How many cups are needed: catechin content of a brewed cup varies almost a hundredfold, and no optimal dose has been established.
- Whether green tea prevents heart attacks or strokes: no randomized trial has measured this; cohort data from Japan show an association only, not seen in Western populations.
- The mechanism in people: proposals come from cell and animal experiments.
- The warfarin interaction at ordinary intakes: one case report at half a gallon to a gallon a day; brewed tea contains very little vitamin K.
- Effects in older men specifically.

**False or misleading**
- "Green tea is a natural alternative to statins": false. The LDL change is a few mg/dL and no outcome benefit has been shown.
- "Green tea extract capsules are just as safe as a cup of tea": false. The liver signal is specific to concentrated extract.
- "Green tea improves all your cholesterol numbers": false. HDL does not change, and triglycerides did not fall significantly (they rose slightly in the largest trial).
