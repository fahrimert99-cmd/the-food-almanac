# Research notes: beans, lentils and blood sugar (slug: beans-blood-sugar)

Access note: PubMed, PMC (Jenkins), JAMA Network and Springer pages blocked direct fetching (cookie/verification walls or 404). The numbers below come from the journal abstracts and publisher/university pages as returned by search and fetch tools (EurekAlert press release for the Jenkins 2012 trial; the open-access PMC full text for Hafiz 2022; abstract snippets for Sievenpiper 2009, Jenkins 1982 and Winham 2011). Where a figure came from a snippet rather than a page I read in full, that is flagged.

## 1. Jenkins 2012: legumes in a low-GI diet, type 2 diabetes (randomized trial)
- Citation: Jenkins DJA, Kendall CWC, Augustin LSA, Mitchell S, Sahye-Pudaruth S, Blanco Mejia S, Chiavaroli L, Mirrahimi A, Ireland C, Bashyam B, Vidgen E, de Souza RJ, Sievenpiper JL, Coveney J, Leiter LA, Josse RG. Effect of legumes as part of a low glycemic index diet on glycemic control and cardiovascular risk factors in type 2 diabetes mellitus: a randomized controlled trial. Arch Intern Med. 2012;172(21):1653-1660. PMID 23089999. DOI 10.1001/2013.jamainternmed.70. Registered: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01063361. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23089999/
- Design: parallel-group randomized trial, 3 months, University of Toronto / St. Michael's Hospital team.
- Population: n = 121 adults with type 2 diabetes. (Mean age not confirmed from the sources I could open; do not state an age.)
- Intervention: low glycemic index (GI) diet including at least 1 cup (about 190 g) of legumes a day. Comparison: a healthy, higher-GI diet with more insoluble fiber from whole wheat products.
- Results:
  - HbA1c fell 0.5 (percentage points) on the legume diet and 0.3 on the wheat-fiber diet; between-arm difference -0.2 (95% CI -0.3 to -0.1; P < .001). The -0.5 and -0.3 are within-group changes; -0.2 is the difference between arms.
  - Systolic blood pressure fell 4.5 mm Hg more on the legume diet than on the wheat-fiber diet (press release; I did not see the CI).
  - Calculated coronary heart disease risk score fell 0.8 percent on the legume diet, largely because of the blood pressure change (the abstract wording is ambiguous on whether this is relative to the control arm; the script says "fell 0.8 percent on the legume diet, mostly because of the blood pressure change").
  - Authors' conclusion (EurekAlert): "legume consumption of approximately 190 g per day (1 cup) seems to contribute usefully to a low-GI diet."
- Limitations: legumes were tested as part of a low-GI diet, so the legume effect cannot be separated from the low-GI pattern; the CHD figure is a calculated risk score, not heart attacks; 3 months only; one research group; an accompanying commentary noted that the legume group also ate more fiber.
- Sources read: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/759746 ; search summaries of the abstract.

## 2. Sievenpiper 2009: non-oil-seed pulses and glycemic control (systematic review and meta-analysis)
- Citation: Sievenpiper JL, et al. Effect of non-oil-seed pulses on glycaemic control: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled experimental trials in people with and without diabetes. Diabetologia. 2009;52(8):1479-1495. DOI 10.1007/s00125-009-1395-7. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00125-009-1395-7
- Design: controlled trials of at least 7 days; 41 trials (39 reports) included.
- Results (standardized mean differences, not mg/dL): pulses alone, 11 trials, lowered fasting blood glucose (-0.82, 95% CI -1.36 to -0.27) and fasting insulin (-0.49, 95% CI -0.93 to -0.04). Pulses in low-GI diets, 19 trials, lowered glycated blood proteins (HbA1c or fructosamine): -0.28 (95% CI -0.42 to -0.14). Pulses in high-fiber diets, 11 trials, lowered fasting glucose (-0.32) and glycated proteins (-0.27).
- Limitations: inter-study heterogeneity was high and unexplained for most outcomes; the authors called for larger, better trials.
- Source: abstract via search summary (not the full text).

## 3. Hafiz 2022: pulse consumption, acute and long-term RCTs (systematic review and meta-analysis)
- Citation: Hafiz MS, Campbell MD, O'Mahoney LL, Holmes M, Orfila C, Boesch C. Pulse consumption improves indices of glycemic control in adults with and without type 2 diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of acute and long-term randomized controlled trials. Eur J Nutr. 2022 (online 2021). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8854292
- Design: 65 RCTs in the review, 59 in the meta-analysis (37 acute, 28 long-term); 2,102 participants (905 with type 2 diabetes, 1,197 without).
- Results in adults with type 2 diabetes, long-term trials: fasting glucose lower by 0.54 mmol/L (95% CI -0.83 to -0.24; 10 RCTs; I-squared 78%), which is about 10 mg/dL (0.54 x 18). HbA1c lower by 0.17 (95% CI -0.33 to 0.00; 6 RCTs; I-squared 78%), which is borderline. Prediction intervals for both (-1.44 to 0.37 for fasting glucose; -0.69 to 0.36 for HbA1c) include no effect.
- Limitations: GRADE certainty "very low" for long-term fasting glucose and HbA1c; high heterogeneity; ten trials at high risk of bias; fragile results (removing one trial changed one result); pulse type, dose and duration varied widely, so no dose could be recommended; trials of pulses inside broader diets (low-GI, high-fiber) were excluded, so Jenkins 2012 is not part of this analysis. The paper's own discussion calls the HbA1c drop "about 0.3%", which does not match the pooled 0.17; the script uses 0.17.
- Source: PMC full text (first ~100,000 characters read).

## 4. Jenkins 1982: the "lentil effect" / second meal effect (small crossover study)
- Citation: Jenkins DJ, et al. Slow release dietary carbohydrate improves second meal tolerance. Am J Clin Nutr. 1982;35(6):1339-1346. (Volume and pages from the search result; PubMed page not opened. Search PubMed for the exact title.)
- Design: 7 healthy volunteers ate breakfasts of lentils or wholemeal (whole wheat) bread with identical carbohydrate content; a standard bread lunch followed 4 hours later.
- Result: after the lentil breakfast the blood glucose response to the lunch was flatter by 38% (p < 0.01). Feeding the bread breakfast slowly over the 4 hours before lunch mimicked the pattern, suggesting slow carbohydrate release is the cause.
- Naming: first called the "lentil effect"; later the "second meal effect" (Wolever et al., Am J Clin Nutr 1988, seen only as a secondary citation; not used for any number).
- Limitations: 7 healthy people; one meal pair; healthy not diabetic; 1982 methods; response measured as glucose rise, not A1C. Not specific to older adults.
- Source: abstract via search summary.

## 5. Winham and Hutchins 2011: does eating beans cause lasting gas? (pooled feeding trials)
- Citation: Winham DM, Hutchins AM. Perceptions of flatulence from bean consumption among adults in 3 feeding studies. Nutr J. 2011;10:128. https://nutritionj.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1475-2891-10-128
- Design: three feeding trials in which adults ate half a cup of beans daily for 8 or 12 weeks; participants reported flatulence and discomfort.
- Result: early on, the share reporting more gas varied by bean type, then declined steadily; by week 8 only 3% still reported increased flatulence. A small share (3 to 11%) reported more gas even on control diets without bean-type foods. Authors concluded concern about gas may be exaggerated.
- Limitations: self-reported; small feeding trials, mostly not in people with diabetes; the figures were read from the abstract via search summary, not from the full text.

## 6. Practical and agency context (secondary)
- American Diabetes Association "diabetes superstar foods" page names beans among foods to include; beans count as both a carbohydrate and a protein food; a half cup of cooked beans or lentils is roughly 15 to 20 grams of carbohydrate with several grams of fiber. I could not open the English ADA page; the carbohydrate and fiber figures came from secondary summaries of ADA material, so the script says "roughly" and gives no exact fiber number.
- Starting amount and rinsing: The Bean Institute suggests starting with 2 to 4 tablespoons a day and building to half a cup, and rinsing canned beans. This is practical advice, not a trial result. Canned beans' sodium varies (about 10 to 330 mg per half cup per DaVita); draining and rinsing helps reduce sodium (Optum, Healthline); no percentage figure is available, so the script gives none.
- National Kidney Foundation (https://www.kidney.org/atoz/content/beans): most people with chronic kidney disease who are not on dialysis usually do not need to limit beans for potassium or phosphorus, but people with higher lab levels or on dialysis should work with a kidney dietitian. Many beans are high in oxalate (relevant to calcium oxalate stones).

## Honest verdict
Well supported:
- In a 3-month randomized trial of 121 adults with type 2 diabetes, a low-GI diet with about a cup of legumes a day lowered HbA1c by 0.5 points, 0.2 points more than a high wheat-fiber diet, and lowered systolic blood pressure by about 4.5 mm Hg more.
- Pooled trials point in the same direction: lower fasting glucose and slightly lower HbA1c with pulses.
- Beans do not cause lasting gas for most people in feeding trials: the complaint fades over weeks.

Uncertain:
- The size of the effect. The improvement beyond a good high-fiber diet is modest (about 0.2 HbA1c points), the certainty of the pooled long-term evidence was graded "very low," and prediction intervals include no effect.
- Whether beans themselves or the low-GI pattern deserve the credit in the Jenkins trial.
- The second meal effect comes from a 7-person healthy-volunteer study from 1982; later work is supportive but I did not verify it in older adults.
- Whether results differ in adults over 60; none of the sources I could open singled this group out.

False or misleading:
- "Beans are a free food that won't raise blood sugar." They contain starch and count as carbohydrate; they raise blood sugar more slowly, not zero.
- "Beans can replace diabetes medicine." Nothing in the evidence supports this.
- "Canned beans don't count." They are cooked beans; the issue is sodium, handled by choosing no-salt-added or rinsing.
