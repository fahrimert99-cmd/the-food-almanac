## Claim 1: COSMOS, the large trial of cocoa extract in older US adults
- **Citation.** Sesso HD, Manson JE, Aragaki AK, Rist PM, Johnson LG, Friedenberg G, Copeland T, Clar A, Mora S, Moorthy MV, Sarkissian A, Carrick WR, Anderson GL; COSMOS Research Group. Effect of cocoa flavanol supplementation for the prevention of cardiovascular disease events: the COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study (COSMOS) randomized clinical trial. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2022;115(6):1490-1500. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqac055. PMID 35294962. PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170467/ . Registration NCT02422745.
- **Design.** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 2x2 factorial trial (cocoa extract and multivitamin; this report covers cocoa extract). Placebo run-in of at least 2 months, excluding poor compliers (under 75 percent of pills).
- **Population.** n = 21,442 US adults: 12,666 women aged 65 and older and 8,776 men aged 60 and older, free of major cardiovascular disease and recent cancer. Mean age 72.1 years. Cocoa extract n = 10,719; placebo n = 10,723.
- **Intervention.** 2 capsules a day providing 500 mg cocoa flavanols including 80 mg (-)-epicatechin (also roughly 50 mg theobromine and 15 mg caffeine). Median follow-up 3.6 years.
- **Results (intention to treat).**
  - Primary outcome, total cardiovascular events (a broad composite that also includes unstable angina and carotid/peripheral surgery): 410 events with cocoa extract versus 456 with placebo; HR 0.90 (95% CI 0.78 to 1.02), P = 0.11. Not significant.
  - Cardiovascular death (secondary): HR 0.73 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.98), i.e. 27 percent lower, with the interval running from 2 to 46 percent lower. The only secondary endpoint reaching nominal significance.
  - Myocardial infarction HR 0.87 (0.66 to 1.16); stroke HR 0.91 (0.70 to 1.17); coronary revascularization HR 0.95 (0.77 to 1.17). None significant.
  - All-cause mortality HR 0.89 (0.77 to 1.03), not significant.
  - Per-protocol (censoring at non-adherence, inverse-probability weighted): total CVD HR 0.85 (0.72 to 0.99).
  - Adverse events: authors reported no safety concerns; nausea slightly more common with extract (HR 1.06, 1.02 to 1.11).
- **Funding.** Mars Edge (a segment of Mars) gave an investigator-initiated grant and donated study pills and packaging; stated to have had no role in design, conduct, analysis or manuscript preparation (it supported blinded assays completed independently). Pfizer Consumer Healthcare (now GSK) donated multivitamin pills. NIH grants also supported COSMOS.
- **Limitations (authors).** Broad primary composite; whole extract tested, so components can't be separated; one dose only; no adjustment for multiple testing, so secondary, subgroup and per-protocol results are hypothesis-generating; per-protocol result rests on extra assumptions; short follow-up; COVID-19 affected event rates and led to broadening the primary outcome; limited racial and ethnic diversity and volunteer, mail-based recruitment.
- **Not used.** The 39 percent "per-protocol cardiovascular death" figure from press coverage (not seen in the paper text I read).
- **Relevance to the topic.** The intervention was a capsule extract with a measured flavanol dose, not chocolate.

## Claim 2: Cocoa and blood pressure (pooled short trials)
- **Citation.** Ried K, Fakler P, Stocks NP. Effect of cocoa on blood pressure. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2017;4:CD008893. DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD008893.pub3. Plain-language summary read at https://www.cochrane.org/CD008893/HTN_effect-cocoa-blood-pressure (Cochrane Library page blocked).
- **Design/population.** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 randomized controlled trials (40 treatment comparisons), 1,804 mainly healthy participants. Duration 2 to 18 weeks, average about 9 weeks.
- **Results.** Systolic BP -1.76 mmHg (95% CI -3.09 to -0.43; P = 0.009; 40 comparisons). Diastolic BP -1.76 mmHg (95% CI -2.57 to -0.94; P < 0.001; 39 comparisons, 1,772 participants). Evidence quality moderate (downgraded for unexplained variation between trials). Search-result text of the abstract also states a larger effect in (pre)hypertensive than normotensive participants.
- **Side effects.** Digestive complaints and dislike of the product in 1 percent of cocoa groups versus 0.4 percent of controls.
- **Limitations.** Short trials; no trial measured long-term daily use or outcomes such as heart attack or stroke; review not restricted to older adults (population "mainly healthy"); heterogeneity.

## Claim 3: Alkalization (Dutch processing) removes most flavanols
- **Citation.** Miller KB, Hurst WJ, Payne MJ, Stuart DA, Apgar J, Sweigart DS, Ou B. Impact of alkalization on the antioxidant and flavanol content of commercial cocoa powders. *J Agric Food Chem.* 2008;56(18):8527-8533. DOI 10.1021/jf801670p. PMID 18710243. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18710243/
- **Design.** Laboratory analysis of commercial natural and alkalized cocoa powders grouped by extractable pH. Not a human study.
- **Results.** Average total flavanols: 34.6 mg/g (natural), 13.8 mg/g (lightly processed), 7.8 mg/g (medium), 3.9 mg/g (heavily processed). Heavy versus natural = about 89 percent lower. Antioxidant capacity and polyphenols fell as pH rose. Red and brown/black powders showed similar declines.
- **Limitations.** Cocoa powders, not finished chocolate bars; 2008 market sample; the flavanol content of a given bar today is not known from this study.

## Claim 4: FDA qualified health claim (2023) is limited to high-flavanol cocoa powder
- **Source.** FDA constituent update, "FDA Announces Qualified Health Claim for Cocoa Flavanols in High Flavanol Cocoa Powder and Reduced Risk of Cardiovascular Disease" (February 2023): https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-announces-qualified-health-claim-cocoa-flavanols-high-flavanol-cocoa-powder-and-reduced-risk
- **Content.** Permitted wording: "Cocoa flavanols in high flavanol cocoa powder may reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease," with a disclosure that FDA found very limited evidence. Product must contain at least 4 percent naturally conserved cocoa flavanols. The claim does not cover regular cocoa powder, foods containing regular cocoa powder, or other cacao products such as chocolate.

## Claim 5: Label rule on alkali
- **Source.** 21 CFR 163.112(c)(1): if an alkali ingredient is used in cocoa, the name of the food must be accompanied by "Processed with alkali" or "Processed with ____" naming the alkali. https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-163/subpart-B/section-163.112
- **Limit.** This section covers cocoa products (breakfast cocoa). I did not verify how alkali must appear on finished chocolate bar labels; the script only advises looking for the phrase in the ingredient list.

## Claim 6: Lead and cadmium in dark chocolate bars
- **Source.** Consumer Reports, December 2022 test of 28 dark chocolate bars: https://www.consumerreports.org/health/food-safety/lead-and-cadmium-in-dark-chocolate-a8480295550/
- **Findings (as read).** Cadmium and lead detected in all 28 bars. 23 bars would put an adult over a level that public health authorities and CR experts say may be harmful for at least one metal at one ounce a day; 5 bars exceeded the levels for both metals. Five bars were relatively low in both (one each Mast, Taza, Valrhona; two Ghirardelli); 8 lower only in lead, 10 only in cadmium. High in both: one each Green & Black's, Lily's, Trader Joe's; two Theo.
- **Advice from CR.** Eat dark chocolate occasionally, not daily; a few servings a week of a lower-level product; when a bar is untested, a lower cacao percentage such as 70 over 80; organic was not safer; milk chocolate generally lower in metals but higher in sugar; children and pregnant people should limit intake. One ounce of even the highest-level bar is unlikely to cause immediate harm; risk comes from frequent consumption over time.
- **Sources of metals.** Cadmium is taken up from soil into the beans as the tree grows; lead mostly lands on the outside of the beans after harvest, from dust while beans dry.
- **Industry response.** The National Confectioners Association said the California benchmark used in such analyses is not a food-safety standard and that products comply with strict safety requirements (seen in search-result summaries of trade-press coverage, not on a page I opened; wording kept general in the script).
- **Limitations.** A consumer-organization test of 28 products, not a peer-reviewed study; thresholds are precautionary benchmarks, not US federal limits; brand levels can change by batch and year.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- Flavanol-rich cocoa products lower blood pressure by a small amount, about 2 mmHg on average, in short trials in mainly healthy adults (Cochrane, moderate-quality evidence).
- Alkali (Dutch) processing removes most flavanols from cocoa powder: heavily processed powders had about 89 percent fewer in the Miller analysis.
- Dark chocolate bars sold in the US can contain measurable lead and cadmium; Consumer Reports found both in all 28 bars tested, with large brand-to-brand variation.
- The FDA's only cocoa-flavanol heart claim is limited, hedged and does not cover chocolate.

**Uncertain**
- Whether cocoa flavanols prevent heart disease. COSMOS (older adults, 500 mg flavanols a day as capsules) missed significance for total events (HR 0.90, CI 0.78 to 1.02); the 27 percent lower cardiovascular death result was a secondary, unadjusted finding. The per-protocol total-events result (HR 0.85) is hypothesis-generating.
- Whether the COSMOS results apply to chocolate bars (flavanol content unknown and varies with processing).
- Whether a small blood pressure effect translates into fewer heart attacks or strokes. No trial in the Cochrane review measured those.
- How often eating dark chocolate is safe given the metals; benchmarks are precautionary.

**False or misleading**
- "Dark chocolate prevents heart attacks." No trial shows that.
- "The higher the cacao percentage, the more flavanols (or the safer)." Cacao percentage does not report flavanol survival after processing, and Consumer Reports advises choosing a lower percentage (70 over 80) when a bar is untested.
- "Organic dark chocolate has less lead and cadmium." Not in Consumer Reports' tests.
- Press headlines that COSMOS "proved" cocoa cuts heart disease deaths.
