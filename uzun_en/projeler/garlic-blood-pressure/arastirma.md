# Research notes: garlic and blood pressure

Slug: garlic-blood-pressure. Pages were opened on 2026-10-16 (abstract or page text). PubMed pages were blocked by a cookie wall, so abstracts were read through Europe PMC records and PMC full text. The Europe PMC and PMC fetches returned summarized abstracts; exact figures below are those reported.

## 1. Meta-analysis, all participants and hypertensive subgroup
- **Claim:** Garlic supplements lowered blood pressure versus placebo; larger effect in people with hypertension.
- **Figures:** 20 randomized trials, 970 participants. Systolic about −5.1 mm Hg and diastolic about −2.5 mm Hg overall. In participants hypertensive at baseline: systolic about −8.7 mm Hg and diastolic about −6.1 mm Hg.
- **Design:** Updated meta-analysis and review of RCTs of garlic preparations versus placebo (humans).
- **Source:** Ried K. Garlic lowers blood pressure in hypertensive individuals, regulates serum cholesterol, and stimulates immunity: an updated meta-analysis and review. J Nutr. 2016;146(2):389S-396S. PMID 26764326. DOI 10.3945/jn.114.202192. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26764326/
- **Limitations:** Single author who has run garlic trials; mixed products and doses; the abstract concludes long-term trials are still needed for cardiovascular events and mortality. I read a summary of the abstract only (full text not opened), so confidence intervals are not recorded.

## 2. Meta-analysis, hypertension only
- **Figures:** 12 RCTs (553 participants with hypertension) for systolic; 8 trials (374 participants) for diastolic. Systolic −8.32 ± 1.93 mm Hg, diastolic −5.48 ± 1.92 mm Hg (both P<0.00001), random-effects, mean differences at longest follow-up. Search Medline 1955 to December 2018.
- **Preparations (all supplements):** Kwai garlic powder 600–900 mg/day (7.8–11.7 mg alliin), 12–24 weeks; Allicor garlic powder 600 mg/day or 2,400 mg/day, 8–12 weeks; a Japanese garlic powder with egg yolk 188 mg/day, 12 weeks; Kyolic aged garlic extract (AGE) 480, 960 or 1,200 mg/day, 12 weeks (2.4 mg S-allylcysteine at 960 mg).
- **Source:** Ried K. Garlic lowers blood pressure in hypertensive subjects, improves arterial stiffness and gut microbiota: a review and meta-analysis. Exp Ther Med. 2019;19(2):1472-1478. DOI 10.3892/etm.2019.8374. PMID 32010325. PMC6966103. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6966103
- **Limitations:** Same author as the 2016 review, overlapping trials, so it is an update and not an independent confirmation. Author was chief investigator of four included trials. Small trials (about 6 to 50 per arm), unclear risk of bias in several (attrition up to 27% in one group). Heterogeneous products pooled. The paper's "16–40% fewer cardiovascular events" is extrapolated from antihypertensive drug trials, not measured in garlic trials (not used in the script). The manufacturer of Kyolic sponsored the author's travel to a symposium. Every preparation listed in the paper was a supplement (powder or aged extract); no raw or cooked kitchen garlic trial is listed.

## 3. Randomized trial, treated but uncontrolled hypertension
- **Figures:** 50 general practice patients with treated but uncontrolled hypertension; aged garlic extract 960 mg/day (four capsules, 2.4 mg S-allylcysteine) or matching placebo for 12 weeks, as add-on to existing medication. In patients with baseline systolic ≥140 mm Hg, systolic was 10.2 ± 4.3 mm Hg lower than controls over 12 weeks (p=0.03). No significant difference in those below 140. 92% found treatment acceptable.
- **Design:** Double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel RCT (humans).
- **Source:** Ried K, Frank OR, Stocks NP. Aged garlic extract lowers blood pressure in patients with treated but uncontrolled hypertension: a randomised controlled trial. Maturitas. 2010;67(2):144-150. PMID 20594781. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20594781/
- **Limitations:** n=50; effect found in the subgroup with baseline systolic ≥140; one product; the authors' claim of being "comparable to first-line medications" is a cross-study comparison, not a head-to-head trial.

## 4. Dose-response trial
- **Figures:** 79 patients with uncontrolled systolic hypertension, add-on to antihypertensive medication, 12 weeks. One, two or four capsules daily (240, 480, 960 mg; 0.6, 1.2, 2.4 mg S-allylcysteine) or placebo. Two capsules: systolic −11.8 ± 5.4 mm Hg versus placebo (P=0.006). Four capsules: −7.4 ± 4.1 mm Hg at 8 weeks, borderline (P=0.07). One capsule and diastolic readings: no significant difference. Tolerability and acceptability 93%.
- **Design:** Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled dose-response RCT (humans).
- **Source:** Ried K, Frank OR, Stocks NP. Aged garlic extract reduces blood pressure in hypertensives: a dose-response trial. Eur J Clin Nutr. 2013;67(1):64-70 (online 2012). PMID 23169470. DOI 10.1038/ejcn.2012.178. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23169470/
- **Limitations:** Small groups; same research team and same product as source 3; short duration.

## 5. Crushing, alliinase and heat
- **Figures:** In rats, 60 seconds of microwave heating or 45 minutes of oven heating of garlic eliminated its ability to block binding of a mammary carcinogen (DMBA) to mammary cell DNA. Letting crushed garlic stand 10 minutes before 60 seconds of microwaving largely preserved the activity. Loss of activity matched inactivation of the enzyme alliinase. The abstract describes allyl sulfur compounds as the main constituents of crushed garlic.
- **Design:** Animal (rat) and lab study; outcome is anticancer-related DNA binding, not blood pressure.
- **Source:** Song K, Milner JA. The influence of heating on the anticancer properties of garlic. J Nutr. 2001;131(3s):1054S-1057S. DOI 10.1093/jn/131.3.1054S. PMID 11238815. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11238815/
- **Limitations:** Not a human or blood pressure study. The kitchen tip "crush and wait about ten minutes" is a reasonable inference from enzyme biology only; whether it changes blood pressure effects is untested. The general chemistry (crushing lets alliinase act on alliin to form allicin, the first of the allyl sulfur compounds) is standard biochemistry; it is consistent with the product descriptions in source 2 (alliin content, "allicin potential") and with source 5, but I did not open a dedicated chemistry source.

## 6. Agency page: effect size, safety and bleeding
- **Statements (NCCIH, NIH):** "Limited evidence suggests that garlic supplements may reduce blood pressure to a small extent in people who have high blood pressure." Garlic taken orally has been used safely in research studies lasting as long as 7 years. Side effects include breath and body odor, abdominal pain, flatulence and nausea; some people are allergic. "Taking garlic supplements may increase the risk of bleeding." People taking anticoagulants, aspirin or other medicines should talk with their health care provider before using garlic. Fresh raw garlic used on the skin can cause severe irritation and chemical burns.
- **Source:** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health. Garlic. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/garlic
- **Limitations:** Consumer fact sheet; the ODS professional fact sheet returned an error and was not used.

## 7. Warfarin safety trial
- **Figures:** 66 screened, 52 randomized, 48 completed (mean age 56 ± 10 years) on stable warfarin. 5 mL aged garlic extract or placebo twice daily for 12 weeks. "There was no evidence of increased hemorrhage in either the placebo or the AGE group." Adverse events did not differ between groups.
- **Design:** Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled pilot (humans).
- **Source:** Macan H, Uykimpang R, Alconcel M, Takasu J, Razon R, Amagase H, Niihara Y. Aged garlic extract may be safe for patients on warfarin therapy. J Nutr. 2006;136(3 Suppl):793S-795S. PMID 16484565. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16484565/
- **Limitations:** Pilot; one liquid product; authors include the manufacturer's researchers (Amagase); INR values not in abstract. Source 2 notes that raw garlic should not be assumed to behave the same way.

## 8. Benchmark: DASH diet and sodium
- **Figures:** 412 participants randomized to a typical US control diet or the DASH diet, each eating high, intermediate and low sodium for 30 days in random order. DASH plus low sodium gave systolic 7.1 mm Hg lower than control with high sodium in participants without hypertension and 11.5 mm Hg lower in those with hypertension.
- **Design:** Randomized controlled feeding trial (humans).
- **Source:** Sacks FM, et al. Effects on blood pressure of reduced dietary sodium and the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet. N Engl J Med. 2001;344(1):3-10. PMID 11136953. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11136953/
- **Limitations:** Controlled feeding for 30-day periods; a different design from the garlic supplement trials, so comparing the numbers is only a rough sense of scale.

## 9. Why a few points matter (drug trials)
- **Figures:** In a meta-analysis of 147 randomized trials of blood-pressure-lowering drugs, a reduction of 10 mm Hg systolic (or 5 mm Hg diastolic) was associated with 22% fewer coronary heart disease events (95% CI 17–27%) and 41% fewer strokes (95% CI 33–48%).
- **Source:** Law MR, Morris JK, Wald NJ. Use of blood pressure lowering drugs in the prevention of cardiovascular disease: meta-analysis of 147 randomised trials in the context of expectations from prospective epidemiological studies. BMJ. 2009;338:b1665. PMID 19454737. PMC2684577. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2684577
- **Limitations:** The figures come from drug trials, not garlic. Garlic trials measured blood pressure only, not heart attacks or strokes.

## Honest verdict
**Reasonably well supported:**
- In randomized trials of garlic supplements (aged extract and standardized powders), people with high blood pressure had lower readings than on placebo, on the order of 8 to 9 mm Hg systolic and 5 to 6 mm Hg diastolic in pooled analyses.
- In the one dose-response trial, a mid dose worked and higher did not do better.
- Garlic is generally well tolerated; the common effects are odor and stomach upset.

**Uncertain:**
- How reliable the effect is. The reviews are by one author and the largest trials came from one team using one product. Trials are small and products differ.
- Whether it changes heart attacks, strokes or deaths. Not measured.
- Whether raw or cooked kitchen garlic does anything for blood pressure. The reviewed trials tested supplements.
- Whether crushing and waiting ten minutes helps blood pressure. Enzyme biology and a rat study only.
- How safe supplements are with blood thinners. One small pilot with aged extract in warfarin patients found no extra bleeding, NCCIH still warns of possible bleeding risk.

**False or unsupported:**
- That garlic replaces blood pressure medicine. In the trials it was an add-on.
- That one clove a day is proven to lower blood pressure.
- That garlic is as strong as prescription drugs in a head-to-head sense. No such trial was found.
