# Research notes: hydration after 60 (slug: hydration-after-60)

Question: Why does thirst fade after 60, and how much water do you really need?

How each source was checked: pages marked "opened" were fetched and read (PubMed record, official page or a news report of the paper). Where I could not open the primary page, the entry says so under "limitations".

## 1. Thirst is blunted in healthy older men

**Claim.** After 24 hours without water, healthy older men were less thirsty and drank less than young men, even though their plasma osmolality, sodium and vasopressin had risen more.

- Design: physiology experiment with a young comparison group. 24 hours of water deprivation.
- Population: 7 healthy, active men aged 67 to 75 and 7 healthy men aged 20 to 31, matched for weight loss during deprivation.
- Findings (abstract):
  - After deprivation the older men had larger increases in plasma osmolality, sodium concentration and vasopressin.
  - Their urine was less concentrated, they felt less thirsty and drank less, so plasma and urine did not return to pre-deprivation levels.
  - The authors concluded there is "a deficit in thirst and water intake in healthy elderly men" while vasopressin responsiveness is maintained or even increased. They suggested the age-related loss of urine concentrating ability is kidney-related, not due to missing vasopressin.
- Source: Phillips PA, Rolls BJ, Ledingham JG, Forsling ML, Morton JJ, Crowe MJ, Wollner L. Reduced thirst after water deprivation in healthy elderly men. N Engl J Med. 1984;311(12):753-759. doi:10.1056/NEJM198409203111202. PMID 6472364. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6472364/ (opened)
- Limitations: n = 14, men only, healthy and active; experimental dehydration, not everyday life; 1984. The abstract does not give the osmolality or sodium values, so the script uses no such numbers. An average group difference, not a rule for every person.

## 2. Review: thirst and fluid intake across ages

**Claim.** Older adults usually drink enough day to day; under fluid deprivation, a hyperosmotic stimulus or exercise in the heat they feel less thirst, drink less and restore fluid balance more slowly.

- Source: Kenney WL, Chiu P. Influence of age on thirst and fluid intake. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 2001;33(9):1524-1532. doi:10.1097/00005768-200109000-00016. https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/influence-of-age-on-thirst-and-fluid-intake/ (abstract opened via the Penn State portal; the PubMed page itself was not retrieved)
- Type: narrative review. No pooled numbers used.
- Limitations: narrative review, not a systematic one; much of the underlying work is small and lab-based.

## 3. Thirst is not a reliable sign of dehydration in older people (Cochrane)

**Claim.** In a Cochrane diagnostic review of dehydration in older people, "feeling thirsty" was among the single signs that should not be relied on alone. No single simple test was consistently useful; combining signs might help.

- Design: Cochrane systematic review of diagnostic accuracy; 3 studies with published diagnostic data plus 21 studies whose raw datasets the authors obtained (24 in total). Reference standard: serum osmolality of 295 mOsm/kg or more.
- Source: Hooper L, et al. Clinical symptoms, signs and tests for identification of impending and current water-loss dehydration in older people. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2015;(4):CD009647. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD009647.pub2. PMID 25924806. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25924806/ (opened; only the first 100,000 characters of the page were read)
- Limitations: the abstract did not give thirst-specific sensitivity and specificity, so the script says only that thirst alone is unreliable. Urine color is named in the list of single tests I saw in a secondary summary of the same review; the script says only "each was unreliable alone", so treat the color claim as secondary-source-based. Older review (2015).

## 4. The kidney's ability to concentrate urine falls with age

**Claim.** In healthy volunteers, after 12 hours of dehydration, urine osmolality fell significantly with advancing age. The fall did not correlate with the age-related fall in creatinine clearance. The ability to conserve solute also fell with age, which may reduce the gradient the kidney needs to concentrate urine.

- Design: cross-sectional physiology study.
- Population: 98 active, community-dwelling volunteers aged 20 to 79 with no known kidney-affecting disease.
- Source: Rowe JW, Shock NW, DeFronzo RA. The influence of age on the renal response to water deprivation in man. Nephron. 1976;17(4):270-278. doi:10.1159/000180731. PMID 951013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/951013/ (opened)
- Limitations: 1976; the abstract gives no osmolality values (none used); a single 12-hour test in healthy people; cross-sectional, so ages are compared rather than the same people followed. Population-level patterns differ (a 2019 NHANES-based paper noted in a search result found age-related declines in the US, but not in two forager groups), so this is not universal. Not used in the script.

## 5. US National Academies guidance on total water

**Claim and figures.**
- Total water, adequate intake: about 2.7 liters (91 ounces) a day for adult women and about 3.7 liters (125 ounces) for adult men, from all beverages and foods. These are averages among adequately hydrated people and cover healthy, sedentary people in temperate climates.
- About 80 percent of total water comes from drinking water and beverages; about 20 percent from food.
- Beverages that count: water, juice, milk, coffee, tea, soda, plus fruits, vegetables and other foods. "Caffeinated beverages can contribute as much as noncaffeinated options."
- The panel set no upper limit for water. Very active people or those in hot climates may need more.
- The panel said people can meet needs by letting thirst guide them and by habitual drinks at meals.
- My arithmetic (not in the source): 80 percent of 91 ounces is about 73 ounces, roughly 9 cups; 80 percent of 125 ounces is 100 ounces, roughly 12 cups. The script says "roughly" and flags this as simple arithmetic.
- Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (then Institute of Medicine). Dietary Reference Intakes for Water, Potassium, Sodium, Chloride, and Sulfate. Washington, DC: National Academies Press; 2005. Report news release: https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/report-sets-dietary-intake-levels-for-water-salt-and-potassium-to-maintain-health-and-reduce-chronic-disease-risk (opened)
- Limitations: I read the National Academies release, not the full 2005 report. The release does not state age-specific values or mention older adults, so the script says only "adult men" and "adult women". Adequate intake is an observed average, not a requirement. The age caveat (that thirst may be a weaker guide after 60) is my synthesis of sources 1 to 3, not a National Academies statement; the script words it that way.

## 6. Heart failure: fluid restriction (FRESH-UP)

**Figures.**
- Design: randomized clinical trial, 504 people with chronic heart failure (mean age 69, 33 percent women, mostly NYHA class II), most on guideline-directed therapy. Assigned to a maximum of 1,500 mL a day or no restriction, for 3 months. People with unstable heart failure, recent hospitalization or low sodium were excluded.
- Reported intake: median 1,764 mL a day with no restriction versus 1,488 mL a day restricted.
- Quality of life (KCCQ overall summary) at 3 months: 74.0 versus 72.2, P = 0.056 (adjusted mean difference 2.17, 95 percent CI −0.06 to 4.39), not statistically significant. Thirst distress was significantly higher in the restricted group. No differences in mortality, hospitalizations or other safety measures reported.
- Sources: Herrmann JJ, Brunner-La Rocca HP, Baltussen LE, et al. Liberal fluid intake versus fluid restriction in chronic heart failure (FRESH-UP randomized clinical trial). Nature Medicine. 2025. Read through the TCTMD news report: https://www.tctmd.com/news/tight-fluid-restriction-not-needed-chronic-hf-patients-fresh (opened). A trial-design page was also seen in search results.
- Guideline context: the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA heart failure guideline (Heidenreich PA, et al. J Am Coll Cardiol. 2022;79(17):e263-e421) is described by TCTMD as saying fluid restriction is commonly recommended but the evidence is of low quality. I could not open the guideline text itself (the AHA page returned HTTP 403).
- Limitations: open-label; self-reported intake; groups differed by only about 280 mL a day; the unrestricted group still drank under 2,000 mL on median; stable outpatients only; I read a news report, not the paper's full text. The trial supports "no clear benefit of a 1,500 mL cap in stable patients", not "everyone with heart failure can drink freely". The script tells viewers to follow their own care team's limit.

## 7. Chronic kidney disease: drinking more water (CKD WIT)

**Figures (from news and institute summaries; the PubMed abstract would not open).**
- Design: randomized trial in 9 centers in Ontario, Canada, 631 adults with stage 3 chronic kidney disease, 1 year; coached to drink more water versus coached to keep usual intake.
- Result: mean change in eGFR −2.2 mL/min per 1.73 m² (more water) versus −1.9 (usual intake), P = 0.74. The extra water raised urine volume by about 0.6 liter a day and lowered copeptin (a vasopressin marker). The authors noted the trial may have been underpowered for a clinically important difference.
- Source: Clark WF, Sontrop JM, Huang SH, et al. Effect of coaching to increase water intake on kidney function decline in adults with chronic kidney disease: the CKD WIT randomized clinical trial. JAMA. 2018;319(18):1870-1879. doi:10.1001/jama.2018.4930. PMID 29801012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29801012/ (citation confirmed; numbers via https://www.lawsonresearch.ca/node/1024 and an MDedge summary)
- Limitations: numbers verified from secondary reports, not the abstract; the manufacturer Danone Research was disclosed as a relationship of the first author. Result: more water did not slow kidney decline over one year. It does not show harm, and it does not say how much people with kidney disease should drink. The script says only the first part, and tells viewers a fluid target should come from their care team.

## 8. Night-time urination and when to drink

**Figures.**
- Clinically relevant nocturia (2 or more voids a night): about 2 to 18 percent of people aged 20 to 40, rising to about 28 to 62 percent of those aged 70 to 80 (varies with definitions and populations).
- Evening or night-time drinking is often a behavioral contributor, especially diuretic drinks such as caffeine and alcohol; reducing evening fluid is listed as the first intervention.
- Practical advice in the review: minimize fluid for at least 2 hours before bed, particularly caffeine or alcohol; empty the bladder before bed; try lifestyle changes for up to 3 months before adding drugs unless the bother is intolerable; take diuretics in the mid-to-late afternoon rather than at bedtime.
- Other contributors named in a separate review snippet (not opened in full): fluid that pools in the legs during the day and sleep apnea.
- Source: Oelke M, De Wachter S, Drake MJ, Giannantoni A, Kirby M, Orme S, Rees J, van Kerrebroeck P, Everaert K. A practical approach to the management of nocturia. Int J Clin Pract. 2017;71(11):e13027. doi:10.1111/ijcp.13027. PMID 28984060. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5698733 (opened; the last 15,000 characters were not read)
- Limitations: expert practical guidance and a review, not a randomized trial of evening fluid timing. I searched for such trials and found only secondary or unverified summaries (a 2021 Seoul trial whose results I could not read; blog claims of 38 to 40 percent reductions with no journal citation), so none are used. The script therefore says "suggested" and "first step", never "proven to cut night trips by X percent". The 2 liters daily limit in the same review is not used because it is conditional and could conflict with the National Academies figures.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- In small experiments, healthy older adults feel less thirst and drink less than young adults after going without water, despite more concentrated blood (Phillips 1984; Kenney and Chiu review 2001).
- Healthy older volunteers concentrate urine less well after fluid deprivation (Rowe 1976).
- Thirst alone is not a reliable signal of dehydration in older people (Cochrane 2015).
- The US National Academies adequate intake figures, the 20 percent food share, and that coffee and tea count.
- Cutting evening fluids (especially caffeine and alcohol) is the standard first step for night-time urination, and diuretics are best taken earlier in the day.

**Uncertain**
- How common real dehydration is among healthy older adults who drink regularly (Kenney and Chiu say they usually drink enough).
- Whether the exact amounts apply to older adults specifically; the National Academies release gave no age-specific values that I could see.
- How much evening timing helps: guidance is expert-based, with no large trial verified.
- Heart failure: stable patients had no clear benefit from a 1,500 mL cap in one trial, but that does not apply to unstable patients or to any individual's personal limit.
- Kidney disease: more water did not slow decline over one year in stage 3 disease; the right amount for any person is for their care team.

**False or misleading**
- "You must drink eight glasses a day" (no source here supports a single magic number; the National Academies number is an average of total water, including food).
- "Coffee and tea don't count" (the National Academies say caffeinated drinks can contribute as much as non-caffeinated ones).
- "Feeling no thirst means you are hydrated" (Phillips; Cochrane).
- "More water protects your kidneys" (no benefit in CKD WIT).
