# Research notes: Hidden sodium (slug: hidden-sodium)

Question: Where does most of the salt you eat really come from?

Pages marked (fetched) were opened and read in this session. Pages marked (search summary) were seen only through search-result summaries (the PubMed and JAMA pages blocked automated fetching); those figures are cross-checked against a second page where possible.

## 1. Where sodium comes from: the 450-adult duplicate-sample study

- **Claim:** About 71 percent of the sodium Americans eat is added to food outside the home (manufacturing, packaging, restaurants). Only about 5 percent is added at the table and about 6 percent in home cooking.
- **Figures (fetched, PMC full text):**
  - Mean total sodium intake: 3,501 mg/day (recommendation: under 2,300 mg/day).
  - Added to food outside the home: 70.9 percent.
  - Inherent (naturally in food): 14.2 percent.
  - Added during home food preparation: 5.6 percent.
  - Added at the table: 4.9 percent.
  - Home tap water and supplements/antacids: each under 0.5 percent.
- **Design/population:** n = 450 adults, 150 each from Birmingham AL, Palo Alto CA and the Minneapolis-St. Paul MN area, equal numbers of women and men, four race/ethnic groups. Four record-assisted 24-hour dietary recalls per person, plus duplicate samples of salt added at the table and in home cooking, which were chemically measured.
- **Source:** Harnack LJ, Cogswell ME, Shikany JM, et al. Sources of Sodium in US Adults From 3 Geographic Regions. Circulation. 2017;135(19):1775-1783. doi:10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.116.024446. PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483828/ ; PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5417577/
- **Limitations:** Convenience sample (more college graduates than the US average, many university employees); only three metro areas; self-reported recalls; possible Hawthorne effect (measuring salt use may have changed it).
- **Type:** Observational measurement study (not an experiment).

## 2. CDC national data: top food categories and places

- **Claim:** The average American eats about 3,400 mg of sodium a day. The top 10 food categories supply 44 percent, the top 25 supply 70 percent. Most of it is not from "salty-tasting" foods; bread alone is number one.
- **Figures (fetched, MMWR):**
  - Mean sodium intake, ages 2 and up: 3,409 mg/day (excluding salt added at the table).
  - Top 10 categories, % of all sodium: yeast breads 6.2; pizza 5.9; sandwiches 5.7; cold cuts and cured meats 5.4; soups 3.8; burritos and tacos 3.8; savory snacks 3.7; chicken (whole pieces) 3.7; cheese 3.5; eggs and omelets 2.6. Top 10 together: 44 percent. Top 25: 70 percent.
  - Where the food came from: stores 60.8 percent; fast-food and pizza restaurants 16.7 percent; restaurants with waitstaff 10.7 percent; school/child/adult care cafeterias 2 percent; other 9.8 percent.
  - Sodium density: restaurants with waitstaff 2,119 mg per 1,000 kcal vs. 1,557 mg per 1,000 kcal for store food.
  - Table salt, not counted in these numbers, is estimated at 5 to 6 percent of intake.
- **Design/population:** NHANES 2013-2014, What We Eat in America 24-hour recall. 8,067 people aged 2 and older with a complete, reliable recall (of 9,813 participants). Not limited to older adults.
- **Source:** Quader ZS, Zhao L, Gillespie C, et al. Sodium Intake Among Persons Aged 2 Years and Older - United States, 2013-2014. MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep. 2017;66(12):324-328. https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6612a3.htm (PMC5657955)
- **Also seen (search summary):** CDC's follow-up report on 2015-2016 data found about 40 percent from the top 10 categories (MMWR 2020;69(32), https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6932a3.htm). Not used for numbers in the script.
- **Limitations:** Self-reported recall, underreporting; non-institutionalized people only; category rankings depend on how foods are grouped (e.g., "sandwiches" contain bread and deli meat, so the categories overlap in spirit); data are about a decade old.
- **Note on the "restaurant" framing:** Store-bought food (packaged foods, bakery, deli) supplied the largest share (about 61 percent); restaurants supplied about 27 percent (fast food/pizza plus waitstaff). The script says "packaged and restaurant food" and gives these numbers rather than implying restaurants alone.

## 3. Recommended limits and label rules

- **Daily limits:** FDA Daily Value for sodium is 2,300 mg a day, about 1 teaspoon of table salt; the Dietary Guidelines for Americans advise adults to stay under 2,300 mg; the American Heart Association says 1,500 mg a day is the ideal limit for most adults. (search summary of FDA "Sodium in Your Diet" https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-education-resources-materials/sodium-your-diet and AHA https://www.heart.org/en/news/2021/02/15/lower-your-sodium-and-blood-pressure-will-follow ; the 2,300 mg figure also appears in the fetched MMWR.)
- **%Daily Value rule of thumb:** 5 percent DV or less per serving = low; 20 percent DV or more = high (FDA, search summary, https://www.fda.gov/media/84261/download).
- **Label claims, 21 CFR 101.61 (search summaries of FDA text; the eCFR page itself redirected and could not be fetched):**
  - Sodium-free: less than 5 mg per serving.
  - Very low sodium: 35 mg or less per serving.
  - Low sodium: 140 mg or less per serving.
  - Reduced sodium: at least 25 percent less sodium than the regular version.
  - Light in sodium: at least 50 percent less than the regular version.
  - "No salt added": no salt added during processing, but the food can still contain sodium.
  - All are per "reference amount" (the serving the FDA uses), which can be smaller than what a person actually eats.
- **Reasoning, not a study finding:** 140 mg is 6 percent of 2,300 mg, so three servings of a "low sodium" food add up to about 420 mg; a "reduced sodium" soup that starts very high can still be high.
- **FDA voluntary targets:** Phase I final guidance 2021 (average intake from about 3,400 toward 3,000 mg); Phase II draft 2024 aims at 2,750 mg a day. Voluntary. (search summary; https://health.gov/news/202110/fda-issues-final-guidance-voluntary-sodium-reduction-targets). Background only; not used in the script.

## 4. Trial in adults 50-75: one week of lower sodium (CARDIA-SSBP)

- **Claim:** In a randomized crossover trial, one week of a low-sodium diet lowered 24-hour systolic blood pressure by about 7 to 8 mmHg compared with a high-sodium week; about three in four people had a lower pressure.
- **Figures (fetched, ACC trial summary; JAMA citation from ClinicalTrials.gov/search):**
  - n = 213 adults aged 50 to 75 (median age 61), 65 percent women; 48 percent had hypertension, 21 percent diabetes; 19 percent took two or more blood pressure medicines.
  - Design: randomized, open-label, crossover. One week of usual diet, then one week of high- or low-sodium diet, then the other. 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure on day 7.
  - Diets: high-sodium = about 2,200 mg/day added to usual diet; low-sodium = about 500 mg/day total. 24-hour urinary sodium: usual 4.62 g, high 5.48 g, low 1.70 g.
  - Systolic BP, high vs. low sodium: 8 mmHg lower on low (95% CI 4-11) in the first period; 7 mmHg (95% CI 3-11) in the second; median within-person reduction 7 mmHg.
  - Low sodium vs. usual diet: about 5 mmHg lower.
  - 74.4 percent had a lower systolic BP on the low-sodium diet.
  - Result was similar in people on blood pressure medicine (7.78 mmHg) and not (7.90 mmHg); did not differ significantly by hypertension status.
  - Adverse events were few and mild (headache, edema, cramping, each a few percent or less).
- **Source:** Gupta DK, Lewis CE, Varady KA, et al. Effect of Dietary Sodium on Blood Pressure: A Crossover Trial. JAMA. 2023;330(23):2258-2266. doi:10.1001/jama.2023.23651. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04258332. Summary page: https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2023/11/10/05/29/cardia-ssbp ; also https://www.tctmd.com/news/slash-dietary-sodium-slash-blood-pressure-cardia-ssbp
- **Limitations:** Only 7 days per diet; the low-sodium diet (about 500 mg/day) is far lower than most people eat or than guidelines ask; long-term adherence and effects on heart attacks or strokes unknown; open-label. Not designed to show who is "salt sensitive."

## 5. Trial in adults 60-80 on blood pressure medicine (TONE)

- **Claim:** In older adults whose blood pressure was controlled on one medicine, those who cut sodium and then had their medicine withdrawn under supervision were less likely to have blood pressure creep back up.
- **Figures (fetched, ACC trial summary; abstract conclusions via search summary):**
  - n = 975 adults aged 60-80 with hypertension controlled on one drug (585 overweight, 390 not overweight). Randomized to sodium reduction, weight loss, both, or usual care. Antihypertensive medicine withdrawal attempted after three months.
  - Sodium goal: 24-hour urine sodium of 1,800 mg or less. At nine months, 36 percent of the sodium-reduction group had reached it vs. 11 percent of the others.
  - Among participants still off medicine at the final visit (n = 328): average blood pressure 131/74 mmHg with sodium reduction, 134/75 with usual care.
  - Combined endpoint (per the trial's primary outcome, among 886 participants whose medication was successfully tapered), hazard ratio vs. usual care: sodium reduction 0.68 (95% CI 0.54-0.82); weight loss 0.75; combined 0.55.
  - Cardiovascular event rates did not differ significantly among groups.
  - Authors' conclusion: reduced sodium intake and weight loss are a feasible, effective and safe nonpharmacologic therapy of hypertension in older persons.
- **Source:** Whelton PK, Appel LJ, Espeland MA, et al. Sodium reduction and weight loss in the treatment of hypertension in older persons: a randomized controlled trial of nonpharmacologic interventions in the elderly (TONE). JAMA. 1998;279(11):839-846. ACC summary https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2010/02/23/19/23/TONE ; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00000535. Follow-up report: Appel LJ, et al. Arch Intern Med. 2001;161(5):685-693.
- **Limitations:** Medicine was withdrawn by the study team with close monitoring; this is not advice to stop medicine. Only about a third of the sodium group reached the goal. Participants were volunteers in a trial, mostly well-controlled. The exact wording of the combined endpoint (high blood pressure returning, restarted medicine or a cardiovascular event) was not re-read on the abstract page in this session; the script describes it loosely ("blood pressure creeping back up or medicine being restarted"), and the ACC page and the trial press release (a 31 percent lower need for medicine with sodium reduction) support that reading.

## 6. General background (no figures used)
- Sodium helps the body keep fluid balance and nerve and muscle function; extra sodium makes the body hold water, which can raise pressure inside the vessels. Standard physiology (AHA and NIH teaching pages); no number is quoted in the script.

## 7. Context not used as a number in the script
- Observational studies that suggest very low sodium intake could be harmful (J-shaped curves) are debated because of measurement problems with spot urine; the guideline position remains to lower sodium for people with high intakes. Mentioned only as "we do not know the perfect number" - no figures cited.
- Salt substitutes (potassium chloride) can be dangerous for people with kidney disease or on certain medicines (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone). The script gives only a general safety note: "ask your doctor or pharmacist before using a salt substitute," with no figures.
- Potassium/kidney/heart-failure patients may be given individual sodium targets by their care team - script says follow your own doctor's number.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Most of the sodium in the American diet is already in the food when you buy it or order it: about 71 percent added outside the home in the three-city study, and about 61 percent from store-bought food plus about 27 percent from restaurants in the national data. Table salt and home cooking together are roughly 10 percent.
- Bread, pizza, sandwiches, cold cuts and soup are among the biggest sources. Typical intake (about 3,400 to 3,500 mg a day) is well above the 2,300 mg limit.
- In adults aged 50 to 75, a week of a low-sodium diet lowered blood pressure by about 7 to 8 mmHg on average, including in people taking blood pressure medicine.
- In adults aged 60 to 80 with controlled hypertension, sodium reduction (with supervised medicine withdrawal) reduced the chance of blood pressure returning or medicine being restarted.
- "Low sodium" on a label has a specific legal meaning (140 mg or less per serving).

**Uncertain**
- Whether the low levels tested (about 500 mg a day in CARDIA-SSBP) can be kept up for years, and what they do to heart attacks and strokes (the trials were short and measured blood pressure, not events).
- How much individual people differ in their response ("salt sensitivity").
- The exact current numbers (the main national data are from 2013-2014; the 2015-2016 report showed a similar picture).
- The best target for very old or frail adults, or for people with heart failure or kidney disease. These need individual advice.

**False or misleading**
- "Salt mostly comes from the salt shaker." It does not: table salt is about 5 percent.
- "If it doesn't taste salty, it's low in salt." Bread and sweet-tasting packaged foods can carry a lot.
- "Reduced sodium" or "no salt added" means low sodium. Not necessarily.
- "Sea salt or pink salt is healthier." Not covered by the sources; the script does not address it.
