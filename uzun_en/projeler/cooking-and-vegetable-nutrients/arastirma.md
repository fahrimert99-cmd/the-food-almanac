# Research notes: Does boiling or microwaving destroy the nutrients in vegetables?

Slug: `cooking-and-vegetable-nutrients`. Prepared 3 November 2026.

How sources were checked: abstracts and records were read through Europe PMC records and search-result abstracts. Full texts were not opened (most are paywalled). Where a detail was not visible in the abstract, it is marked "not verified" below and is **not** used in the script. The NIH ODS vitamin C page returned HTTP 403 to direct fetching; its wording was taken from a search-result excerpt of the ODS page (flagged below).

---

## 1. Boiling vs steaming vs pressure steaming vs microwaving, 13 frozen vegetables

- **Citation:** Bureau S, Mouhoubi S, Touloumet L, Garcia C, Moreau F, Bédouet V, Renard CMGC. Are folates, carotenoids and vitamin C affected by cooking? Four domestic procedures are compared on a large diversity of frozen vegetables. *LWT – Food Science and Technology*. 2015;64(2):735-741. DOI: 10.1016/j.lwt.2015.06.016. Open record: https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02635963
- **Design:** laboratory comparison of four home procedures (boiling in water, steaming, pressure steaming, microwaving) on 13 frozen vegetables; folates, carotenoids and vitamin C measured before and after.
- **Key figures (boiling, fresh-matter basis, average across vegetables):** total vitamin C −51%; folates −68%; lutein −15%; beta-carotene −9%.
- **Dry-matter basis (boiling):** vitamin C −44%; folates −65%; boiling "not the less suitable" for carotenoids, where it improved extractability of some carotenoids, including lutein and beta-carotene.
- **Authors' conclusion:** the best methods to preserve nutritional quality could be, alternatively, steaming, microwaving or pressure cooking; boiling was generally the least suitable. Results varied widely by vegetable and nutrient.
- **Limitations:** laboratory measurements of nutrient content, not absorption in people; frozen (blanched) vegetables as the starting material; results depend on vegetable, time and water volume. Per-method figures for steaming and microwaving were not visible in the abstract, so none are quoted.

## 2. Review of cooking effects on vegetable phytochemicals

- **Citation:** Palermo M, Pellegrini N, Fogliano V. The effect of cooking on the phytochemical content of vegetables. *J Sci Food Agric*. 2014;94(6):1057-1070. PMID 24227349. DOI: 10.1002/jsfa.6478. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24227349/
- **Design:** narrative review of more than 100 articles.
- **Findings used:** two opposing effects of heat: degradation, and softening of the food matrix that makes compounds easier to extract. The net result depends on cooking conditions, food structure and the compound. Steaming best preserves phenols and glucosinolates because the vegetable does not contact cooking water or oil, limiting leaching and heat damage. Carotenoids behaved differently and in some cases increased after more intensive processing.
- **Limitations:** review; mostly laboratory measurements of content.

## 3. NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, vitamin C

- **Citation:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. Vitamin C Fact Sheet for Consumers. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/
- **Findings used (from a search-result excerpt of the page; direct fetch returned 403):** the vitamin C content of food may be reduced by prolonged storage and by cooking; steaming or microwaving may lessen cooking losses; many of the best vitamin C food sources (fruits and vegetables) are usually eaten raw.
- **Limitation:** consumer fact sheet; no loss figures. Fact-checker should open the page to confirm the wording.

## 4. Carrots, zucchini and broccoli: boiling, steaming, frying

- **Citation:** Miglio C, Chiavaro E, Visconti A, Fogliano V, Pellegrini N. Effects of different cooking methods on nutritional and physicochemical characteristics of selected vegetables. *J Agric Food Chem*. 2008;56(1):139-147. PMID 18069785. DOI: 10.1021/jf072304b. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18069785/
- **Design:** laboratory comparison of boiling, steaming and frying of carrots, courgettes (zucchini) and broccoli.
- **Findings used:** water-based cooking (boiling and steaming) preserved carotenoids better in all three vegetables, and ascorbic acid in carrots; frying retained fewer antioxidant compounds; antioxidant activity values (TEAC, FRAP, TRAP) rose in all cooked vegetables, attributed to softer tissue and better extractability. Authors argue cooked vegetables are not necessarily lower in nutritional quality.
- **Limitations:** laboratory content measurements; three vegetables; details beyond the abstract were not verified.

## 5. Tomatoes: heating raises extractable lycopene, lowers vitamin C

- **Citation:** Dewanto V, Wu X, Adom KK, Liu RH. Thermal processing enhances the nutritional value of tomatoes by increasing total antioxidant activity. *J Agric Food Chem*. 2002;50(10):3010-3014. PMID 11982434. DOI: 10.1021/jf0115589. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11982434/
- **Design:** laboratory study; tomatoes heated at 88 °C (about 190 °F) for 2, 15 and 30 minutes.
- **Figures (from the abstract as shown in search results):** trans-lycopene in raw tomato 2.01; after 2, 15, 30 minutes: 3.11, 5.45, 5.32 (same units; the abstract text gives "mg per g", which may be a typo for µg per g, so the script uses only the **ratio**: 5.45 / 2.01 ≈ 2.7 times at 15 minutes). Vitamin C 0.76 → 0.54 µmol/g at 30 minutes (−29%). Total antioxidant activity increased; total phenolics and flavonoids did not change significantly.
- **Limitations:** laboratory measurement of extractable content, not absorption in people.

## 6. Lycopene absorption in people: paste vs fresh tomatoes

- **Citation:** Gärtner C, Stahl W, Sies H. Lycopene is more bioavailable from tomato paste than from fresh tomatoes. *Am J Clin Nutr*. 1997;66(1):116-122. PMID 9209178. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/66.1.116. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9209178/
- **Design:** human single-meal study; same dose, 23 mg lycopene, from fresh tomatoes or tomato paste, each with 15 g corn oil; carotenoids measured in the chylomicron fraction of blood.
- **Findings:** paste gave about 2.5-fold higher peak total and all-trans lycopene and about 3.8-fold higher area under the curve than fresh tomatoes; alpha- and beta-carotene responses did not differ.
- **Limitations:** number of participants not visible in the abstract record (not stated in the script); it compares paste (a processed product) with fresh tomatoes, not simply cooked with raw; single meal.

## 7. Carrots: beta-carotene release in a digestion model

- **Citation:** Hedrén E, Diaz V, Svanberg U. Estimation of carotenoid accessibility from carrots determined by an in vitro digestion method. *Eur J Clin Nutr*. 2002;56(5):425-430. PMID 12001013. DOI: 10.1038/sj.ejcn.1601329. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12001013/
- **Design:** in vitro (laboratory) digestion model.
- **Figures:** beta-carotene released: raw carrot pieces 3%; raw homogenized (mashed) carrot 21%; cooked mash 27%; cooked mash with cooking oil 39%. Alpha-carotene followed similar trends.
- **Limitations:** not people; accessibility in a model is not the same as absorption in the body.

## 8. Dietary fat and carotenoid absorption from salads

- **Citation:** Brown MJ, Ferruzzi MG, Nguyen ML, Cooper DA, Eldridge AL, Schwartz SJ, White WS. Carotenoid bioavailability is higher from salads ingested with full-fat than with fat-reduced salad dressings as measured with electrochemical detection. *Am J Clin Nutr*. 2004;80(2):396-403. DOI: 10.1093/ajcn/80.2.396. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15277161/
- **Design:** randomized crossover; 7 subjects ate the same salad with fat-free, reduced-fat or full-fat (canola oil) dressing (0, 6 or 28 g oil); chylomicron carotenoids over 12 hours, at least 2 weeks washout.
- **Findings:** with fat-free dressing, absorption of alpha-carotene, beta-carotene and lycopene was essentially negligible; absorption rose with reduced-fat and was greater still with full-fat dressing.
- **Limitations:** 7 adults; canola oil, not olive oil; the participants' ages are not stated in the abstract (a press report said ages 19 to 28, not verified).

## 9. Tomato sauce, simmering time and extra virgin olive oil

- **Citation:** Vallverdú-Queralt A, Regueiro J, de Alvarenga JF, Torrado X, Lamuela-Raventos RM. Carotenoid profile of tomato sauces: effect of cooking time and content of extra virgin olive oil. *Int J Mol Sci*. 2015;16(5):9588-9599. PMID 25927580. DOI: 10.3390/ijms16059588. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25927580/
- **Findings (abstract summary):** longer cooking time and added extra virgin olive oil both increased antioxidant activity, total carotenoids and Z-lycopene isomers in sauces.
- **Limitations:** laboratory analysis of sauces, not absorption in people. Exact oil amounts not verified.

## 10. Spinach: food matrix, lutein and beta-carotene in people

- **Citation:** Castenmiller JJ, West CE, Linssen JP, van het Hof KH, Voragen AG. The food matrix of spinach is a limiting factor in determining the bioavailability of beta-carotene and to a lesser extent of lutein in humans. *J Nutr*. 1999;129(2):349-355. PMID 10024612. DOI: 10.1093/jn/129.2.349. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10024612/
- **Design:** controlled human feeding for 3 weeks; n = 10 control; n = 12 per spinach group; spinach as whole leaf, minced, enzymatically liquefied, or liquefied with added fiber; serum carotenoids.
- **Figures:** relative bioavailability vs a carotenoid supplement: beta-carotene about 5.1%, 6.4%, 9.5%, 9.3%; lutein about 45%, 52%, 55%, 54% (same order). Beta-carotene responses differed between whole leaf/minced and liquefied; lutein responses did not differ among groups.
- **Limitations:** compares food forms (not raw vs cooked directly); participants' age not stated in the abstract; 1999.
- **Not used:** a lab cell-model study (O'Sullivan et al. 2008, Caco-2 cells) reportedly found lutein transport greater from uncooked spinach. It was seen only as a search listing, not opened, so it is excluded.

## 11. Lutein supplements and macular degeneration (AREDS2)

- **Citation:** Age-Related Eye Disease Study 2 Research Group. Lutein + zeaxanthin and omega-3 fatty acids for age-related macular degeneration: the AREDS2 randomized clinical trial. *JAMA*. 2013;309(19):2005-2015. DOI: 10.1001/jama.2013.4997. PMID 23644932. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23644932/
- **Design:** multicenter, double-masked, placebo-controlled randomized trial, 2 × 2 factorial; 4,203 people aged 50 to 85 at risk of advanced AMD; lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg, DHA/EPA, both, or placebo, on a background AREDS formulation; median follow-up 5 years.
- **Figures:** estimated 5-year progression to advanced AMD: placebo 31%, lutein + zeaxanthin 29%, DHA/EPA 31%, both 30%. Hazard ratio for lutein + zeaxanthin 0.90 (98.7% CI 0.76-1.07): not significant.
- **Limitations:** supplements, not food; people at risk of AMD; says nothing about spinach.

## 12. NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, vitamin K and warfarin

- **Citation:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. Vitamin K Health Professional Fact Sheet. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/
- **Finding used (from a search-result summary of the page; not opened in full):** people taking warfarin and similar anticoagulants need steady vitamin K intake from food and supplements; abrupt changes can strengthen or weaken the drug's effect.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Boiling vegetables in water leaches water-soluble vitamins. In 13 frozen vegetables the average losses were 51% for vitamin C and 68% for folate; lutein and beta-carotene lost much less.
- Steaming, microwaving and pressure cooking are the methods the authors and NIH name as better at preserving vitamin C and folate.
- For lycopene (tomatoes), heat plus fat increases absorption: about 2.5 times the peak and 3.8 times the total in a human study comparing paste with fresh tomatoes.
- Carotenoids need dietary fat to be absorbed well (negligible with fat-free dressing in 7 adults).

**Uncertain**
- How much of lab findings (carrot beta-carotene, sauces) translates to real-life absorption.
- Whether olive oil specifically beats other oils (the human trial used canola oil).
- Exact retention numbers for steaming and microwaving by vegetable (not in the abstracts read).
- Whether any of these differences change health outcomes; none of the studies measured disease.
- Most studies were not designed around adults over 60.

**False or unsupported**
- "Microwaving destroys nutrients": not supported; it is among the methods named as best at preserving vitamin C and folate.
- "Cooking destroys the nutrients in vegetables": too broad; heat lowers vitamin C and folate but raises the availability of lycopene and some carotenoids.
- "Cooking spinach makes the lutein in it much easier to absorb": not supported by the one human feeding study found (lutein response did not differ between whole, minced and liquefied spinach).
- "Eating lutein for your eyes is proven": the large AREDS2 trial of lutein + zeaxanthin supplements found no significant reduction in progression to advanced AMD.
