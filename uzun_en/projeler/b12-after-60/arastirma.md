# Research notes: Why is vitamin B12 deficiency so common after 60?

Slug: `b12-after-60`. Audience: US adults 55+, many on metformin, PPIs or H2 blockers.

Note on access: PubMed pages and the NIH ODS fact-sheet pages returned cookie walls / HTTP 403 when fetched directly. Items marked **(abstract via search)** were read as search-result abstract text, not the full page; items marked **(page opened)** were opened in full via PMC. Claims that rest only on a search snippet are labeled so, and the script words them cautiously.

## 1. Atrophic gastritis, PPI use and B12 status in older adults, and the effect of fortified foods **(page opened, PMC)**

- **Citation:** Porter KM, Hoey L, Hughes CF, Ward M, Clements M, Strain JJ, Cunningham C, Casey MC, Tracey F, O'Kane M, Pentieva K, McAnena L, McCarroll K, Laird E, Molloy AM, McNulty H. Associations of atrophic gastritis and proton-pump inhibitor drug use with vitamin B-12 status, and the impact of fortified foods, in older adults. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2021;114(4):1286–1294. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8488868
- **Design/population:** Observational analysis of the Trinity-Ulster-Department of Agriculture (TUDA) cohort of older adults born on the island of Ireland. 3,299 participants analyzed (of 5,186), age 60 and older; mean age 74.0 ± 7.8 y with atrophic gastritis (AG), 72.8 ± 8.0 y without. Excluded: B-vitamin supplement users, people on interfering medicines, short-term PPI users, missing data.
- **AG definition:** serum pepsinogen I:II ratio below 3, measured in PPI non-users only.
- **Figures:**
  - AG prevalence: 15% (317 of 2,083 PPI non-users).
  - B12 deficiency (combined B12 indicator ≤ −0.5): AG 38%; PPI users 21%; controls (no AG, no PPI) 15%.
  - Fortified foods, regular intake (≥5 portions/week): deficiency 30% vs 50% in people with AG (regular vs non-regular); 10% vs 21% in controls. Regular intake did not fully normalize B12 status in AG.
- **Limitations:** observational (association only); PPI duration recorded only as <6 months vs ≥6 months; pepsinogen ratio is a screening tool, not a diagnostic test; MMA data for about 30% only; no significant fortified-food associations in men who used PPIs or had AG; participants from Ireland/Northern Ireland, so may not generalize to the US; fortified-food intake assessed with a limited 7-item food frequency section.

## 2. Long-term metformin use and B12 deficiency, Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study **(page opened, PMC)**

- **Citation:** Aroda VR, Edelstein SL, Goldberg RB, et al. Long-term metformin use and vitamin B12 deficiency in the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study. *J Clin Endocrinol Metab.* 2016;101(4):1754–1761. doi:10.1210/jc.2015-3754. PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4880159
- **Design/population:** Secondary (post hoc) analysis of the randomized DPP and its follow-up DPPOS at 27 US centers. Adults with elevated fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance, and overweight/obesity. Placebo n = 1,082; metformin n = 1,073 (2,155 randomized). Metformin or placebo for 3.2 y, then open-label metformin in the metformin group for about 9 more years. Mean age 51.2 y at DPP baseline; 56.0 (placebo) / 56.7 (metformin) at DPPOS year 1; 63.9 / 65.5 at year 9.
- **Cutoffs:** low B12 ≤ 203 pg/mL; borderline 204–298 pg/mL.
- **Figures (metformin vs placebo):**
  - Low B12 at about 5 y: 4.3% vs 2.3% (P = .02).
  - Low B12 at about 13 y: 7.4% vs 5.4% (P = .12, not significant).
  - Low or borderline at 5 y: 19.1% vs 9.5% (P < .01); at 13 y: 20.3% vs 15.6% (P = .02).
  - Each year of total metformin use: odds ratio for B12 deficiency 1.13 (95% CI 1.06–1.20).
  - Neuropathy (monofilament exam) more common in metformin participants with low B12 at year 9 (P = .03), but based on only 13 cases among 56 participants; the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument showed no difference by B12 status.
  - Anemia more common in metformin group (14.3% vs 10.5% at year 1) but did not differ by B12 status.
- **Authors' conclusion:** routine testing of B12 in metformin-treated patients should be considered.
- **Limitations:** post hoc; B12 measured only at year 1 and year 9; no baseline samples; no MMA/holotranscobalamin; over-the-counter supplement use not collected; neuropathy finding is small.

## 3. Long-term acid-suppressive medications and B12 deficiency **(abstract via search; Kaiser/secondary summaries)**

- **Citation:** Lam JR, Schneider JL, Zhao W, Corley DA. Proton pump inhibitor and histamine 2 receptor antagonist use and vitamin B12 deficiency. *JAMA.* 2013;310(22):2435–2442. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24327038/
- **Design:** Nested case-control study, Kaiser Permanente Northern California, 1997–June 2011. 25,956 patients with a new B12 deficiency diagnosis vs 184,199 controls.
- **Figures:** Two or more years' supply of PPIs dispensed: odds ratio 1.65 (95% CI 1.58–1.73) (12.0% of cases). Two or more years' supply of H2 receptor antagonists (without PPI): OR 1.25 (95% CI 1.17–1.34) (4.2% of cases). Higher doses associated with higher odds; the association decreased after discontinuation of use.
- **Limitations:** observational; authors could not exclude residual confounding; diagnosis-coded deficiency (not a systematic blood screen); the age profile of cases was not verified by me, so the script does not state it.

## 4. Plasma B12 and intake source, Framingham Offspring Study **(abstract via search)**

- **Citation:** Tucker KL, Rich S, Rosenberg I, Jacques P, Dallal G, Wilson PWF, Selhub J. Plasma vitamin B-12 concentrations relate to intake source in the Framingham Offspring Study. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2000;71(2):514–522. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10648266/
- **Design/population:** Cross-sectional, 2,999 Framingham Offspring participants; age groups 26–49, 50–64, 65–83 (not an older-adults-only sample).
- **Figures:** 39% had plasma B12 < 258 pmol/L, 17% < 185 pmol/L, 9% < 148 pmol/L, with little difference between age groups. Supplement users were less likely to have < 185 pmol/L (8% vs 20% in non-users). Among non-supplement users, those eating fortified cereal more than 4 times a week (12%) vs no fortified cereal (23%) differed significantly (these percentages refer to the < 185 pmol/L category).
- **Limitations:** cross-sectional association; self-reported intake; 25 years old; participants were mostly white and from Massachusetts; wide age range, so only partly relevant to 60+.

## 5. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency in elderly patients, review **(abstract via search)**

- **Citation:** Andrès E, Loukili NH, Noel E, et al. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) deficiency in elderly patients. *CMAJ.* 2004;171(3):251–259. doi:10.1503/cmaj.1031155. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15289425/
- **Figures:** deficiency occurs frequently (> 20%) among elderly people; often unrecognized because clinical manifestations are subtle; complications can be serious, particularly neuropsychiatric and hematologic. Causes: food-cobalamin malabsorption syndrome (> 60% of all cases), pernicious anemia (15–20%), inadequate dietary intake, other malabsorption.
- **Limitations:** narrative review, 2004, mostly European clinical populations; the ">20%" figure depends on the definition and cutoff used and is not a US national estimate.
- **Background definition (not separately fetched):** "food-cobalamin malabsorption" means inability to release B12 from food proteins, which typically happens when stomach acid and enzymes are reduced, as in atrophic gastritis or with acid-suppressing drugs.

## 6. NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Vitamin B12 consumer fact sheet **(search-result text only; page returned 403)**

- URL: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/
- Statements seen (quoted or closely paraphrased in search output):
  - Only animal foods naturally contain B12; strict vegetarians and vegans may be at risk.
  - Many older adults do not have enough hydrochloric acid in the stomach to absorb the B12 naturally present in food.
  - People with atrophic gastritis might not absorb enough B12 (too little acid and intrinsic factor).
  - People over 50 should get most of their B12 from fortified foods or dietary supplements, since the body can usually absorb B12 from those sources.
  - Deficiency signs: tiredness or weakness (megaloblastic anemia); numb or tingly hands and feet; problems with balance, depression, confusion, dementia, poor memory, sore mouth or tongue.
  - Symptoms can take years to appear because the body stores large reserves; nerve damage can occur even without megaloblastic anemia, so early treatment matters.
- **Not used:** the RDA number and any "10–30%" figure, because I could not confirm them from a page I saw.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Older adults are a high-risk group; deficiency is often missed because the signs are subtle (review, plus NIH).
- Reduced stomach acid (atrophic gastritis) is linked to low B12 in older adults: 38% vs 15% in 3,299 adults aged 60+ (observational).
- Long-term metformin is linked to lower B12 in a large randomized-trial follow-up (4.3% vs 2.3% low at about 5 years; 19.1% vs 9.5% low or borderline).
- Two or more years of PPIs (OR 1.65) or H2 blockers (OR 1.25) are linked to a B12 deficiency diagnosis (observational, large).
- Fortified foods and supplements are associated with better B12 status (Framingham; TUDA), consistent with NIH advice for people over 50.

**Uncertain**
- Causation for PPIs/H2 blockers (observational; residual confounding).
- Metformin effect at 13 years in clearly low B12 (not statistically significant); borderline levels remain higher.
- Whether the neuropathy signal in DPPOS is real (13 cases).
- Exact prevalence in US adults over 60; the "over 20%" figure comes from a 2004 review of mainly European clinical populations.
- Fortified foods did not fully fix B12 status in those with atrophic gastritis (30% still deficient).
- Framingham data are cross-sectional and include people under 50.

**False / not supported**
- That fatigue, tingling or memory slips are always "just aging": they have many causes and low B12 is one worth checking.
- That B12 pills fix tiredness or memory in people with normal levels: not claimed anywhere in these sources.
- That people should stop metformin or acid reducers because of B12: nothing here supports it.
