# Research notes: Should you wash raw chicken before cooking?

Slug: `washing-raw-chicken` · Prepared for The Food Almanac (US audience, mostly 60+).

**How the sources were checked.** Pages marked **[opened]** were fetched and read in this session. Pages marked **[secondary]** were only seen through a search summary or news coverage; the script uses only the figures marked as safe to use. Several official pages (FSIS, CDC pages with older URLs, PubMed, the Food Protection Trends PDFs) returned errors or unreadable binary, so a few primary documents could not be read directly. Those gaps are listed under "Limits of this research".

## 1. Home kitchen observational study: NC State / RTI / USDA FSIS (2022)

- **Citation:** Shumaker ET, Kirchner M, Shelley L, Goulter R, Goodson L, Jaykus LA, Chapman B, Cates SC, Bernstein C, Lavallee A. *Observational Study of the Impact of a Food Safety Intervention on Consumer Poultry Washing.* Journal of Food Protection. 2022. DOI: 10.4315/JFP-21-397
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.4315/JFP-21-397 · coverage: https://news.ncsu.edu/2022/04/raw-chicken-and-food-safety/ and https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/04/220404105643.htm **[opened: the NC State news page and the ScienceDaily page; the journal abstract itself was not opened]**
- **Design:** Observational test-kitchen study. n = 300 home cooks who said they washed poultry (158 control, 142 intervention that received a "don't wash" message). Each prepared chicken thighs carrying a **harmless tracer bacterial strain** (not Salmonella) and a salad; sinks, surfaces and lettuce were sampled.
- **Figures (as reported by NC State):**
  - Did not wash the chicken: **93%** of the intervention group vs **39%** of controls (P < 0.0001).
  - Lettuce cross-contamination: control group, chicken washers **26%**, non-washers **31%**; intervention group, washers 30% (very few people), non-washers **15%**.
  - Headline: **25%** of participants contaminated their salad with the raw chicken tracer.
  - "Regardless of whether people washed their chicken, the kitchen sinks became contaminated by the raw chicken" (Shumaker); counters had relatively little contamination. No percentage for sinks was given on the pages read.
  - Authors suspect "hand-facilitated cross-contamination" explains the lettuce contamination (from the abstract as quoted).
- **Conclusion by the authors:** washing is still not a good idea, but the study shows the need to focus on preventing sink contamination, hand washing and cleaning and sanitizing surfaces.
- **Limits:** a tracer organism, not a real pathogen; one test kitchen; cooks knew they were observed; it measured contamination, not illness; the 30% (intervention washers) figure is based on very few people. Washers and non-washers were not randomly assigned (they chose), so the comparison between them is observational.

## 2. "Chickensplash" laboratory study (2022)

- **Citation:** Carmody CD, Mueller RC, Grodner BM, Chlumsky O, Wilking JN, McCalla SG. *Chickensplash! Exploring the health concerns of washing raw chicken.* Physics of Fluids 34(3):031910. March 24, 2022. DOI: 10.1063/5.0083979
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8958727/ **[opened]**
- **Design:** Laboratory experiment with sterile water. 16 trials (8 skinless breasts, 8 skin-on thighs; 2 flow rates; 2 faucet heights, 15 and 40 cm; 2 flow types), 20 to 30 second rinses. Agar plates placed beside the chicken; high-speed video at 400 to 1,500 frames per second.
- **Findings:** Bacteria transferred from the raw chicken surface to the plates through splashing. Faucet height, flow type and surface stiffness changed splash height and distance; a soft surface like chicken forms a divot that promotes splashing; higher faucet heights can cause flow instability that increases splashing. Sequencing did **not** identify foodborne pathogens, only organisms in the same genera as known pathogens (e.g. Pseudomonas, Serratia, Staphylococcus, Aeromonas).
- **Background cited by the paper:** USDA's 2013 campaign was based on a 2003 study that used red-dyed water to see how far droplets travel outside a sink; it did not test for microbes.
- **Limits:** sterile water and lab setup, not a real kitchen; transfer judged visually from plate growth, not quantified; no Salmonella or Campylobacter counts; it shows the splash route, not the number of illnesses.

## 3. USDA FSIS / RTI / NC State kitchen study (2019 release)

- **Source:** USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, *Washing Raw Poultry: Our Science, Your Choice* (2019 release and study of home meal preparation, conducted by RTI International with NC State).
- **URLs read:** https://abc7news.com/washing-poultry-food-related-illnesses-usda-healthcheck/5481670/ **[opened, news]** and https://www.food-safety.com/articles/6318-usda-to-consumers-do-not-wash-raw-poultry **[opened, news]**. The FSIS page itself returned HTTP 403 and was not read.
- **Figures safe to use:** about **25%** of side salads became contaminated; **75%** of volunteers did not wash their hands (properly), although they said they did. FSIS administrator Carmen Rottenberg: "The only way to kill it is by cooking it to a safe internal temperature of 165 degrees." USDA Deputy Under Secretary Mindy Brashears: "The best practice is not to wash poultry."
- **Seen only in search summaries (not used in the script):** 60% of washers had bacteria in the sink; 14% still after cleaning; 26% lettuce transfer. The "14%" is, however, confirmed in a different form by the CDC page below ("1 in 7").
- **Limits:** news coverage, not the report; sample size not seen.

## 4. CDC, Chicken and Food Poisoning

- **URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/chicken.html **[opened]**
- **Key statements:** raw chicken is ready to cook and doesn't need to be washed first; if you wash it anyway, run the water gently and clean and sanitize the sink afterward; use a food thermometer to confirm a safe internal temperature of **165°F**; USDA study: "1 in 7 people who cleaned their sink after washing chicken still had germs in the sink"; about **1 million** people in the US get sick each year from eating contaminated poultry; **1 in 25** chicken packages at the grocery store are contaminated with Salmonella; Salmonella causes more foodborne illness than any other bacteria; use a separate cutting board for raw chicken; wash boards, utensils and counters with hot, soapy water after preparing chicken.
- **Limits:** agency summary; the page cites a USDA study without giving n.

## 5. CDC, burden of foodborne illness and older adults

- **URL:** https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/about/index.html **[opened]**
- **Figures:** about **48 million** illnesses, **128,000** hospitalizations and **3,000** deaths per year in the US. Adults **65 and older** are among the groups more likely to become very sick, with children under 5, people with weakened immune systems and pregnant women.
- **Mechanisms [secondary, search summaries of archived CDC pages]:** aging weakens how well the immune system spots and clears germs; stomach acid output declines with age (CDC Listeria page); diarrhea and dehydration are especially dangerous for older adults. The script states these in general terms ("may", "tends to") only.
- **Not used:** a "roughly half of people 65+ with these infections are hospitalized" figure, which was seen only on a secondary site (AARP).

## 6. Education campaign pilot (Drexel)

- **Citation:** Henley SC, et al. *Don't Wash Your Chicken!: A Food Safety Education Campaign to Address a Common Food Mishandling Practice.* Food Protection Trends. 2016.
- **URL:** https://www.foodprotection.org/publications/food-protection-trends/archive/2016-01-don-t-wash-your-chicken-a-food-safety-education-campaign-to-address-a-common-food-mishandlin/ **[secondary: the PDF returned unreadable binary; details from a search summary]**
- **Design (per summary):** comic-style (fotonovela) brochures and videos, posttest-only design with control and intervention sites, surveys after four weeks. The intervention group showed significantly better behavior toward not washing, knowledge that washing doesn't reduce illness risk, and self-efficacy.
- **Limits:** self-reported survey outcomes; no contamination measurement. The script mentions it only briefly and in general terms.

## 7. UK Food Standards Agency, Campylobacter

- **URL:** https://www.food.gov.uk/safety-hygiene/campylobacter **[secondary: surfaced in search results; the page text was not opened]**
- **Statement (per search summary):** washing raw chicken can spread Campylobacter by splashing it onto hands, work surfaces, clothing and cooking equipment.
- **Limits:** UK agency; used only for the Campylobacter point, with attribution to the FSA.

## What was not found / not verified

- No study that measured **illness** (not contamination) caused by washing chicken at home. The script says so.
- The 2003 USDA dye study was only read about via the Chickensplash paper. The "71 cm" splash distance seen in another paper was a secondary citation and is **not** used.
- Mazengia et al. 2015 (56 households, hands properly washed after raw poultry only 12% of the time) was seen only in a search summary; **not used**.
- A statement that USDA says brining or washing in salt water, vinegar or lemon juice does not destroy bacteria came from a search summary of FSIS material. It is stated in the script as USDA's position in one sentence; it is consistent with the principle that only heat kills the germs.
- Practical tips not drawn from a specific study: pat dry with paper towels; 20 seconds of hand washing with warm soapy water and refrigerating leftovers within two hours (common CDC guidance repeated across coverage).

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Raw chicken often carries Salmonella and Campylobacter (CDC: about 1 in 25 packages with Salmonella).
- Rinsing raw chicken splashes bacteria off the meat (lab study with video and agar plates).
- In real-kitchen studies using a tracer organism, the sink and salad became contaminated; sinks were contaminated whether or not people washed the chicken, so hands and surfaces matter as much as the water.
- Cooking to 165°F is what makes chicken safe; USDA and CDC advise against washing it.
- Adults 65 and older are among the groups more likely to become very sick from foodborne germs (CDC).

**Uncertain**
- How many real illnesses come from rinsing specifically (not measured).
- Whether the splashes in a typical kitchen carry enough live Salmonella or Campylobacter to cause disease; the lab study found only relatives of pathogens, and the kitchen study used a tracer.
- Exact effectiveness of different sink-cleaning methods (CDC cites 1 in 7 still positive after cleaning).

**False**
- That rinsing makes chicken safe, or that rinsing with vinegar, lemon juice or salt water kills the germs.
- That skipping the rinse by itself keeps the kitchen safe: contamination spread in the study anyway.
