# Research notes: calcium and vitamin D supplements and fractures after 60

Topic: "Do calcium and vitamin D supplements prevent fractures after 60?" (slug `calcium-vitamin-d-fractures`).
Pages opened for this note (via WebFetch/WebSearch, 30 Oct 2026): PubMed records for LeBoff 2022, Jackson 2006, Chapuy 1992, the USPSTF 2018 statement and Qaseem 2017; the USPSTF recommendation page; the JAMA Network Open page for Yao 2019; the BMJ paper of Bolland 2011 (PMC); the Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation (BHOF) calcium/vitamin D patient page.
Pages that did NOT open (403 / bot check): NIH Office of Dietary Supplements calcium fact sheets, NCBI IOM book, PubMed page of Hsia 2007, the MESA calcium paper (Anderson 2016). Nothing from those pages is used in the script.

## 1. VITAL: vitamin D3 alone, community-dwelling US adults (no benefit)
- Figures: vitamin D3 2,000 IU/day vs placebo. Total fractures 769/12,927 (vitamin D) vs 782/12,944 (placebo), HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.08, P = 0.70). Nonvertebral HR 0.97 (0.87–1.07). Hip fracture HR 1.01 (0.70–1.47). No effect modification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI or baseline serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D.
- Design/population: ancillary study of the VITAL randomized trial (2×2 factorial with omega-3), n = 25,871; men ≥ 50 and women ≥ 55 in the US; not selected for vitamin D deficiency, low bone mass or osteoporosis. Median follow-up 5.3 years. Fractures self-reported and adjudicated from records.
- Source: LeBoff MS, et al. Supplemental vitamin D and incident fractures in midlife and older adults. N Engl J Med. 2022. PMID 35939577. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35939577/
- Limits: tested vitamin D only (no calcium); participants not selected for deficiency or osteoporosis, so it cannot speak to people with those problems; the abstract did not give mean age or the number of men and women with very low vitamin D.

## 2. Women's Health Initiative calcium + vitamin D trial (modest bone density gain, no significant fracture drop, more kidney stones)
- Figures: 1,000 mg elemental calcium (carbonate) + 400 IU vitamin D3 daily vs placebo, average 7.0 years. Hip bone density 1.06% higher (P < 0.01). Hip fracture HR 0.88 (0.72–1.08). Clinical spine fracture HR 0.90 (0.74–1.10). Total fractures HR 0.96 (0.91–1.02). Kidney stones HR 1.17 (1.02–1.34). When women were censored after they stopped taking the study pills, hip fracture HR 0.71 (0.52–0.97). Effects did not vary significantly by baseline serum vitamin D.
- Design/population: randomized, placebo-controlled; n = 36,282 postmenopausal women aged 50–79 who were already in other WHI trials.
- Source: Jackson RD, et al. Calcium plus vitamin D supplementation and the risk of fractures. N Engl J Med. 2006;354:669-683. PMID 16481635. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16481635/
- Limits: women only; many participants also took their own calcium/vitamin D pills outside the trial (noted by Bolland 2011); the adherence analysis is not intention-to-treat and is less reliable; I did not see the age 60+ subgroup, so the script does not mention it.
- USPSTF restates the stone figure: about 1 woman diagnosed with a urinary stone per 273 women supplemented over 7 years (USPSTF page, below).

## 3. USPSTF 2018 recommendation (community-dwelling adults)
- Postmenopausal women: recommends AGAINST daily 400 IU or less of vitamin D with 1,000 mg or less of calcium for primary prevention of fractures (Grade D). Higher doses in postmenopausal women, and any dose in men and premenopausal women: evidence insufficient (I statement). Adequate evidence that vitamin D + calcium increases kidney stones (judged a small harm).
- Does not apply to: people with a history of osteoporotic fracture, increased fall risk, diagnosed osteoporosis or vitamin D deficiency; people in nursing homes or other institutions; the evidence review also excluded people taking osteoporosis-associated drugs (long-term steroids).
- Falls: separate USPSTF recommendation against vitamin D to prevent falls in community-dwelling adults 65+; recommends exercise for fall prevention in older adults at increased risk. A high-dose annual regimen was linked to more falls in one study. The page says an update is in progress.
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force. Vitamin D, calcium, or combined supplementation for the primary prevention of fractures in community-dwelling adults: US Preventive Services Task Force recommendation statement. JAMA. 2018;319(15):1592-1599. PMID 29677309. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29677309/ and https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vitamin-d-calcium-or-combined-supplementation-for-the-primary-prevention-of-fractures-in-adults-preventive-medication (final statement 17 April 2018).
- Limits: 2018; an update is in progress, so the script says "in 2018".

## 4. Chapuy 1992: women in institutions (benefit)
- Figures: 3,270 ambulatory women, mean age 84 ± 6; 1,634 got tricalcium phosphate (1.2 g elemental calcium) + 800 IU (20 µg) vitamin D3 daily, 1,636 got double placebo; 18 months. Among women who completed the study, hip fractures were 43% lower (P = 0.043) and nonvertebral fractures 32% lower (P = 0.015); intention-to-treat gave similar results. Parathyroid hormone fell 44%; serum 25(OH)D rose 162%. In a subset (n = 56) proximal femur density rose 2.7% in the treatment group and fell 4.6% on placebo.
- Population: per the paper's methods, women living in 180 nursing homes or apartment houses for older people (seen in a search excerpt of the full text; the PubMed abstract only says "healthy ambulatory women").
- Source: Chapuy MC, et al. Vitamin D3 and calcium to prevent hip fractures in elderly women. N Engl J Med. 1992;327(23):1637-1642. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1331788/
- Limits: 1992; only women; very old; the 43% and 32% figures are for completers; hormone and density measurements were in small subsets. It combined calcium and vitamin D, so it cannot separate their effects.

## 5. Yao 2019 meta-analysis
- Figures: vitamin D alone, 11 RCTs, 34,243 participants: any fracture RR 1.06 (0.98–1.14), hip RR 1.14 (0.98–1.32). Calcium + vitamin D, 6 RCTs, 49,282 participants: any fracture RR 0.94 (0.89–0.99), hip RR 0.84 (0.72–0.97). Risk of bias: 9 of 11 vitamin D trials high, 1 uncertain, 1 low; 5 of 6 calcium + vitamin D trials high, 1 low. Authors: vitamin D alone not associated with lower risk; calcium + vitamin D "a more promising strategy" but larger trials needed before recommending it for hip fracture prevention.
- Source: Yao P, et al. Vitamin D and calcium for the prevention of fracture: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Netw Open. 2019. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.17789 (page: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2757873)
- Limits: I read the first 100,000 characters (abstract and most of the body); mostly high risk of bias; trials differ in population (some in institutions).

## 6. Calcium supplements and heart attacks (secondary analysis; uncertain)
- Figures: reanalysis of WHI limited-access data (36,282 women): among the 16,718 women (46%) not taking personal calcium supplements at randomization, cardiovascular hazard ratios with calcium + vitamin D were 1.13–1.22; no change in women taking personal calcium. Meta-analysis of 3 placebo-controlled trials of calcium + vitamin D: MI RR 1.21 (1.01–1.44), stroke RR 1.20 (1.00–1.43). Trial-level meta-analysis of calcium with or without vitamin D (8 trials + WHI non-users, 28,072 participants, 1,384 MI/stroke events): MI RR 1.24 (1.07–1.45), P = 0.004. Authors' conclusion: calcium supplements modestly increase cardiovascular risk, particularly MI, and the role of calcium supplements in osteoporosis management warrants reassessment.
- Source: Bolland MJ, Grey A, Avenell A, Gamble GD, Reid IR. Calcium supplements with or without vitamin D and risk of cardiovascular events: reanalysis of the Women's Health Initiative limited access dataset and meta-analysis. BMJ. 2011;342:d2040. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079822
- Limits: post hoc subgroup and trial-level pooling, not a new trial; the outcomes were not the primary endpoints in the original trials. The BHOF page says extra calcium may cause "constipation, kidney stones, or possibly heart calcification" (not proven). I could not open the MESA cohort paper on calcium supplements and coronary calcium, so the script says nothing about arterial calcium scans.

## 7. Calcium targets, food sources and supplement tips (BHOF patient page)
- Targets (total from food + supplements): women 51+ 1,200 mg/day; men 70 and younger 1,000 mg; men 71+ 1,200 mg. Vitamin D 800–1,000 IU/day for adults 50+. Upper limit of vitamin D 4,000 IU/day for most adults (NAM/NIH, as cited on the page). No calcium UL given; extra calcium "offers no added benefit and may cause constipation, kidney stones, or possibly heart calcification".
- Estimated calcium: plain low-fat yogurt 6 oz 310 mg; Greek yogurt 6 oz 200 mg; milk 8 oz 300 mg; canned salmon with bones 3 oz 180 mg; canned sardines with bones 3 oz 325 mg; fortified soy/almond/rice/oat milk 8 oz 300 mg; fortified orange juice 8 oz 300 mg; frozen collard greens 8 oz 360 mg; cheddar 1 oz 205 mg. Values are estimates; labels vary.
- Supplements: absorbed best in amounts of 500–600 mg or less at a time; spread through the day with meals; most supplements (carbonate) with food, calcium citrate with or without food; start with 200–300 mg; check "elemental calcium" on the label; ask provider/pharmacist about drug interactions. Earlier BHOF guide text (2014 edition, seen in search excerpts only): diet first, supplements if diet insufficient. The 2022 edition's numbers were not seen.
- Source: Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation. Calcium/vitamin D (patient page). https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/calciumvitamin-d/
- Limits: advocacy foundation page, not a trial; USPSTF reads the evidence for supplements more cautiously.

## 8. When doctors do prescribe: ACP guideline 2017
- Women with known osteoporosis: offer alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid or denosumab to reduce hip and spine fractures (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence). Treat for 5 years (weak). Men with clinically recognized osteoporosis: offer bisphosphonates for spine fracture risk (weak). Osteopenia in women 65+ at high fracture risk: discuss treatment with the patient, weighing preferences, risk, benefits, harms, costs (weak). Not recommended for osteoporosis in women: menopausal estrogen or raloxifene.
- Source: Qaseem A, Forciea MA, McLean RM, Denberg TD; Clinical Guidelines Committee of the ACP. Treatment of low bone density or osteoporosis to prevent fractures in men and women: a clinical practice guideline update from the American College of Physicians. Ann Intern Med. 2017;166(11):818-839. PMID 28492856. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28492856/
- Limits: 2017; the abstract did not give a calcium/vitamin D recommendation, so the script does not say supplements are prescribed alongside these drugs.

## Honest verdict
- Well supported: In healthy community-dwelling adults, vitamin D alone (VITAL, 2,000 IU, n = 25,871) did not lower fractures. The WHI (n = 36,282) found a small hip bone density gain, no significant fracture reduction in the intention-to-treat analysis, and more kidney stones. The USPSTF recommends against low-dose calcium + vitamin D for fracture prevention in postmenopausal women living at home and calls the evidence for higher doses and for men insufficient. Women with known osteoporosis are offered prescription medicines, strongly recommended by the ACP.
- Uncertain: whether calcium + vitamin D lowers fractures in frail people in care (Chapuy 1992 says yes; Yao 2019 pooled data lean that way but with mostly high-risk-of-bias trials). Whether calcium pills raise heart attack risk (secondary analysis, authors say yes, modestly; no new trial). Whether food calcium works better than pills for fractures: guidelines prefer food, but no trial in my sources compares them directly for fractures.
- False or unsupported: that every older adult needs a calcium + vitamin D pill to protect their bones; that more is better (BHOF: extra calcium offers no added benefit); that high-dose vitamin D prevents falls (USPSTF recommends against it for community-dwelling adults 65+).
