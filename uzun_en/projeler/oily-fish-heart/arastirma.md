# Research notes: oily fish vs fish-oil pills and the heart

Slug: `oily-fish-heart`. Audience: US adults, mostly 60+.

Access note: PubMed pages were blocked (cookie wall). Figures below come from Europe PMC records, the ACC trial summary page, the FDA page, and search excerpts of AHA material. Where I saw only a secondary summary, I say so.

## 1. VITAL (fish oil, healthy older adults)
- **Citation:** Manson JE, Cook NR, Lee IM, et al. Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. N Engl J Med. 2019;380(1):23-32. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1811403. PMID 30415637.
- **Design:** randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 2x2 factorial with vitamin D3 (2,000 IU/day); this paper reports the n-3 comparison.
- **Population:** n = 25,871 US adults, men 50 or older and women 55 or older (includes 5,106 Black participants). Participants had no known cardiovascular disease or cancer at entry (from the paper's abstract; not re-verified in this session's excerpt).
- **Dose and follow-up:** 1 g/day marine n-3 fatty acids vs placebo; median 5.3 years.
- **Results (n-3 vs placebo):**
  - Major CV events (MI, stroke, CV death): 386 vs 419 participants; HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.80-1.06; P=0.24).
  - Invasive cancer: 820 vs 797; HR 1.03 (0.93-1.13).
  - Total MI: HR 0.72 (0.59-0.90). This was a secondary outcome.
  - Stroke: HR 1.04 (0.83-1.31). CV death: HR 0.96 (0.76-1.21). All-cause death: HR 1.02.
  - No excess bleeding or other serious adverse events.
- **Limits:** the result is for supplement-taking healthy older adults, not people with heart disease; MI finding is a secondary outcome (treated as hypothesis-generating); background fish intake varied.

## 2. ASCEND (fish oil, adults with diabetes)
- **Citation:** ASCEND Study Collaborative Group. Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. N Engl J Med. 2018;379:1540-1550. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1804989. PMID 30146932.
- **Design/population:** randomized, placebo-controlled (olive oil); n = 15,480 adults with diabetes and no known atherosclerotic CVD; 1 g/day n-3; mean follow-up 7.4 years.
- **Results:** first serious vascular event in 8.9% (n-3) vs 9.2% (placebo); rate ratio 0.97 (95% CI 0.87-1.08; P=0.55). No difference in all-cause death or serious adverse events.
- **Limits:** people with diabetes only; 1 g/day dose.

## 3. REDUCE-IT (prescription EPA)
- **Citation:** Bhatt DL, Steg PG, Miller M, et al. Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. N Engl J Med. 2019;380:11-22. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1812792. PMID 30415628.
- **Figures** (confirmed through a search summary of the paper, since the abstract itself could not be opened): n = 8,179 statin-treated adults with high triglycerides, 70.7% secondary prevention; icosapent ethyl 4 g/day vs placebo; median follow-up 4.9 years.
- **Primary composite endpoint:** 17.2% vs 22.0%; HR 0.75 (95% CI 0.68-0.83; P<0.001).
- **CV death:** 4.3% vs 5.2%; HR 0.80 (0.66-0.98).
- **Safety:** hospitalization for atrial fibrillation/flutter 3.1% vs 2.1% (P=0.004); serious bleeding 2.7% vs 2.1% (P=0.06).
- **Limits:** high-risk, high-triglyceride patients on statins; the result may not apply to people with normal triglycerides; the reason EPA-only worked while STRENGTH did not is debated (not resolved). Placebo-oil criticisms exist but I did not verify them and do not use them in the script.

## 4. STRENGTH (prescription EPA+DHA)
- **Citation:** Nicholls SJ, Lincoff AM, Garcia M, et al. Effect of High-Dose Omega-3 Fatty Acids vs Corn Oil on Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in Patients at High Cardiovascular Risk. JAMA. 2020;324(22):2268-2280. Details verified at the ACC trial summary: https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Clinical-Trials/2020/11/11/21/29/STRENGTH
- **Design/population:** randomized, double-blind; n = 13,078 statin-treated adults, mean age 63, 35% female, 70% diabetes; triglycerides 180-500 mg/dL; omega-3 carboxylic acids (EPA+DHA) 4 g/day vs corn oil; median follow-up 42 months.
- **Results:** primary composite 12.0% vs 12.2% (P=0.84). Atrial fibrillation 2.2% vs 1.3% (P<0.001). GI adverse events 24.7% vs 14.7%. Stopped early for low probability of benefit (futility).
- **Limits:** the hazard ratio was not on the page I saw, so the script uses only the percentages. A search excerpt gave HR 0.99 (0.90-1.09) but I did not verify it, so it is not used.

## 5. Omega-3 supplements and atrial fibrillation
- **Citation:** Gencer B, Djousse L, Al-Ramady OT, et al. Effect of Long-Term Marine omega-3 Fatty Acids Supplementation on the Risk of Atrial Fibrillation in Randomized Controlled Trials of Cardiovascular Outcomes. Circulation. 2021;144:1981-1990. PMID 34612056 (seen via search summary).
- **Design:** meta-analysis of 7 RCTs, 81,210 patients, weighted mean follow-up 4.9 years.
- **Result:** pooled HR for AF 1.25 (95% CI 1.07-1.46; P=.013). Above 1 g/day: HR 1.49; at 1 g/day or less: HR 1.12 (P for interaction <.001).
- **Limits:** absolute risks are modest; authors say AF risk should be balanced against benefit on atherosclerotic outcomes.

## 6. Whole fish and coronary heart disease
- **Citation:** Zhang B, Xiong K, Cai J, Ma A. Fish Consumption and Coronary Heart Disease: A Meta-Analysis. Nutrients. 2020;12(8):2278. DOI 10.3390/nu12082278. PMID 32751304.
- **Design:** meta-analysis of 40 prospective cohort studies.
- **Results:** highest vs lowest fish intake: CHD incidence RR 0.91 (95% CI 0.84-0.97; 22 studies, 918,783 participants, 28,261 cases); CHD mortality RR 0.85 (0.77-0.94; 27 studies, 1,139,553 participants, 10,568 events). Each extra 20 g/day of fish was associated with about 4% lower incidence and about 4% lower mortality.
- **Limits:** observational; residual confounding (healthier lifestyles in fish eaters); heterogeneity I2 about 47-51%; mixed populations, not specific to 60+.

## 7. Fish intake in the VITAL cohort (a caution)
- **Citation:** Caldeira D, Nogueira-Garcia B, Abreu A, Pinto FJ. Fish intake and risk of cardiovascular events: an analysis of the VITAL cohort. Eur J Clin Nutr. 2023. DOI 10.1038/s41430-022-01244-w. PMID 36482183.
- **Result:** n = 25,435; 11,921 ate at least 1.5 servings/week, 13,514 less. At least 1.5 servings/week was not independently associated with fewer CV outcomes (reported HR 0.78, 99% CI 0.57-1.07; outcome not specified in the excerpt). Authors say a benefit cannot be ruled out. The script does not use the HR.
- **Limits:** observational, secondary analysis, one cohort of older US adults.

## 8. AHA science advisory and public guidance
- **Citation:** Rimm EB, Appel LJ, Chiuve SE, et al. Seafood Long-Chain n-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids and Cardiovascular Disease: A Science Advisory From the American Heart Association. Circulation. 2018;138(1):e35-e47. DOI 10.1161/CIR.0000000000000574. PMID 29773586.
- **Content:** supports 1 to 2 seafood meals per week to reduce risk of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke and sudden cardiac death; benefit is greatest when seafood replaces less healthy foods; proposed mechanisms (antiarrhythmic, anti-inflammatory, hematologic, endothelial) mostly need longer dietary trials.
- **AHA public guidance** (heart.org news item, 25 May 2018, https://www.heart.org/en/news/2018/05/25/eating-fish-twice-a-week-reduces-heart-stroke-risk; seen via search excerpts, the page returned 403): two 3.5-ounce servings of non-fried fish a week (about three quarters of a cup flaked); oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, herring, lake trout, sardines, albacore tuna; two studies of over 90,000 Americans linked fried fish at least weekly with up to 48% higher heart failure risk; earlier AHA advisory does not recommend supplements to prevent CVD in the general public.
- **Limits:** the 3.5-ounce and 48% figures come from the news item's excerpt, not the advisory text.

## 9. FDA/EPA mercury guidance
- **Source:** FDA, Advice About Eating Fish. https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish (page opened).
- **Best Choices (lower mercury):** includes anchovy, herring, salmon, sardine, trout (freshwater), Atlantic mackerel, canned light tuna (including skipjack), cod, shrimp and others.
- **Good Choices:** includes albacore/white tuna (canned and fresh/frozen), yellowfin tuna, halibut, snapper.
- **Choices to Avoid (highest mercury):** king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, tilefish (Gulf of Mexico), bigeye tuna.
- A serving can be estimated with the palm of the hand. The explicit servings-per-week numbers on the page are for pregnancy, breastfeeding and children, so the script does not state them for other adults.
- **Limits:** the page ranks species; it does not give mercury numbers I could verify here.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- Large trials (VITAL, ASCEND) found that plain 1 g/day fish oil capsules did not significantly cut major cardiovascular events in healthy older adults or adults with diabetes.
- Omega-3 supplements are linked with more atrial fibrillation in pooled trials, more at higher doses.
- The AHA recommends eating fish about twice a week, non-fried, and lists sardines and salmon among suitable fish.
- The FDA lists sardines, salmon, herring and canned light tuna as Best Choices for mercury.

**Uncertain**
- Why prescription EPA (REDUCE-IT) lowered events while EPA+DHA (STRENGTH) did not.
- Whether the lower heart risk with fish eating is caused by fish itself, the omega-3, other nutrients, or fish replacing less healthy foods. Evidence is observational and long dietary trials are lacking.
- The 28% lower total MI in VITAL (secondary outcome).
- Whether supplements help people who eat almost no fish (not directly tested in these results).

**False or misleading**
- "Fish oil pills protect your heart just like eating fish" is not supported by the large trials.
- "All fish are high in mercury" is not accurate: the FDA lists sardines, salmon and canned light tuna among the lower-mercury Best Choices.
- "Prescription omega-3 proves over-the-counter capsules work" is wrong: the prescription trials used about 4 g/day in high-risk patients with high triglycerides.
