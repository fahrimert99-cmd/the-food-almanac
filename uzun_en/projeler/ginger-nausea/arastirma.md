# Research notes: Does ginger really settle an upset stomach?

Slug: `ginger-nausea`. Notes written for the script in `proje.json`.

How these sources were read: PubMed pages would not open (cookie wall), so the abstracts were read through the Europe PMC record of each PMID. NCCIH, MSKCC and the PMC open-access case report were opened directly. Summaries below use only what those records stated. Where a source did not state something (for example, a ginger dose in a review), it is marked "not stated".

## 1. Nausea and vomiting after surgery (original pooled trials and meta-analyses)

### 1a. Chaiyakunapruk 2006 (older meta-analysis)
- Citation: Chaiyakunapruk N, Kitikannakorn N, Nathisuwan S, Leeprakobboon K, Leelasettagool C. The efficacy of ginger for the prevention of postoperative nausea and vomiting: a meta-analysis. Am J Obstet Gynecol. 2006;194(1):95-99. PMID 16389016. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16389016/
- Design: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials; two reviewers selected studies and extracted data. Objective: effect of a fixed dose of ginger vs placebo on nausea and vomiting in the 24 hours after surgery.
- Population/n: 5 randomized trials, 363 patients pooled.
- Result: summary relative risk 0.69 for postoperative nausea and vomiting (about 31% lower risk) and 0.61 for postoperative vomiting (about 39% lower). Only side effect reported: abdominal discomfort.
- Conclusion: a fixed dose of at least 1 g ginger was more effective than placebo.
- Limitations: five small trials, short-term (24 hours); a pooled-trials result from 2006, since superseded by larger pools. The relative risk figures are the study's own; the percentages above are simple 1 minus RR conversions.

### 1b. Lu 2022 (larger, newer meta-analysis)
- Citation: Lu C, Chen X, Yan X, He J, Nie Z. Effects of ginger on postoperative nausea and vomiting: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (preventive and relieving effects). Int J Nurs Stud. 2022;125:104094 (epub 1 Oct 2021). DOI 10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2021.104094. PMID 34700257. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34700257/ (registered CRD42020220916)
- Design: random-effects meta-analysis of RCTs, searched through November 2020 (Chinese and English databases).
- Population/n: 14 trials, 1,506 patients.
- Result: ginger gave significantly lower nausea scores than placebo at 2, 6 and 12 hours after surgery. Except in the recovery room, no significant association with vomiting, combined nausea and vomiting, or antiemetic drug use.
- Conclusion (authors): ginger can reduce postoperative nausea; more high-quality trials needed.
- Limitations: heterogeneous trials, differing ginger forms and doses; the quality of the pooled trials is limited (see overview 3c).
- Note: the exact title wording was paraphrased by the page reader; use the journal, volume and DOI for citation.

## 2. Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV)

### 2a. Ryan 2012 (large randomized trial)
- Citation: Ryan JL, Heckler CE, Roscoe JA, Dakhil SR, Kirshner J, Flynn PJ, Hickok JT, Morrow GR. Ginger (Zingiber officinale) reduces acute chemotherapy-induced nausea: a URCC CCOP study of 576 patients. Support Care Cancer. 2012;20(7):1479-1489. PMID 21818642. (URCC = University of Rochester Cancer Center.)
- Design: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, four arms: placebo, 0.5 g, 1.0 g or 1.5 g ginger daily, as 250 mg capsules (three twice daily at the top dose), taken for 6 days starting 3 days before chemotherapy.
- Population/n: 744 randomized, 576 analyzed; adults having chemotherapy; 91% female, mean age 53.
- Result: all ginger doses reduced Day 1 (acute) nausea vs placebo (p = 0.003). Largest reductions at 0.5 g (p = 0.017) and 1.0 g (p = 0.036); the 1.5 g dose was not the best.
- Limitations: mostly women with a mean age of 53 (not an older-adult trial); acute nausea only; the abstract does not give the effect in absolute symptom units.

### 2b. Crichton 2019 (systematic review update)
- Citation: Crichton M, Marshall S, Marx W, McCarthy AL, Isenring E. Efficacy of ginger (Zingiber officinale) in ameliorating chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and chemotherapy-related outcomes: a systematic review update and meta-analysis. J Acad Nutr Diet. 2019;119(12):2055-2068. PMID 31519467. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31519467/
- Design/n: systematic review and meta-analysis, 18 articles in adults having chemotherapy.
- Result: ginger at 1 g/day or less for more than 3 days was associated with reduced acute vomiting. Ginger for under 3 days was associated with less fatigue (one study only). No significant association with overall or delayed vomiting, or with nausea.
- Conclusion: ginger "might" benefit CINV vomiting and fatigue; stronger designs and standardized ginger products needed.
- Limitations: heterogeneous products and doses.

### 2c. Context
- A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (Li M et al., Asian Biomedicine 2026;20(4):272-290, PMC13718367) reported non-significant pooled effects on acute and delayed nausea and vomiting, with benefit only in patients under 50 (read via search summary only; not used in the script).
- NCCIH: "It's uncertain whether ginger is a helpful addition to standard treatments" for chemotherapy-related and post-surgery nausea. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger

## 3. Motion sickness, pregnancy, and the overall picture

### 3a. Lien 2003 (small lab trial, motion sickness)
- Citation: Lien HC, Sun WM, Chen YH, Kim H, Hasler W, Owyang C. Effects of ginger on motion sickness and gastric slow-wave dysrhythmias induced by circular vection. Am J Physiol Gastrointest Liver Physiol. 2003;284(3):G481-G489. PMID 12576305.
- Design: double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled crossover; nausea induced by circular vection (an apparatus that creates a feeling of spinning).
- n: 13 volunteers with a history of motion sickness.
- Result: baseline maximal nausea score 2.5 +/- 0.2 on a 0-3 scale. Ginger 1,000 mg and 2,000 mg reduced nausea, tachygastric activity (an irregular, fast stomach rhythm) and plasma vasopressin. Ginger 2,000 mg did not change nausea or bradygastria produced by infusing vasopressin.
- Limitations: n = 13, induced nausea in a lab, healthy volunteers, short. The exact capsule form is not stated in the abstract.

### 3b. NCCIH (US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health)
- Page: Ginger. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger (opened).
- "Research shows that ginger may be helpful for nausea and vomiting associated with pregnancy." For motion sickness, most studies do not show benefit. For chemotherapy-related and post-surgery nausea, "It's uncertain whether ginger is a helpful addition to standard treatments." Possible side effects when taken by mouth include abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea, and mouth and throat irritation. "If you take any type of medicine, talk with your health care provider before using ginger." No dose is given. Blood thinners are not specifically mentioned on this page.

### 3c. Overview of reviews, 2024
- Citation: Li Z, Wu J, Song J, Wen Y. Ginger for treating nausea and vomiting: an overview of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Int J Food Sci Nutr. 2024;75(2):122-133 (online Dec 2023). DOI 10.1080/09637486.2023.2284647. PMID 38072785.
- 15 meta-analyses; methodological quality "critically low to low". Authors conclude ginger may help CINV in cancer patients, reduce PONV and ease pregnancy symptoms, and describe it as a safe botanical option, while noting that the quality of published meta-analyses needs to improve.

## 4. Forms, doses and ginger ale
- Doses in the evidence: 1 g or more fixed dose (Chaiyakunapruk 2006); 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 g/day in capsules (Ryan 2012); 1 g or less per day for more than 3 days (Crichton 2019); 1,000 and 2,000 mg single doses (Lien 2003). All are measured amounts, mostly capsules. No trial reviewed here used tea or fresh root; the ginger content of a mug of tea is not standardized (my inference; not a cited number).
- Ginger ale: consumers brought a US class action against Dr Pepper Snapple Group (Canada Dry) saying a "made with real ginger" label was misleading because ginger was not in the ingredient list; lab tests cited in a 2018 lawsuit reportedly found only a trace of ginger. The "made with real ginger" claim was removed from US packaging in 2019, and the US label now reads "less than 2% ginger extract" (derived from ginger oleoresin). Source: news article opened at https://www.aol.com/articles/ginger-ale-brand-sued-not-231700778.html (a news report, not a peer-reviewed analysis; no peer-reviewed study of ginger content across brands was found). The script only states the one brand's label and the lawsuit, and tells viewers to read the ingredient list.

## 5. Safety: blood thinners, surgery, blood sugar
- MSKCC About Herbs page for ginger: https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ginger (opened). Ginger supplements may increase the risk of bleeding; interactions with warfarin and other blood thinners; NSAIDs listed; a case report of an 80-year-old on a blood thinner with fatal bleeding after a ginger-cinnamon mixture; ginger supplements to be stopped about 2 weeks before surgery and not used right after surgery for nausea; ginger may lower blood glucose (relevant with insulin or glucose-lowering drugs); may raise tacrolimus levels. Common side effects: heartburn, skin irritation. Not suggested in pregnancy (MSKCC), which contrasts with NCCIH's view on pregnancy nausea, so pregnancy is left to "ask your clinician" and is not covered in the script.
- Rubin D, Patel V, Dietrich E. Effects of oral ginger supplementation on the INR. Case Rep Med. 2019;2019 (PMC6594244; PMID 31281366). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6594244/ (opened). Case report: a 70-year-old woman on long-term warfarin (7.5 mg daily, 10 mg Wednesdays) with stable other medicines started a daily 48 mg chewable ginger supplement. INR was 2.7 a month earlier and 8.0 at the visit, with no reported bleeding; after 3 warfarin doses were held and ginger stopped, INR was 2.6 a week later. The authors note the literature is inconsistent and that unregulated supplements may contain unlisted ingredients.
  - The same paper summarizes: Jiang et al. 2005, an open-label three-way crossover randomized study in healthy subjects, in which a ginger product equivalent to 0.4 g of rhizome powder did not significantly affect clotting; and Shalansky et al. 2007, a prospective longitudinal study that linked ginger use with a higher bleeding risk in anticoagulated patients but not with supratherapeutic INR. (Both are cited second-hand through Rubin 2019; the original abstracts were not opened.)
- Limitations: the strongest alarms are single case reports; controlled data are small and mixed. The honest message is "uncertain, ask first", not "dangerous".

## Honest verdict
**Reasonably well supported**
- Ginger, taken as a measured dose of about 1 gram a day (range in trials 0.5 to 2 g), may lower nausea scores after surgery (Lu 2022, 14 trials, 1,506 patients; Chaiyakunapruk 2006, RR 0.69 for nausea and vomiting after surgery).
- Side effects in trials were mild and digestive (abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea).

**Uncertain**
- Vomiting: not reliably reduced after surgery (Lu 2022 outside the recovery room); in chemotherapy only acute vomiting was lower with 1 g/day or less for over 3 days (Crichton 2019), with no clear effect on delayed vomiting or nausea in that review.
- Chemotherapy nausea: the large Ryan 2012 trial found lower Day 1 nausea at all doses, but reviews are mixed and NCCIH calls it uncertain.
- Motion sickness: one tiny lab trial (n = 13) was positive; NCCIH says most studies show no benefit.
- Bleeding risk with blood thinners: case reports and a mixed pair of controlled studies; MSKCC advises caution with supplements. Not proven, not ruled out.
- Quality of the review literature itself is low (Li 2024 overview).

**False or misleading**
- "Ginger ale is a ginger remedy." One major brand's US label lists less than 2 percent ginger extract, after a class action over a "made with real ginger" claim; check the ingredient list.
- "Ginger is a proven cure for nausea" or "more ginger is always better" (the 1.5 g arm in Ryan 2012 did not beat the lower doses).
- "Natural means no interactions."
