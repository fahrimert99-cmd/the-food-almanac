# Research notes: Is a gluten-free diet healthier if you don't have celiac disease?

Slug: `gluten-free-healthier`. Audience: US adults, mostly 60+. Notes written from abstracts and agency pages opened during research (Europe PMC records, NIDDK pages). PubMed and PMC pages themselves blocked automated fetching, so abstracts were read through Europe PMC's index of the same PubMed records; links below go to the PMC/PubMed/DOI pages.

## 1. Who truly needs a gluten-free diet

**Celiac disease, wheat allergy, non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) — agency definitions**
- NIDDK (NIH), "Definition & Facts for Celiac Disease": "experts estimate about 2 million people in the United States have celiac disease"; about 1 percent of people worldwide; many affected people are undiagnosed. Wheat allergy = immune reaction to wheat; symptoms (such as itchy eyes or trouble breathing) differ from celiac disease and it does not cause long-term damage to the small intestine. NCGS = symptoms that can resemble celiac disease (abdominal pain, tiredness) but without damage to the small intestine.
- URL: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease/definition-facts
- Limitation: agency page, no study design. Gluten described as protein in wheat, barley and rye (standard NIDDK/clinical description).

**Global prevalence**
- Singh P, Arora A, Strand TA, Leffler DA, Catassi C, Green PH, Kelly CP, Ahuja V, Makharia GK. Global Prevalence of Celiac Disease: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol. 2018;16(6):823-836.e2. PMID 29551598. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29551598/
- Design: systematic review and meta-analysis, 96 studies included (from 3,843 articles; searches Jan 1991–Mar 2016).
- Pooled prevalence by blood antibodies (tTG-IgA and/or EMA): **1.4%** (95% CI 1.1–1.7), 275,818 individuals. Biopsy-confirmed: **0.7%** (95% CI 0.5–0.9), 138,792 individuals. North America 0.5%.
- Limitations: heterogeneity by country, many countries without data; antibody-positive is not the same as confirmed disease.

**Gluten avoidance without celiac disease is rising in the US**
- Choung RS, Unalp-Arida A, Ruhl CE, Brantner TL, Everhart JE, Murray JA. Less hidden celiac disease but increased gluten avoidance without a diagnosis in the United States: findings from NHANES 2009 to 2014. Mayo Clin Proc. 2017. PMC5459670. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5459670
- Design: US national cross-sectional survey (NHANES), 2009–2014 cycles.
- Share of people WITHOUT celiac disease who followed a gluten-free diet: **0.52%** (2009–10), 0.99% (2011–12), **1.69%** (2013–14), i.e. more than tripled. Celiac prevalence stable at about 0.6–0.8%.
- Limitations: survey, self-report; the study cannot say why people avoided gluten.

## 2. Key study: gluten intake and heart disease (the "Harvard cohort")

- Lebwohl B, Cao Y, Zong G, Hu FB, Green PHR, Neugut AI, Rimm EB, Sampson L, Dougherty LW, Giovannucci E, Willett WC, Sun Q, Chan AT. Long term gluten consumption in adults without celiac disease and risk of coronary heart disease: prospective cohort study. BMJ. 2017;357:j1892. PMID 28465308. DOI 10.1136/bmj.j1892. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5421459
- Design: prospective cohort. 64,714 women (Nurses' Health Study) and 45,303 men (Health Professionals Follow-up Study), total 110,017, no history of coronary heart disease (CHD) at baseline. 131-item semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire in 1986, updated every 4 years through 2010. Gluten intake estimated from the questionnaires; ranked into fifths.
- Follow-up: 26 years, 2,273,931 person-years; 2,431 women and 4,098 men developed CHD (fatal or non-fatal myocardial infarction).
- Unadjusted incidence: **352 per 100,000 person-years** (lowest fifth of gluten) vs **277** (highest fifth), difference of 75 fewer cases per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 51–98).
- Multivariable-adjusted HR, highest vs lowest fifth: **0.95 (95% CI 0.88–1.02; P trend 0.29)**: no significant association.
- Additional adjustment for whole grains: HR **1.00 (0.92–1.09; P trend 0.77)**. Additional adjustment for refined grains: HR **0.85 (0.77–0.93; P trend 0.002)**, interpreted by the authors as lower risk linked to the whole-grain intake that travels with gluten intake.
- Authors' conclusion: long-term gluten intake was not associated with CHD risk; avoiding gluten may reduce whole grain intake, which may affect CHD risk; promoting gluten-free diets among people without celiac disease should not be encouraged.
- Limitations: observational (association, not cause); self-reported diet; mostly health professionals; residual confounding; gluten intake strongly correlated with grain type. The age of participants at baseline was not verified for these notes, so the video does not claim the cohort was older or younger.

## 3. Gluten intake and type 2 diabetes (same research group)

- Zong G, Lebwohl B, Hu FB, Sampson L, Dougherty LW, Willett WC, Chan AT, Sun Q. Gluten intake and risk of type 2 diabetes in three large prospective cohort studies of US men and women. Diabetologia. 2018. PMID 30074058. DOI 10.1007/s00125-018-4697-9. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30074058/
- Design: three prospective cohorts: NHS (n = 71,602 women), NHS II (n = 88,604 women), HPFS (n = 41,908 men), about 200,000 total (sum 202,114). FFQ every 2–4 years. 4.24 million person-years, 15,947 confirmed type 2 diabetes cases.
- Pooled HRs by fifth (lowest = reference): 0.89, 0.84, 0.78, **0.80 (0.76–0.84)** for the highest fifth; P trend < 0.001. After adjusting for cereal fiber, highest-vs-lowest HR **0.87 (0.81–0.93)**. Largely linear inverse dose–response up to 12 g/day.
- Gluten intake correlated strongly (Spearman > 0.6) with refined grains, starch and cereal fiber. Authors note that limiting gluten is also associated with lower cereal fiber intake.
- Limitations: observational; cannot show gluten protects; diet self-reported.
- Interpretation used in video: the highest-gluten fifth had about 20 percent lower risk (13 percent after cereal-fiber adjustment, 1 − 0.87). Described as an association only.

## 4. Is non-celiac gluten sensitivity gluten-specific?

- Biesiekierski JR, Newnham ED, Irving PM, Barrett JS, Haines M, Doecke JD, Shepherd SJ, Muir JG, Gibson PR. Gluten causes gastrointestinal symptoms in subjects without celiac disease: a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial. Am J Gastroenterol. 2011;106(3):508-514. PMID 21224837. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21224837/
  - Design: double-blind randomized placebo-controlled rechallenge; 34 adults with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), celiac disease excluded, symptoms controlled on gluten-free diet; gluten (two slices of bread plus one muffin daily) or placebo for up to 6 weeks.
  - Result: symptoms not adequately controlled in **13 of 19 (68%)** on gluten vs **6 of 15 (40%)** on placebo (P = 0.0001). No change in celiac antibodies, inflammation markers or intestinal permeability.
  - Limitations: small, IBS patients, short.
- Biesiekierski JR, Peters SL, Newnham ED, Rosella O, Muir JG, Gibson PR. No effects of gluten in patients with self-reported non-celiac gluten sensitivity after dietary reduction of fermentable, poorly absorbed, short-chain carbohydrates. Gastroenterology. 2013;145(2):320-328. PMID 23648697. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23648697/
  - Design: double-blind crossover; 37 adults (aged 24–61) with self-reported NCGS and IBS, no celiac disease. Two weeks of reduced-FODMAP diet, then one week each of high-gluten (16 g/day), low-gluten (2 g gluten + 14 g whey) and control (16 g whey), washout at least 2 weeks. A 3-day rechallenge in 22 participants.
  - Result: symptoms improved on reduced FODMAPs, then worsened to a similar degree with gluten or whey protein; gluten-specific effects in only **8%** of participants; no biomarker differences; not reproduced at rechallenge; an order effect was seen.
  - Limitations: small, IBS patients, short exposures.
- Leonard MM, Sapone A, Catassi C, Fasano A. Celiac Disease and Nonceliac Gluten Sensitivity: A Review. JAMA. 2017;318(7):647-656 (volume/pages as commonly cited). PMID 28810029. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28810029/ — clinicians cannot distinguish celiac disease from NCGS by symptoms alone, so celiac screening should happen before a gluten-free diet is started.
- Our reading: symptoms in people who feel better off gluten are real; but in controlled trials the gluten-specific effect is often small or absent, and expectation (nocebo) plays a role (placebo group: 40% still not controlled). General statement; not claimed as a number from a source beyond the trial figures.

## 5. What gluten-free packaged foods contain

- Wu JH, Neal B, Trevena H, Crino M, Stuart-Smith W, Faulkner-Hogg K, Yu Louie JC, Dunford E. Are gluten-free foods healthier than non-gluten-free foods? An evaluation of supermarket products in Australia. Br J Nutr. 2015;114(3):448-454. PMID 26119206. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26119206/
  - Design: cross-sectional, nutrition-panel data on **3,213** packaged products in four large Sydney supermarkets (2013), ten categories; main outcome Health Star Rating (0.5–5).
  - Results: gluten-free dry pasta scored nearly **0.5 stars lower** than regular pasta (P < 0.001); breads and breakfast cereals no significant difference. Gluten-free pasta had **52% less protein**, breads **32% less** (both P < 0.001). A large share of discretionary foods carried a gluten-free label (e.g. 87% of processed meats). Authors: gluten-free products unlikely to bring health benefits unless clear evidence of gluten intolerance.
  - Limitations: Australian products, label data only, single time point; US shelves differ.
- Missbach B, Schwingshackl L, Billmann A, Mystek A, Hickelsberger M, Bauer G, König J. Gluten-free food database: the nutritional quality and cost of packaged gluten-free foods. PeerJ. 2015;3:e1337. PMID 26528408. DOI 10.7717/peerj.1337.
  - Design: 63 gluten-free products vs 126 gluten-containing counterparts, 12 Austrian supermarkets, seven categories.
  - Results: protein more than two-fold lower in **57%** of categories; 19% of gluten-free foods high in fiber (> 6 g/100 g); prices **+205% (cereals) to +267% (bread and bakery)**. Authors: no clear health benefit for healthy consumers; useful tool for people with celiac disease, some nutrients need attention.
  - Limitations: Austrian market, small product count, cross-sectional.
- Not claimed: that gluten-free products are always higher in sugar or fat (inconsistent across studies; not verified).

## 6. Testing before cutting gluten

- NIDDK, "Diagnosis of Celiac Disease": blood tests look for antibodies often higher in untreated celiac disease and may reveal related problems such as anemia; small-intestine biopsy during upper endoscopy; advises NOT starting a gluten-free diet before testing because it can affect results. https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/celiac-disease/diagnosis
- Husby S, Murray JA, Katzka DA. AGA Clinical Practice Update on Diagnosis and Monitoring of Celiac Disease: Changing Utility of Serology and Histologic Measures: Expert Review. Gastroenterology. 2019. PMID 30578783. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578783/ — expert review; the abstract text opened confirms serology is central and that the paper covers people already on a gluten-free diet. The statement that gluten-free or low-gluten diets before diagnosis lower the accuracy of serology and histology, and that people already gluten-free may be advised on a supervised gluten reintroduction, comes from a search-result summary of this update, not from text I read myself. The video therefore keeps this wording general and attributes it also to NIDDK.
- Leonard et al. JAMA 2017 (above): test before diet.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Celiac disease (about 1% of people; about 2 million Americans), wheat allergy and non-celiac gluten sensitivity are real reasons to avoid gluten; celiac disease needs lifelong avoidance under medical care.
- In two large US cohorts (about 110,000 for heart disease, about 200,000 for diabetes) people without celiac disease who ate less gluten had no lower heart disease risk after adjustment, and a higher type 2 diabetes risk. Both are associations.
- Packaged gluten-free substitutes often have less protein and cost far more (Australia, Austria); gluten-free labels are not a nutrition guarantee.
- Celiac testing is unreliable once gluten is removed, so test first.

**Uncertain**
- Whether any benefit exists for people who feel better off gluten: symptoms are real but trials suggest gluten itself is often not the trigger, small samples, IBS populations.
- Whether the diabetes and heart findings reflect gluten or the whole grains and fiber that come with it (authors point to the latter). Cohorts are mostly health professionals and may not reflect all older adults; no outcome data in people strictly gluten-free for decades.

**False or unsupported**
- That a gluten-free diet is healthier, helps heart health or helps weight for people without a medical reason to avoid gluten.
- That "gluten-free" on a package means low sugar, low fat or nutritious.
- That you should cut gluten before being tested for celiac disease.
