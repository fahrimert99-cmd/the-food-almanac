# Research notes: Does pickle juice really stop nighttime leg cramps?

Slug: `pickle-juice-leg-cramps`. Audience: US adults, mostly 60+.

Access note: PubMed and PMC pages were blocked for the fetch tool (cookie/verification wall). Abstract details below were read through Europe PMC records, Cochrane Library summaries returned by search, the AAFP full-text page (opened), and a university news release. Anything I could not see is marked **NOT VERIFIED** and is not used in the script.

## 1. Pickle juice and electrically induced cramps (original study)

**Citation.** Miller KC, Mack GW, Knight KL, Hopkins JT, Draper DO, Fields PJ, Hunter I. Reflex inhibition of electrically induced muscle cramps in hypohydrated humans. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2010;42(5):953–961. DOI 10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181c0647e. PMID 19997012. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19997012/ (abstract read via Europe PMC record).

**Design.** Crossover, human, healthy young men (indexed "young adult"). Men were made mildly dehydrated (about 3% loss of body weight; plasma osmolality about 295 mOsm/kg). On two days a week apart, a cramp was induced electrically in a foot muscle (nerve stimulation at the ankle). On a second induced cramp, they drank either plain (deionized) water or pickle juice, at 1 mL per kg of body weight (a news release puts this at about 2.5 fluid ounces).

**Figures.**
- Cramp duration before drinking: about 152 seconds in both trials.
- After water: about 134 seconds. After pickle juice: about 85 seconds.
- Difference: pickle juice about 49 seconds shorter than water. 85/134 gives about 37% shorter than water (consistent with the "37% faster than water" in the NDSU/Newswise release).
- A news release also says about 45% shorter than no fluid (about 150 seconds with no fluid). **Not used** (only a secondary source, exact seconds not seen).
- Plasma composition changed little 5 minutes after drinking either fluid; authors concluded relief was not explained by rapid restoration of fluids/electrolytes and proposed a neural reflex from the mouth/throat that inhibits the motor neurons of the cramping muscle.

**Limitations.**
- Sample size **NOT VERIFIED** (secondary sources disagree: 9, 10 or 12). The script does not state a number.
- Healthy young men only; the lead researcher said in the release that all his studies had been in healthy young men.
- Cramps were artificial (electrically induced in a foot muscle in awake volunteers), not spontaneous night calf cramps.
- Measured the duration of a single induced cramp; did not test prevention or frequency of cramps.
- Compared only with water (and, per the release, no fluid), so which component (salt, vinegar, taste) matters is unknown.
- Reflex mechanism is a proposed explanation, not confirmed.
- I found no trial of pickle juice for nocturnal leg cramps in older adults.
- Release: researcher advised not drinking large amounts and checking with a physician first, because high blood pressure is common.

News release: "Does pickle juice stop muscle cramps?" Newswise / NDSU. https://www.newswise.com/articles/does-pickle-juice-stop-muscle-cramps

## 2. Magnesium for cramps

**Citation.** Garrison SR, Korownyk CS, Kolber MR, Allan GM, Musini VM, Sekhon RK, Dugré N. Magnesium for skeletal muscle cramps. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2020;9:CD009402. DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD009402.pub3. PMID 32956536. https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD009402.pub3/information (PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8020016)

**Design.** Systematic review of randomized trials, searches to 9 September 2019. 11 trials (9 parallel, 2 crossover), 735 participants. Five trials (408 women) pregnancy-associated cramps; five trials (271 people) idiopathic cramps; one trial (29) liver cirrhosis.

**Figures.**
- Idiopathic cramps: change in cramps per week at 4 weeks, magnesium vs placebo: mean difference −0.18 (95% CI −0.84 to 0.49; 5 studies, 307 participants), i.e. not significant and the interval includes no benefit and slight harm.
- Major adverse events similar (2/72 vs 3/68). Minor adverse events trended higher with magnesium (RR 1.51, 95% CI 0.98 to 2.33; 4 studies, 254 participants), mostly gastrointestinal such as diarrhea.
- Authors: "unlikely that magnesium supplementation provides clinically meaningful cramp prophylaxis to older adults." Pregnancy evidence conflicting.

**Limitations.** Mostly small trials; no trials for exercise- or most disease-associated cramps. The script says "everyday kind of cramps with no known cause", not "most older adults".

## 3. Stretching

**Citation (trial).** Hallegraeff JM, van der Schans CP, de Ruiter R, de Greef MH. Stretching before sleep reduces the frequency and severity of nocturnal leg cramps in older adults: a randomised trial. *J Physiother.* 2012;58(1):17–22. DOI 10.1016/S1836-9553(12)70068-1. PMID 22341378. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01421628.

**Design.** Randomized trial, 80 adults over 55 with nocturnal leg cramps, not taking quinine. Intervention: calf and hamstring stretching nightly before sleep for 6 weeks vs no specific stretching. Stretching group had significantly greater reductions in cramp frequency and severity. (Effect sizes seen only in a search snippet: frequency mean difference 1.2 cramps per night, severity 1.3 cm on a 10 cm VAS. **Not used in the script**.) Disputed in correspondence (Garrison; Daniell and Pentrack); authors replied (Hallegraeff et al., *J Physiother* 2013;59(4)).

**Citation (review).** Hawke F, Sadler SG, Katzberg HD, Pourkazemi F, Chuter V, Burns J. Non-drug therapies for the secondary prevention of lower limb muscle cramps. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2021;5:CD008496. PMID 33998664. https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008496.pub3/information

- 3 randomized trials, 201 participants, all 50 or older, none with neurological disease; all tested stretching.
- Calf plus hamstring stretching daily for 6 weeks may reduce the severity of night cramps in people 55+, effect on frequency uncertain; low-certainty evidence from a single trial of 80 people.
- Calf stretching alone vs sham for 12 weeks in people 60+: may make little or no difference to frequency.
- Limits: small studies, unvalidated outcomes, inconsistent definitions.

## 4. Prevalence, causes, medicines, quinine

**Citation.** Allen RE, Kirby KA. Nocturnal leg cramps. *Am Fam Physician.* 2012;86(4):350–355. PMID 22963024. https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2012/0815/p350.html (page opened).

- Nocturnal leg cramps reported by 50 to 60 percent of adults; prevalence rises with age.
- Cramps can accompany peripheral vascular disease, venous insufficiency, peripheral neuropathy; evaluate if bothersome.
- Medicines most strongly linked (Table 1, incidence in trials): IV iron sucrose 23%; raloxifene 5.9–12.1%; conjugated estrogens 3.5–14%; naproxen 3%; teriparatide 2.6%. Overall incidence described as very low.
- Diuretics are "commonly believed" to cause leg cramps through electrolyte changes "but they have not been implicated in evidence-based reviews."
- Statins: AFP links them to myalgia/myositis, not cramps. Not used in the script.
- Passive stretching and massage are "harmless, patient-controlled maneuvers" that may be tried despite limited proof of effectiveness.
- Magnesium: mixed benefit in non-pregnant adults, low-quality evidence.
- Quinine: the FDA warned in 2010 about serious adverse effects outweighing modest benefit; quinine is no longer recommended for cramps.

**FDA.** Drug Safety Communication, July 2010 (Qualaquin, quinine sulfate): not approved for, and should not be used for, nighttime leg cramps; risk of serious and life-threatening hematologic reactions including serious bleeding from thrombocytopenia (low platelets) and hemolytic uremic syndrome; led to a REMS. Seen via search results quoting the FDA notice (fda.gov page itself returned 404 on one URL; drugs.com mirror). https://www.fda.gov/Drugs/DrugSafety/ucm218202.htm

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Pickle juice shortened electrically induced foot cramps by about 49 seconds (about 37%) versus water in one lab crossover study of healthy, mildly dehydrated young men, apparently without time to change blood chemistry.
- Magnesium did not beat placebo for idiopathic cramps in a Cochrane review (11 trials, 735 people) and is unlikely to help older adults.
- Quinine should not be used for leg cramps (FDA, 2010).
- Some medicines are linked to leg cramps (raloxifene, teriparatide, conjugated estrogens, naproxen, IV iron sucrose), though most people taking them do not get cramps.

**Uncertain**
- Whether pickle juice helps spontaneous night cramps in older adults (untested as far as I found), prevents them, or which component matters; the nerve-reflex explanation.
- Stretching before bed: one 80-person trial plus a low-certainty Cochrane judgment (may reduce severity; frequency unclear); disputed.
- Whether diuretics cause cramps (commonly believed, not confirmed in reviews).

**False or misleading**
- "Pickle juice works by replacing lost salt and electrolytes" (blood chemistry barely changed at 5 minutes).
- "Magnesium is a proven cure for night cramps."
- "Quinine is a safe cramp remedy."
