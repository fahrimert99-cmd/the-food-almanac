# Research notes: Are frozen vegetables less nutritious than fresh ones?

Method note: every entry below was read as an abstract/record (Europe PMC records, PubMed-indexed records, NHS page). Full texts were NOT available (paywalls, bot checks), so **most abstracts give no numbers**. Only figures that appear in an abstract I read are used in the script. Where a detail is missing it is marked "not in abstract".

## 1. Bouzari, Holstege, Barrett 2015 (original study, lab measurements)
- Citation: Bouzari A, Holstege D, Barrett DM. Vitamin retention in eight fruits and vegetables: a comparison of refrigerated and frozen storage. J Agric Food Chem. 2015;63(3):957-962. PMID 25526594.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25526594/ (read via Europe PMC record: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25526594)
- Design: lab analysis of ascorbic acid (vitamin C), riboflavin, alpha-tocopherol (vitamin E) and beta-carotene in corn, carrots, broccoli, spinach, peas, green beans, strawberries, blueberries; refrigerated ("fresh-stored") vs frozen; samples analysed at three storage times per treatment (days/temperatures not in abstract). No people; nutrient content only.
- Findings (abstract, no numerical values given):
  - Vitamin C: no significant difference in 5 of 8 commodities; higher in frozen samples for the other 3.
  - Riboflavin: higher in frozen broccoli, lower in frozen peas; no significant difference in the rest.
  - Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol): higher in frozen samples for 3 commodities; no difference in the rest.
  - Beta-carotene: "found to decrease drastically in some commodities" in frozen samples (abstract wording); I did not get a clean list of which ones (record paraphrase was inconsistent about spinach), so the script says only "some".
  - Conclusion: frozen vitamin content comparable to, and sometimes higher than, refrigerated.
- Limitations: no numbers in abstract; sample number/lot count not in abstract; funding not in the record. Secondary sources (trade press, Frozen Food Foundation page) say the work was partly funded by the Frozen Food Foundation (industry body); not verified in a primary record.

## 2. Li, Pegg, Eitenmiller, Chun, Kerrihard 2017 (original study)
- Citation: Li L, Pegg RB, Eitenmiller RR, Chun JY, Kerrihard AL. Selected nutrient analyses of fresh, fresh-stored, and frozen fruits and vegetables. J Food Compos Anal. 2017;59:8-17. DOI 10.1016/j.jfca.2017.02.002.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2017.02.002 (record: https://digitalcommons.montclair.edu/nutr-foodstudies-facpubs/93)
- Design: two-year study; broccoli, cauliflower, corn, green beans, green peas, spinach, blueberries, strawberries; three categories: fresh, frozen, "fresh-stored" (5 days refrigerated, to model home storage); measured vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid), trans-beta-carotene (provitamin A) and total folate; triplicate analysis, standardized methods, QC plan.
- Findings: most comparisons showed no significant difference; where a difference existed, frozen beat fresh-stored more often than the reverse. Authors: the results do not support the belief that fresh is significantly more nutritious than frozen once refrigerated storage is considered.
- Limitations: abstract names no specific commodities or numbers; sample/lot count not in record. Secondary sources say partly funded by the Frozen Food Foundation (not verified in a primary record).

## 3. Favell 1998 (original study)
- Citation: Favell DJ. A comparison of the vitamin C content of fresh and frozen vegetables. Food Chem. 1998;62(1):59-64.
- URL: Europe PMC record (search) https://europepmc.org/search?query=Favell%20vitamin%20C%20fresh%20frozen%20vegetables ; Elsevier: Food Chemistry 62(1).
- Design: vitamin C as marker; fresh vegetables at different stages of distribution and storage compared with commercially quick-frozen versions kept deep-frozen up to 12 months.
- Findings (abstract): frozen peas and broccoli close to typical market product and better than peas stored at home for several days; fresh peas and broccoli stayed in good condition up to 14 days chilled; freezing caused no loss in green beans or carrots (similar to fresh at harvest); frozen spinach compared reasonably with freshly harvested spinach and was clearly better than all market spinach tested. Overall: frozen comparable to, sometimes better than, fresh that had been through distribution or home storage.
- Limitations: no numerical values, temperatures or sample sizes in abstract; 1998 product/supply chain.

## 4. Galgano, Favati, Caruso, Pietrafesa, Natella 2007 (original study, broccoli)
- Citation: Galgano F, Favati F, Caruso M, Pietrafesa A, Natella S. The influence of processing and preservation on the retention of health-promoting compounds in broccoli. J Food Sci. 2007;72(2):S130-S135. DOI 10.1111/j.1750-3841.2006.00258.x. PMID 17995854.
- Design: broccoli (cv. Marathon) stored at 6 °C, 95% relative humidity for 35 days; frozen at -18 °C for 60 days; cooked by boiling, steaming, pressure-cooking, microwaving, pressure-microwave; vitamin C and sulforaphane measured. Lab study, no people.
- Numbers: chilled 35 days: vitamin C about -39%, sulforaphane about -29%. Freezing 60 days: similar losses, mostly during the blanching step. Boiling about -34% vitamin C, steaming about -22% (sulforaphane no longer detectable after either). Pressure-cooking, microwaving and the combination kept more than 90% of vitamin C.
- Limitations: one cultivar; 35 days is longer than typical home storage; the abstract does not give the frozen figure as a separate number ("similar"); cooking details (time, water) not in abstract.

## 5. Razzak et al. 2023 (original study, cooking)
- Citation: Razzak et al. (first-author initials and exact title not in the record I read): cooking effects on ascorbic acid and beta-carotene in six vegetables, Bangladesh. Heliyon. 2023. DOI 10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e21709. PMID 38027907. (Full author list/title not in the record I read.)
- Findings: boiling caused the largest ascorbic acid loss, from 9.83% to 70.88% across the six vegetables, spinach losing the most; microwaving preserved over 90% of ascorbic acid; steaming kept more beta-carotene than boiling or microwaving, except in carrots.
- Limitations: markets/varieties in one country; abstract only.

## 6. Yuan et al. 2009 (original study, broccoli cooking)
- Citation: Yuan et al. (initials not confirmed). Effects of different cooking methods on health-promoting compounds of broccoli. J Zhejiang Univ Sci B. 2009. DOI 10.1631/jzus.b0920051. PMID 19650196.
- Finding (abstract): every cooking method except steaming caused significant losses of chlorophyll and vitamin C; steaming retained nutrients best overall. No percentages in abstract. Note this partly conflicts with Galgano (steaming -22%): results depend on time, water and method details.

## 7. Miglio, Chiavaro, Visconti, Fogliano, Pellegrini 2008 (original study, cooking)
- Citation: Miglio C, et al. Effects of different cooking methods on nutritional and physicochemical characteristics of selected vegetables. J Agric Food Chem. 2008;56(1):139-147. PMID 18069785. DOI 10.1021/jf072304b.
- Design: carrots, zucchini, broccoli; boiling, steaming, frying; polyphenols, carotenoids, glucosinolates, ascorbic acid, antioxidant capacity (TEAC, TRAP, FRAP), texture, colour.
- Findings: water-based cooking (boiling, steaming) preserved antioxidants best, especially carotenoids, and ascorbic acid in carrots and zucchini; frying lost more; antioxidant capacity rose in all cooked vegetables, attributed to softening and better extraction; authors: processed vegetables do not necessarily have lower nutritional quality; best method depends on vegetable.
- Limitations: antioxidant capacity assays are lab measures, not health outcomes; abstract only.

## 8. Rickman, Barrett, Bruhn 2007 (review, parts 1 and 2)
- Part 1: Rickman JC, Barrett DM, Bruhn CM. Nutritional comparison of fresh, frozen and canned fruits and vegetables. Part 1. Vitamins C and B and phenolic compounds. J Sci Food Agric. 2007;87(6):930-944. DOI 10.1002/jsfa.2825.
- Part 2: Rickman JC, Bruhn CM, Barrett DM. ... Part 2. Vitamin A and carotenoids, vitamin E, minerals and fiber. J Sci Food Agric. 2007;87(7):1185-1196. DOI 10.1002/jsfa.2824.
- Key points: nutrient losses in fresh produce during storage and cooking may be larger than commonly assumed; freezing and canning can preserve nutrients depending on commodity; heat early in processing destroys water-soluble, oxygen-sensitive vitamins (C, B); frozen products lose fewer nutrients during brief blanching than canned but lose more during frozen storage (oxidation); carotenoids, vitamin E, minerals, fibre generally similar across forms, canned often higher carotenoids (possibly wet-weight reporting, extractability or cultivar); processing can add sodium; authors urge eating a variety. Recommending only fresh overlooks canned and frozen options.
- Limitations: narrative review, inconsistent methods across studies; both authors are linked with frozen/canned food research groups (funding not in the record).

## 9. NHS 5 A DAY (agency page)
- NHS. 5 A DAY: what counts. https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/
- 80 g of fresh, canned or frozen fruit and vegetables counts as 1 portion of 5 A DAY. Page makes no claim about nutrient content.

## 10. Background-only (not used for numbers)
- Mahmud MM, et al. Mechanistic insights into colour and texture stability of frozen tropical vegetables. Food Sci Nutr. 2026;14(6):e71895. DOI 10.1002/fsn3.71895 (review abstract: colour and texture loss in long frozen storage). Used only for "texture can change".
- Spinach/broccoli storage percentages seen only in search-engine snippets (e.g. mature spinach about 80% vitamin C lost in 3 days) were NOT verified and are NOT used.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- Vegetables kept fresh-stored for days lose vitamin C; commercially frozen vegetables often match or beat fresh-stored in vitamin C and several other vitamins (Bouzari 2015, Li 2017, Favell 1998).
- Freezing itself costs some vitamin C, mostly in the blanching step (Galgano 2007, Rickman 2007).
- Cooking method matters: microwaving/pressure cooking kept over 90% vitamin C in broccoli; long boiling lost more (Galgano 2007, Razzak 2023).
- Frozen counts as vegetables for dietary guidance (NHS).

**Uncertain**
- Exact differences by vegetable: abstracts give few numbers; beta-carotene looks lower in some frozen vegetables, but not consistent across sources.
- Which cooking method is best varies between studies (steaming: -22% in Galgano, best in Yuan).
- Industry funding of some studies (reported by secondary sources, unverified).
- Whether any of this changes health outcomes: all evidence is nutrient content in food, not outcomes in people.

**False / not supported**
- "Frozen vegetables are less nutritious than fresh" as a blanket statement. Fresh just picked is best, but shop-bought, home-stored fresh is often equal or lower than frozen.
- "Frozen vegetables lose nothing": they do lose some vitamin C early.
