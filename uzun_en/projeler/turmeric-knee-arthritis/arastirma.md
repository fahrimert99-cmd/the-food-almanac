# Research notes: Does turmeric help knee arthritis pain?

Topic: curcumin / turmeric extract in knee osteoarthritis (OA). Audience: adults 55+, many with joint pain, many on daily medicines.
Note: "knee arthritis" in the title means knee osteoarthritis, the wear-and-tear type. All trials below are in knee OA. Nothing here applies to rheumatoid arthritis.

General background (no numbers used): knee OA is the type of arthritis where the cartilage cushioning the joint thins and the joint can become painful and stiff. Turmeric is the golden-yellow spice from the root of *Curcuma longa*; curcumin is its main active compound (curcuminoids). Spice powder contains only a few percent curcumin by weight (search-result excerpts quoted roughly 0.06 to 2 percent in one Brazilian sample study and 2 to 6 percent as a market range; the study page itself returned 403, so the narration says only "a small share").

---

## 1. Curcuma domestica extract vs ibuprofen (Thailand)

- **Citation:** Kuptniratsaikul V, Dajpratham P, Taechaarpornkul W, et al. Efficacy and safety of *Curcuma domestica* extracts compared with ibuprofen in patients with knee osteoarthritis: a multicenter study. *Clin Interv Aging.* 2014;9:451-458. doi:10.2147/CIA.S58535. PMID 24672232. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24672232 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964021/ (page opened)
- **Design:** multicenter, double-blind, randomized **noninferiority** trial, eight tertiary hospitals in Thailand (NCT00792818). Per-protocol analysis.
- **Population:** 367 randomized (185 extract, 182 ibuprofen). Primary knee OA, knee pain 5 or more out of 10, age 50 or older; mean age about 60; about 90 percent women.
- **Intervention:** *C. domestica* extract 1,500 mg per day vs ibuprofen 1,200 mg per day, capsules three times daily, **4 weeks**. Tramadol allowed as rescue.
- **Results at week 4 (WOMAC, ibuprofen minus extract):** total 3.23 vs 3.36, difference −0.07 (95% CI −0.43 to 0.29), noninferiority P = 0.010; pain difference −0.09 (−0.47 to 0.29), P = 0.018; function P = 0.010; stiffness difference −0.15 (−0.59 to 0.29), P = 0.060 (did **not** reach noninferiority). 6-minute walk 348 vs 345 m, no difference.
- **Safety:** any adverse event 35.7 percent ibuprofen vs 29.7 percent extract (P = 0.222, not significant). Abdominal pain or distension 18.1 vs 10.8 percent (P = 0.046, the only significant difference). Dropouts due to adverse events: 7 ibuprofen vs 1 extract. Two ibuprofen patients had melena (black stool); none on extract.
- **Limitations:** 4 weeks only; per-protocol; uneven dropout; ibuprofen dose lower than some trials use; mostly Thai women; stiffness missed the noninferiority margin; "noninferior" is weaker than "as effective". Extract was not a standard curcumin product (a *C. domestica* extract), so the dose of curcumin itself is not comparable to supplements on shelves.

## 2. Curcuma longa extract vs placebo, with MRI (Tasmania)

- **Citation:** Wang Z, Jones G, Winzenberg T, Cai G, Laslett LL, Aitken D, Hopper I, Singh A, Jones R, Fripp J, Ding C, Antony B. Effectiveness of *Curcuma longa* extract for the treatment of symptoms and effusion-synovitis of knee osteoarthritis: a randomized trial. *Ann Intern Med.* 2020;173(11):861-869. doi:10.7326/M20-0990. (ACTRN12618000080224.) Abstract seen through Europe PMC; the journal page returned 403.
- **Design:** randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single centre (southern Tasmania, Australia), **12 weeks**, 2 capsules a day.
- **Population:** 70 adults with symptomatic knee OA and ultrasound-defined effusion-synovitis (swelling inside the joint). Extract n = 36, placebo n = 34. (Mean age not available in the abstract text I saw.)
- **Results:** pain on a visual analogue scale improved more with extract than placebo by −9.1 mm (95% CI −17.8 to −0.4; P = 0.039). WOMAC knee pain −47.2 mm (−81.2 to −13.2; P = 0.006). MRI joint swelling (effusion-synovitis volume) **no significant change** (3.2 mL, CI −0.3 to 6.8). Cartilage composition (T2 relaxation) **no significant change** (−0.4 ms, CI −1.1 to 0.3).
- **Safety:** adverse events 14 of 36 (39 percent) extract vs 18 of 34 (53 percent) placebo, P = 0.16. Two extract and five placebo events possibly treatment-related.
- **Limitations (authors):** modest sample, short duration. Funded by University of Tasmania and Natural Remedies Private Limited (the extract maker). Effect on pain was small: 9.1 mm on a 100 mm scale.

## 3. Bio-enhanced curcumin extract vs placebo

- **Citation:** Lopresti AL, Smith SJ, Jackson-Michel S, Fairchild T. An investigation into the effects of a curcumin extract (Curcugen) on osteoarthritis pain of the knee: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study. *Nutrients.* 2022;14(1):41 (published December 2021). doi:10.3390/nu14010041. PMID 35010916. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8746505/ (details seen via Europe PMC record; PMC page showed a bot check).
- **Design:** randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, **8 weeks**, 500 mg standardized curcumin extract twice daily vs placebo.
- **Population:** 101 adults with knee OA.
- **Results:** KOOS knee pain improved more than placebo (P = 0.009); numeric knee pain rating (P = 0.001). Better timed-up-and-go, 6-minute walk and JOA score (P ≤ 0.05). **No** significant benefit on 30-second chair stand or 40-m fast-paced walk. Pain-relief medication reduced in 37 percent of curcumin group vs 13 percent of placebo group.
- **Limitations (authors):** short; call for longer studies, other doses/extracts, more objective measures. Industry involvement (product named in title; authors from the supplement company side per author list; funding not verified by me).

## 4. Meta-analysis of curcuminoids in knee OA

- **Citation:** Feng J, Li Z, Tian L, Mu P, Hu Y, Xiong F, Ma X. Efficacy and safety of curcuminoids alone in alleviating pain and dysfunction for knee osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *BMC Complement Med Ther.* 2022;22:276. doi:10.1186/s12906-022-03740-9. PMID 36261810. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9580113/ (page opened, first 100,000 characters read)
- **Scope:** 15 RCTs, 1,670 patients; 10 placebo-controlled, 5 active-controlled (ibuprofen, diclofenac, paracetamol); duration 4 weeks to 6 months.
- **Vs placebo (weighted mean difference):** VAS pain −1.77 (95% CI −2.44 to −1.09; 11 studies, 870 patients; I² 86.8 percent). WOMAC pain −1.42 (−2.41 to −0.43) in the abstract (−1.94 in body text; internal inconsistency; the narration avoids WOMAC pain). Only VAS pain and WOMAC total exceeded the authors' minimal clinically important difference thresholds (VAS 1.18; WOMAC total 8.97, using the body-text figure of −10.47; the abstract gives −7.06, which would fall short, so the narration does not claim WOMAC total passes).
- **Vs NSAIDs:** no significant difference in pain or function; authors say "not inferior". VAS pain −0.3 (−0.63 to 0.04).
- **Adverse events:** vs placebo RR 1.03 (0.69 to 1.53), no difference; overall incidence 25.06 percent with curcuminoids vs 35.57 percent with controls; mostly gastrointestinal. vs NSAIDs RR 0.65 (0.41 to 1.03) in body text, not significant.
- **Quality:** risk of bias low in 8, moderate in 5, high in 2 studies. Heterogeneity substantial (I² 77 to 87 percent for pain and function). GRADE certainty for pain and function: moderate to very low. Possible publication bias (funnel plot asymmetry). Mixed formulations and doses.
- **Authors' conclusion:** cautious recommendation for broader clinical use.

## 5. Absorption

- **Anand P, Kunnumakkara AB, Newman RA, Aggarwal BB.** Bioavailability of curcumin: problems and promises. *Mol Pharm.* 2007;4(6):807-818. Abstract confirmed through search: poor absorption, rapid metabolism and rapid elimination give low blood and tissue levels, even though doses up to 12 g a day were safe in early human trials.
- **NCCIH, Turmeric (opened):** oral products often contain piperine from black pepper; "combining curcumin with piperine is one way to improve the bioavailability of curcumin." https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric
- **Shoba G, et al.** Influence of piperine on the pharmacokinetics of curcumin in animals and human volunteers. *Planta Med.* 1998;64(4):353-356. PMID 9619120. Abstract: 2 g curcumin alone gave very low blood levels in volunteers; with 20 mg piperine the abstract reports bioavailability increased by 2000 percent. Tiny study; a later study (Fança-Berthon 2021, as reported by the UK Committee on Toxicity) did not replicate. Narration uses only the qualitative message: absorption is poor; piperine and special formulations raise levels; so trial products differ from kitchen turmeric. Not used: the 2000 percent figure.

## 6. Safety

- **NCCIH (opened):** "initial evidence is positive; higher-quality evidence is needed to reach definitive conclusions" for OA. Oral turmeric/curcumin "likely safe in the recommended amounts for up to 2 or 3 months." Side effects: nausea, vomiting, acid reflux, stomach upset, diarrhea, constipation. Liver damage has been reported in some people who used bioavailable formulations; stop and contact a provider with fatigue, nausea, poor appetite, dark urine or jaundice. Ask a provider before herbal products if you take medicines.
- **Liver:** Halegoua-DeMarzio D, et al. Liver injury associated with turmeric: a growing problem? Ten cases from the Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network. *Am J Med.* 2023;136(2):200-206. PMID 36252717. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36252717/ (seen via search summary). Case series of adjudicated cases 2004-2022: 10 cases of turmeric-associated liver injury, 5 hospitalized, 1 died. 7 of the 10 carried the gene variant HLA-B*35:01. Of the 7 products tested, 3 contained piperine. Rare (10 cases over about 18 years) but serious. This is a case series: it cannot give a rate.
- **Blood thinners:** Medsafe (New Zealand), 30 April 2018: a patient on warfarin with a previously stable INR had an INR above 10 within a few weeks of starting a turmeric-containing product; reporter judged an interaction; curcumin's antiplatelet effect may also add to bleeding risk with other medicines affecting bleeding; warning does not apply to turmeric in food; do not stop medicines without advice. https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/safety/ews/2018/Turmeric.asp (page opened). Evidence = case reports, not controlled trials.
- **Gallbladder:** The EU herbal monograph for *Curcuma longa* rhizome (EMA/HMPC, 2018 revision) is reported (search-result excerpt; the PDF text could not be extracted) to say it is not recommended in bile duct obstruction, cholangitis, liver disease, gallstones or other biliary disease, because of possible stimulation of bile secretion. https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/herbal-monograph/final-european-union-herbal-monograph-curcuma-longa-l-rhizoma-revision-1_en.pdf Narration therefore says "European herbal guidance advises against turmeric supplements" in that context, without numbers. NCCIH does not mention gallstones.
- Not used: "40 mg curcumin gave 50 percent gallbladder contraction" (only via advocacy site, original study not seen).

## Honest verdict

**Reasonably supported**
- In several short randomized trials (4 to 12 weeks) in adults with knee OA, curcumin or turmeric extracts reduced pain a little more than placebo, and in one 4-week trial were noninferior to ibuprofen for pain and function.
- A 2022 meta-analysis of 15 trials found a pooled pain benefit vs placebo and no significant difference vs NSAIDs, with few extra side effects in trials.

**Uncertain**
- Size of the benefit: modest (about 9 mm on a 100 mm scale in the Tasmanian trial), with large heterogeneity, risk of bias, possible publication bias, much industry involvement; GRADE moderate to very low.
- Which product, dose and formulation work; trials used different extracts, not kitchen turmeric.
- Long-term benefit (trials 4 weeks to 6 months), and whether it protects the joint (MRI swelling and cartilage showed no change in the one trial that measured them).
- Whether older adults specifically respond the same way (mean age about 60 in the Thai trial; the rest not confirmed).

**False or overstated**
- "Turmeric repairs cartilage / treats the cause": no evidence; the one MRI trial showed no change.
- "Turmeric is a proven replacement for pain medicine": no; one noninferiority trial over 4 weeks does not show that.
- "It's all natural so it can't hurt": liver injury cases, bleeding-risk reports and bile-duct cautions exist, mostly with concentrated supplements, not with cooking.
- Sprinkling turmeric on food in the amounts used in cooking is not what the trials tested.
