# Research notes: Does cooling rice or potatoes lower the blood sugar spike?

Slug: `cooling-rice-resistant-starch`. Notes written 2026-11-04. Pages marked "opened" were read in full or in the abstract/results; anything not opened is flagged.

## Mechanism (background)
- When starch is cooked in water it gelatinizes (swells, becomes easy to digest). When cooked starch cools, some of the starch molecules (mainly amylose) re-associate into tight crystals. This is called retrogradation. The result is "type 3 resistant starch" (RS3): starch that is not digested in the small intestine and behaves more like fiber.
- Source: Sonia 2015 (below) states that cooling cooked starch causes retrogradation and raises resistant starch; Pugh 2023 notes that type 3 comes from cooking and then cooling starchy foods (cooked and cooled potatoes, bread, pasta, rice).
- Reheating does not necessarily undo it, but one review cited in a secondary summary suggests reheating may convert some RS back to digestible starch. Not verified from a primary source.

## Claim 1: Cooled-then-reheated white rice has more resistant starch and a lower glucose response (healthy adults, small)
**Sonia S, Witjaksono F, Ridwan R. Effect of cooling of cooked white rice on resistant starch content and glycemic response. Asia Pac J Clin Nutr. 2015;24(4):620-625. DOI 10.6133/apjcn.2015.24.4.13.** PMID 26693746. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26693746/ (PubMed page would not load; details opened via Europe PMC record and a search summary of the abstract.)
- Lab part: resistant starch (g per 100 g rice) was 0.64 in freshly cooked white rice, 1.30 in rice cooled 10 hours at room temperature, 1.65 in rice cooled 24 hours at 4 °C and then reheated.
- Clinical part: randomized, single-blind crossover trial, n = 15 healthy adults. Glycemic response (area under the curve) was 125 ± 50.1 mmol·min/L after cooled-and-reheated rice versus 152 ± 48.3 mmol·min/L after fresh rice (p = 0.047). That is 27 units, or about 18 percent, lower (my calculation: 27/152 = 17.8%).
- Limitations: tiny n, healthy people, one rice, borderline p value (0.047), response given as AUC (no mg/dL peak reported in the abstract), not people with diabetes. Age of participants not seen.

## Claim 2: A second small rice crossover, mg/dL figures
**Dhar A, Kumar D, Sharma A, Dewan D. Effect of hot and cooled carbohydrate diet on glycemic response in healthy individuals: a cross over study. Int J Res Med Sci. 2021.** https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/9324 (opened).
- Randomized single-blind crossover, n = 20 healthy adults, 7-day washout. Freshly prepared hot white rice versus white rice cooked then cooled at 4 °C for 12 hours. Glucose measured for 2 hours with a glucometer.
- Mean glucose 121.9 ± 17.4 mg/dL (cooled rice) versus 128.0 ± 22.1 mg/dL (hot rice): about 6 mg/dL lower. Largest difference at 30 minutes (p < 0.001; the size at 30 minutes not given on the page).
- Limitations: small, healthy, lower-tier journal, age/sex not reported on the page, AUC not reported. It is not clear whether the cooled rice was reheated.

## Claim 3: Parboiling matters more than cooling (rice)
**Lu LW, Venn B, Lu J, Monro J, Rush E. Effect of cold storage and reheating of parboiled rice on postprandial glycaemic response, satiety, palatability and chewed particle size distribution. Nutrients. 2017;9(5):475.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5452205 (opened).
- Randomized repeated-measures crossover, n = 28 healthy adults (18 women, 10 men), Dunedin, New Zealand; about half normal weight, half overweight/obese. 140 g rice served at about 65 °C, visits one week apart.
- Incremental AUC (mmol/L·min): freshly cooked medium-grain white rice (control) 144.7; fresh parboiled rice 94.9; parboiled rice cooled 24 h at 4 °C and reheated 83.5.
- Control vs reheated parboiled: 1.7-fold higher (95% CI 1.2–2.6, p < 0.001). Control vs fresh parboiled: 1.5-fold higher (p = 0.001). Reheated parboiled vs fresh parboiled: no significant difference (p = 0.445).
- Interpretation by me and noted in the paper's analysis: the benefit came mainly from parboiling, not from cold storage.
- Limitations: healthy adults, single test of each condition, specific brands and methods. Authors say more trials are needed.

## Claim 4: Pasta
**Hodges C, Archer F, Chowdhury M, Evans BL, Ghelani DJ, Mortoglou M, Guppy FM. Method of food preparation influences blood glucose response to a high-carbohydrate meal: a randomised cross-over trial. Foods. 2019;9(1):23.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7022949/ (opened). PMID 31881647.
- Randomized, unblinded crossover, n = 45 volunteers aged 20–24 (University of Brighton, UK). White fusilli (100 g dry) with tomato sauce, three ways: hot; cooked then chilled 24 h at 4 °C and eaten cold; chilled then microwaved 3 min.
- Glucose AUC over 2 hours: reheated 703 ± 56 vs hot 735 ± 77 mmol·L⁻¹·min⁻¹ (p = 0.003). That is about 4 percent lower (32/735 = 4.4%, my calculation). Cold pasta did not differ significantly from hot.
- 2-hour glucose: reheated 5.78 vs hot 6.03 mmol/L (p = 0.013), roughly 104 vs 109 mg/dL (my conversion, × 18.02). No significant difference at any single time point. Reheated returned to baseline by 90 minutes; hot had not returned by 120 minutes.
- Limitations: young adults, unblinded, resistant starch not measured, one pasta and sauce, one cooling cycle.

## Claim 5: Potatoes
**Patterson MA, Nolte Fong J, Maiya M, Kung S, Sarkissian A, Nashef N, Wang W. Chilled potatoes decrease postprandial glucose, insulin, and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide compared to boiled potatoes in females with elevated fasting glucose and insulin. Nutrients. 2019;11(9):2066. DOI 10.3390/nu11092066.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6769955/ (opened).
- Randomized crossover, n = 30 premenopausal women with overweight or obesity (BMI 28–40), age 18–45 (mean 29.6), mean fasting glucose 110.5 mg/dL. 250 g russet potatoes: boiled and eaten hot, versus baked, stored 4 °C for five days, skin removed and eaten cold.
- Resistant starch was not measured; authors cite about 2.3 g per 100 g for hot potatoes and about 5.6 g per 100 g for cooked-then-chilled.
- Results (chilled vs boiled): glucose 2-hour AUC no difference; glucose lower at 15 min (−4.8%) and 30 min (−9.2%). Insulin AUC 17.7% lower; GIP AUC 35.1% lower. GLP-1 and PYY no difference. No difference in fullness overall.
- Limitations: women only, mean age under 30, not blinded, RS not measured, potatoes were eaten cold (not reheated).

## Claim 6: Systematic review of resistant starch in type 2 diabetes
**Pugh JE, Cai M, Altieri N, Frost G. A comparison of the effects of resistant starch types on glycemic response in individuals with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Front Nutr. 2023;10:1118229. DOI 10.3389/fnut.2023.1118229.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10085630/ (abstract and results opened; last ~16,000 characters not read).
- 36 randomized trials (n = 982), 31 in the meta-analysis. Types 1 and 2 resistant starch lowered post-meal glucose. Type 3, 4 and 5 need more research; the full text found no significant effect of type 3 on acute post-meal glucose.
- Limitations noted by the authors: heterogeneity, some high risk of bias, publication bias in some analyses.
- Note: this review is about resistant starch interventions in people with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes, not about home-cooled-and-cooled foods specifically.

## Claim 7: Food safety (Bacillus cereus)
**CDC. Bacillus cereus food poisoning associated with fried rice at two child day care centers, Virginia, 1993. MMWR Epidemiologic Notes and Reports.** https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00025744.htm (opened; page does not show volume/date).
- 14 of 67 people (21%) who ate a catered lunch became ill; 12 of 14 cases were children aged 2.5 to 5. Chicken fried rice was the implicated food. B. cereus grew to more than 10⁶ organisms per gram. The rice was cooked the night before, cooled at room temperature, then refrigerated, pan-fried, held without refrigeration and served.
- B. cereus is a spore-forming bacterium. Spores can survive cooking; if cooked rice is held at room temperature, bacteria multiply and make toxin. The emetic (vomiting) type has onset 1–6 hours and is caused by a heat-stable toxin that survives brief stir-frying. Fried rice is a leading cause of the emetic type. Illness is usually mild and self-limiting.
- CDC advice after the outbreak: stop cooling rice and other foods at room temperature; keep food below 41 °F (5 °C) or above 140 °F (60 °C); use a thermometer.

**Secondary source (opinion, no citations):** Marler B. Publisher's Platform: Bacillus cereus and leftover rice, perhaps best to avoid. Food Safety News, 16 Feb 2025. https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2025/02/publishers-platform-bacillus-cereus-and-leftover-rice-perhaps-best-to-avoid (opened).
- Cool rice quickly (thin layer or small portions); refrigerate within two hours in airtight containers below 40 °F (4 °C); eat within three to four days; reheat to at least 165 °F; reheat only what you will eat.
- Caveat: this is a contributed opinion piece. USDA FSIS "Leftovers and Food Safety" (2 hours, or 1 hour above 90 °F; 165 °F reheating) and FDA Bad Bug Book pages returned 403/404 and could NOT be opened; I did not cite them. The script words the timing as general food-safety guidance and leans on the CDC report. Do not claim a specific agency rule beyond what CDC's report states.
- The claim that reheating to 165 °F does not remove the heat-stable toxin is supported by CDC's statement that the emetic toxin survives stir-frying (heat-stable).

## General-knowledge statements used in the script (not from a cited trial)
- Parboiled rice is partly cooked in the husk before milling (definition only).
- A smaller portion means less starch to digest (logical statement, no figure given).
- Starch is chains of glucose that digestive enzymes break down (basic physiology).

## Not found / gaps
- No randomized crossover trial of cooled rice, potato or pasta in people with type 2 diabetes was found in the searches. All trials above are in healthy or at-risk young and middle-aged adults. No trial in adults 60+ was found. The script must say so.
- A 2022 chilled pasta study (8 healthy young men, no significant difference) and a 2026 type 1 diabetes pasta trial were seen only in search summaries and are not cited.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- Cooling cooked starchy food raises its resistant starch (rice measured: 0.64 to 1.65 g per 100 g).
- In small crossover trials in healthy adults, cooled and reheated rice, and reheated pasta, produced modestly lower glucose responses (about 18% lower AUC in rice, n = 15; about 4% in pasta, n = 45; about 6 mg/dL lower mean glucose in rice, n = 20).
- Cooked rice left at room temperature can grow B. cereus and cause vomiting illness; spores survive cooking.

**Uncertain**
- Whether the effect holds in older adults, people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, or with medicines such as metformin: untested in these trials.
- Whether the size matters for health. A few mg/dL or a few percent in AUC is small compared with portion size or food order.
- Potatoes: insulin and GIP were lower but glucose AUC was not.
- The most-cited reviews do not show a significant acute type 3 effect on glucose.
- Whether reheating erases part of the benefit.

**False or overstated**
- "Cold rice is a free fix that cancels the spike" or "makes rice safe for diabetes". The effect is modest, and rice is still mostly digestible starch.
- "Reheated rice is always dangerous." The risk comes from slow cooling and leaving rice at room temperature, not from reheating itself.
- Parboiling rather than cooling may explain more of the effect in the rice trial that compared both.
