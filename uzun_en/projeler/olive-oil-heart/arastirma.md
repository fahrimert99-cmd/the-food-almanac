# Research notes: extra virgin olive oil and the heart

Slug: `olive-oil-heart`. Question: "What does extra virgin olive oil really do to your heart?"
Audience: US adults, mostly 60+. Notes written 27 October 2026. Every figure below was read on the page named, except where marked **(secondary)**.

## 1. PREDIMED, republished 2018 (the key randomized trial)

- **Citation:** Estruch R, Ros E, Salas-Salvadó J, et al.; PREDIMED Study Investigators. Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease with a Mediterranean Diet Supplemented with Extra-Virgin Olive Oil or Nuts. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378(25):e34. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1800389. PMID 29897866. https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389 (abstract read through the Europe PMC record: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:29897866%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json).
- **Design:** multicenter randomized trial in Spain. n = 7,447 adults aged 55 to 80 (57% women), at high cardiovascular risk but without cardiovascular disease at enrollment. Three arms: Mediterranean diet + extra-virgin olive oil (EVOO), Mediterranean diet + mixed nuts, or a control diet (advice to reduce dietary fat). Quarterly education sessions; free olive oil, nuts or small non-food gifts depending on arm.
- **Primary endpoint:** heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death. Median follow-up 4.8 years; stopped after a prespecified interim analysis.
- **Results:** 288 events: 96 (3.8%) olive-oil arm, 83 (3.4%) nuts arm, 109 (4.4%) control. Intention-to-treat, adjusted: olive oil vs control HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.53 to 0.91), i.e. 31% lower relative risk (CI: 9% to 47% lower). Nuts vs control HR 0.72 (0.54 to 0.95). Results were similar after excluding 1,588 participants whose assignment was known or suspected to have departed from the protocol.
- **Absolute difference (my arithmetic from the crude percentages):** 4.4% minus 3.8% = 0.6 percentage points over about 5 years.
- **Retraction:** the 2013 paper was withdrawn and this corrected version published (electronic 13 June 2018). Deviations: household members enrolled without randomization, and inconsistent use of randomization tables at one site (description from the Europe PMC/NEJM record summary and the Harvard Nutrition Source page, https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/2018/06/22/predimed-retraction-republication; the search tool described the Harvard page as the most reliable account; I did not read the full text of the Harvard page, so the narration keeps this to the two deviations above).
- **Limitations:** one trial, one country, older adults at high risk; the intervention is a whole Mediterranean diet, so the oil cannot be separated from the rest of the pattern; control was "eat less fat" advice, not "eat as usual"; open-label diet trial; stopped early; randomization problems required republication.

## 2. PREDIMED observational analysis of olive-oil amounts

- **Citation:** Guasch-Ferré M, Hu FB, Martínez-González MA, et al. Olive oil intake and risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality in the PREDIMED Study. *BMC Med* 2014;12:78. DOI 10.1186/1741-7015-12-78. PMID 24886626. Full text (read): https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4030221/fullTextXML
- **Design:** prospective, observational analysis inside the trial, n = 7,216 men and women aged 55 to 80 (mean age 67, 57.4% women), median follow-up 4.8 years, 277 CVD events and 323 deaths. Food-frequency questionnaire.
- **Baseline mean intake by tertile (Table 1):** total olive oil 21.4, 38.8, 56.9 g/day; EVOO 9.1, 19.5, 34.6 g/day. (1 tablespoon = 13.5 g per the JACC cohort papers, so 34.6 g is about 2.6 tablespoons and 9.1 g is about two-thirds of a tablespoon.)
- **Results:** top vs bottom tertile of EVOO: HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.85) for CVD; total olive oil HR 0.65 (0.47 to 0.89). Each +10 g/day EVOO: 10% lower CVD risk. No significant association with cancer or all-cause mortality (abstract).
- **Limitations:** observational, so confounding is possible; authors mention limited statistical power for some outcomes.
- **Not used:** the 50 mL per day instruction for the PREDIMED oil group appeared only in a secondary summary, so the script does not quote it.

## 3. PREDIMED atrial fibrillation analysis

- **Citation:** Martínez-González MÁ, Toledo E, Arós F, et al. Extravirgin olive oil consumption reduces risk of atrial fibrillation: the PREDIMED trial. *Circulation* 2014;130(1):18-26. DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.113.006921. PMID 24787471.
- **Design:** post hoc analysis, n = 6,705 without AF at baseline, median 4.7 years. New AF cases: 72 (EVOO), 82 (nuts), 92 (control). EVOO vs control HR 0.62 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.85); nuts HR 0.89 (0.65 to 1.20).
- **Limitations:** post hoc, not the primary endpoint, small event numbers. Not used in the script, except as optional context (left out to keep the video focused).

## 4. US cohorts: cardiovascular disease (2020)

- **Citation:** Guasch-Ferré M, Liu G, Li Y, et al. Olive Oil Consumption and Cardiovascular Risk in U.S. Adults. *J Am Coll Cardiol* 2020;75(15):1729-1739. DOI 10.1016/j.jacc.2020.02.036. PMID 32147453. PMC7233327. (Abstract read via Europe PMC.)
- **Design:** Nurses' Health Study (61,181 women) and Health Professionals Follow-up Study (31,797 men), free of CVD and cancer at baseline in 1990, followed to 2014 (24 years). 9,797 incident CVD cases (6,034 CHD, 3,802 stroke).
- **Results:** more than 0.5 tablespoon (7 g) a day vs never/rarely: CVD pooled HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.94), CHD HR 0.82 (0.73 to 0.91). No significant association for total or ischemic stroke. Replacing 5 g/day of margarine, butter, mayonnaise or dairy fat with olive oil: 5% to 7% lower CVD and CHD risk. No significant association when compared with other plant oils combined.
- **Limitations:** observational; food-frequency questionnaires; mostly non-Hispanic white health professionals.

## 5. US cohorts: mortality (2022)

- **Citation:** Guasch-Ferré M, Li Y, Willett WC, et al. Consumption of Olive Oil and Risk of Total and Cause-Specific Mortality Among U.S. Adults. *J Am Coll Cardiol* 2022;79(2):101-112. DOI 10.1016/j.jacc.2021.10.041. PMID 35027106. Full text (read): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8851878/ . Press release (read): https://www.acc.org/About-ACC/Press-Releases/2022/01/10/18/46/Higher-Olive-Oil-Intake-Associated-with-Lower-Risk-of-CVD-Mortality
- **Design:** 60,582 women (NHS) and 31,801 men (HPFS), free of CVD and cancer at baseline 1990, 28 years of follow-up, 36,856 deaths. Diet by questionnaire every 4 years. Mean baseline age about 56 to 57 (enrollment ranges 30 to 55 for women, 40 to 75 for men). One tablespoon = 13.5 g. Olive oil summed from salad dressings, added to food or bread, and home baking and frying.
- **Results (>7 g/day vs never/rarely, pooled):** total mortality HR 0.81 (0.78 to 0.84); CVD 0.81 (0.75 to 0.87); cancer 0.83 (0.78 to 0.89); neurodegenerative 0.71 (0.64 to 0.78); respiratory 0.82 (0.72 to 0.93). Per +5 g/day, total mortality HR 0.96. Replacing 10 g/day of margarine, butter, mayonnaise or dairy fat with olive oil: 8% to 34% lower total and cause-specific mortality; margarine 0.87, butter 0.86, mayonnaise 0.81, dairy fat 0.87 (total mortality). No significant association when substituted for other vegetable oils.
- **Limitations (authors):** observational, residual confounding (higher users were more physically active, smoked less, ate more fruit and vegetables, more often had Southern European ancestry); mostly non-Hispanic white health professionals; "could not distinguish between olive oil varieties containing different amounts of polyphenols"; dressings, bread, baking and frying summed together, so raw vs cooked use was not compared. Editorialist Larsson asked whether the associations are causal.

## 6. EUROLIVE: phenolic content of olive oil

- **Citation:** Covas MI, Nyyssönen K, Poulsen HE, et al.; EUROLIVE Study Group. The effect of polyphenols in olive oil on heart disease risk factors: a randomized trial. *Ann Intern Med* 2006;145(5):333-341. DOI 10.7326/0003-4819-145-5-200609050-00006. PMID 16954359. (Abstract read via Europe PMC.)
- **Design:** randomized, crossover, 200 healthy men, six centers in five European countries; 25 mL a day of olive oil with low (2.7 mg/kg), medium (164) or high (366) phenolic content; 3 weeks each with 2-week washouts. The age range (20 to 60) comes from a trade-press summary **(secondary)**; the abstract does not state age.
- **Results:** HDL rose linearly with phenolic content: +0.025 (low), +0.032 (medium), +0.045 mmol/L (high). In mg/dL (my conversion, x 38.67): about +1.0, +1.2, +1.7. Total cholesterol/HDL ratio fell linearly. Triglycerides fell about 0.05 mmol/L with all three oils. Oxidized LDL: +1.21 U/L (low, CI -0.8 to 3.6), -1.48 (medium), -3.21 (high, CI -5.1 to -0.8).
- **Limitations (authors):** short intervention periods, self-reported diet, possible interactions; healthy men only; surrogate markers, not heart attacks.

## 7. FDA qualified health claim for oleic acid oils

- **Source:** FDA constituent update, 19 November 2018, "FDA Completes Review of Qualified Health Claim Petition for Oleic Acid and the Risk of Coronary Heart Disease." http://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-completes-review-qualified-health-claim-petition-oleic-acid-and-risk-coronary-heart-disease
- **Claim:** "Supportive but not conclusive scientific evidence suggests that daily consumption of about 1½ tablespoons (20 grams) of oils containing high levels of oleic acid, when replaced for fats and oils higher in saturated fat, may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease," and the oil should not increase total daily calories. Oils with at least 70% oleic acid, including olive oil. Qualified claims do not meet the "significant scientific agreement" standard.
- **Not used:** the "six of seven studies" detail came from trade-press coverage only.

## 8. American Heart Association

- Lichtenstein AH, Appel LJ, Vadiveloo M, et al. 2021 Dietary Guidance to Improve Cardiovascular Health: A Scientific Statement From the American Heart Association. *Circulation* 2021;144(23):e472-e487. DOI 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001031. PMID 34724806. Recommends liquid plant oils instead of tropical oils and partially hydrogenated fats. It does not single out extra virgin olive oil (as far as the abstract shows).
- A 2026 AHA update was mentioned in news results but I did not read it, so it is not used.

## 9. Heating extra virgin olive oil (lab studies)

- Gómez-Alonso S, Fregapane G, Salvador MD, Gordon MH. Changes in phenolic composition and antioxidant activity of virgin olive oil during frying. *J Agric Food Chem* 2003;51(3):667-672. DOI 10.1021/jf025932w. PMID 12537439. Oil reused for french fries: the first fry (10 min at 180 °C) cut hydroxytyrosol-related compounds to 50 to 60% of the original level; about 10% remained after six fries; polar compounds rose sharply from the sixth fry.
- Brenes M, García A, Dobarganes MC, Velasco J, Romero C. Influence of thermal treatments simulating cooking processes on the polyphenol content in virgin olive oil. *J Agric Food Chem* 2002;50(21):5962-5967. DOI 10.1021/jf020506w. PMID 12358466. Frying at 180 °C reduced hydroxytyrosol- and tyrosol-type compounds; lignans persisted; microwaving 10 min caused only minor losses.
- Allouche Y, Jiménez A, Gaforio JJ, Uceda M, Beltrán G. How heating affects extra virgin olive oil quality indexes and chemical composition. *J Agric Food Chem* 2007;55(23):9646-9654. DOI 10.1021/jf070628u. PMID 17935291. Arbequina and Picual EVOO heated at 180 °C for 36 h: tocopherols and polyphenols degraded most; oleic acid, sterols and triterpenic compounds stayed stable.
- All three: lab studies measuring chemistry, not health outcomes in people. All read as abstracts via Europe PMC. I found no human outcome trial of cooking with vs without extra virgin olive oil. Smoke-point figures were not verified, so none are used.

## 10. Quality of "extra virgin" on US shelves

- UC Davis Olive Center news release, July 2010: https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/most-imported-olive-oils-don’t-match-‘extra-virgin’-claims-study-finds . Samples bought in one week from 3 March 2010 in California stores: 14 imported and 5 California brands labeled extra virgin (three bottles per imported brand, two per California brand). 69% of imported and 10% of California oils failed international extra virgin standards (sensory and chemical tests, Australian Oils Research Laboratory and UC Davis). Funded by Corto Olive, California Olive Ranch and the California Olive Oil Council.
- **Limitations:** 2010 data, California stores only, small sample, industry funding (producers who benefit from the finding), and the International Olive Council chemistry group disputed it **(secondary)**. The script uses it only as "labels can vary", not as a brand ranking.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Replacing butter, margarine, mayonnaise and dairy fat with olive oil is linked with lower cardiovascular and total mortality in two large US cohorts (about 92,000 people, 28 years).
- A Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil produced fewer major cardiovascular events than a low-fat advice group in older Spanish adults at high risk (HR 0.69), and the result survived republication and exclusion of 1,588 participants.
- Oils high in oleic acid, replacing saturated fat, carry an FDA qualified health claim at about 1.5 tablespoons (20 g) a day.

**Uncertain**
- Whether the oil itself, rather than the whole diet pattern or the lifestyle of people who choose olive oil, causes the benefit.
- Whether extra virgin beats refined olive oil for heart outcomes. EUROLIVE shows small differences in HDL and oxidized LDL with higher-phenolic oil; the US cohorts could not tell varieties apart; PREDIMED observational estimates for EVOO and total olive oil were similar (HR 0.61 vs 0.65).
- The best amount. Spanish top-tertile EVOO users averaged about 35 g/day; US benefit was linked to more than 7 g/day; FDA cites 20 g/day.
- Stroke: no clear link in the US cohorts.
- Whether cooking changes the heart benefit. Heating lowers phenolics, but no study compared cooked and raw use for health outcomes.

**False or unsupported**
- That olive oil is a medicine or can replace prescribed treatment.
- That heating olive oil turns it into a harmful substance: the lab data show phenolics fade and, with repeated frying, breakdown products accumulate, but not that moderate home cooking is dangerous. Equally, "stable when heated" is not a health outcome.
- That olive oil beats other vegetable oils: in both US cohorts there was no significant difference against other vegetable oils combined.
- That more is always better: the FDA claim requires no extra calories.
