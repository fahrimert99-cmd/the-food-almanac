# Research notes: Can the MIND diet slow memory decline after 60?

Slug: `mind-diet-memory`. Notes written 30 October 2026. Every figure below was read on the PubMed/PMC page named in the entry, unless marked "not opened".

## 1. Rush cohort: MIND diet and Alzheimer's disease incidence
- **Citation:** Morris MC, Tangney CC, Wang Y, Sacks FM, Bennett DA, Aggarwal NT. MIND diet associated with reduced incidence of Alzheimer's disease. *Alzheimers Dement.* 2015;11(9):1007-1014. doi:10.1016/j.jalz.2014.11.009. PMID 25681666; PMC4532650.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25681666/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4532650/
- **Design/population:** Prospective cohort (Rush Memory and Aging Project, Chicago). n = 923, ages 58–98, free of dementia at baseline. Mean follow-up about 4.5 years. Diet by semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire. Adjusted for age, sex, education, APOE-ε4, cognitively stimulating activities, physical activity, total energy. 144 incident Alzheimer's cases in the results text.
- **Numbers (hazard ratio vs lowest third of MIND score):** middle third HR 0.65 (95% CI 0.44–0.98), i.e. 35% lower rate; top third HR 0.47 (0.26–0.76), i.e. 53% lower rate. The 35% and 53% are calculated as 1 − HR. The CI for the top third (0.26–0.76) therefore corresponds to a rate that is about 24% to 74% lower (also 1 − HR); the script uses this when it says the margin of error is wide. For the DASH diet only the top third was significant (HR 0.61, 0.38–0.97); for the Mediterranean diet only the top third (HR 0.46, 0.26–0.79).
- **MIND score (Table 1, PMC):** 15 items, score 0–15 (each item 0, 0.5 or 1; olive oil 0 or 1). Ten brain-healthy: whole grains ≥3 servings/day; green leafy vegetables ≥6 servings/week; other vegetables ≥1/day; berries ≥2/week; nuts ≥5/week; beans >3/week; fish ≥1/week; poultry ≥2/week; olive oil as the primary oil at home; wine 1 drink/day. Five limited: red meat <4/week; butter and stick margarine <1 tablespoon/day; cheese <1/week; pastries and sweets <5/week; fried or fast food <1/week. Observed range 2.5–12.5; tertile 1 = 2.5–6.5, tertile 2 = 7–8, tertile 3 = 8.5–12.5.
- **Limitations (authors):** observational, so no cause and effect; food questionnaire limited (the berry item covered strawberries only; olive oil was a single question); average 3.8 years between diet assessment and diagnosis, so people with preclinical disease may already have changed their diet (excluding early cases did not change the results much). The CI for the top third is wide.

## 2. Rush cohort: MIND diet and cognitive decline
- **Citation:** Morris MC, Tangney CC, Wang Y, Sacks FM, Barnes LL, Bennett DA, Aggarwal NT. MIND diet slows cognitive decline with aging. *Alzheimers Dement.* 2015;11(9):1015-1022. doi:10.1016/j.jalz.2015.04.011. PMID 26086182; PMC4581900.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26086182/
- **Design/population:** Prospective cohort, Rush Memory and Aging Project, n = 960, average follow-up 4.7 years, older adults without dementia at entry. Higher MIND score linked to slower decline in global cognition and in five cognitive domains.
- **Numbers:** the top-scoring group's rate of decline was comparable to people 7.5 years younger than those in the lowest-scoring group (the authors' translation of the effect size into age).
- **Limitations:** observational; the authors state that a dietary intervention trial is needed to confirm. The abstract pages I read did not give a mean age for this cohort, so the script does not state one.

## 3. Rush cohort: leafy greens
- **Citation:** Morris MC, Wang Y, Barnes LL, Bennett DA, Dawson-Hughes B, Booth SL. Nutrients and bioactives in green leafy vegetables and cognitive decline: prospective study. *Neurology.* 2018;90(3):e214-e222. doi:10.1212/WNL.0000000000004815. PMID 29263222; PMC5772164.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29263222/
- **Design/population:** Memory and Aging Project, n = 960, ages 58–99, mean follow-up 4.7 years, at least two cognitive assessments.
- **Numbers:** highest fifth of intake (median 1.3 servings/day) declined more slowly than the lowest fifth (median 0.09 servings/day), by 0.05 standardized units per year (p = 0.0001), "the equivalent of being 11 years younger in age" (abstract wording).
- **Limitations:** observational; a serving was not defined in grams or cups on the page I read; food questionnaire.

## 4. Nurses' Health Study: berries
- **Citation:** Devore EE, Kang JH, Breteler MMB, Grodstein F. Dietary intakes of berries and flavonoids in relation to cognitive decline. *Ann Neurol.* 2012;72(1):135-143. doi:10.1002/ana.23594. PMID 22535616; PMC3582325.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22535616/
- **Design/population:** Prospective cohort, n = 16,010 women aged 70 or older (all women, nurses). Diet questionnaire every 4 years from 1980; cognition tested at baseline (1995–2001) and twice more at 2-year intervals.
- **Numbers (global cognitive score, highest vs lowest intake, adjusted):** blueberries mean difference 0.04 (95% CI 0.01–0.07); strawberries 0.03 (0.00–0.06). The authors compared these effects to about 1.5 to 2.5 years of aging, i.e. "delaying cognitive aging by up to 2.5 years".
- **Limitations:** observational; women only; the strawberry CI touches zero; residual confounding by other healthy habits.

## 5. The randomized trial (NEJM 2023)
- **Citation:** Barnes LL, Dhana K, Liu X, et al. Trial of the MIND diet for prevention of cognitive decline in older persons. *N Engl J Med.* 2023;389(7):602-611. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2302368. PMID 37466280. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02817074. Author list (20) on PubMed begins Barnes, Dhana, Liu, Carey, Ventrelle, and ends Tangney, Holland, Aggarwal, Arfanakis, Morris, Sacks. The site/institution names beyond Rush were not visible on the pages I opened, so the script credits Barnes and Rush University only.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37466280/ ; summary of investigator comments: https://www.news-medical.net/news/20230727/Long-term-commitment-to-MIND-diet-needed-for-reaping-brain-health-benefits.aspx (the NEJM site itself returned 403, so I could not read the full text).
- **Design/population:** two-site randomized trial, 3 years. 1,929 screened, 604 enrolled: 301 MIND diet, 303 control diet. Participants were cognitively unimpaired, aged 65 or older, with a family history of dementia, body-mass index above 25 and a suboptimal diet on a 14-item questionnaire. Both groups received mild caloric restriction and adherence counseling. Primary outcome: change in a global cognition score (12-test battery). Secondary: MRI brain measures in a non-random subsample. 93.4% completed the trial. Funded by the National Institute on Aging.
- **Numbers:** global cognition rose +0.205 standardized units (MIND) and +0.170 (control). Mean difference 0.035 (95% CI −0.022 to 0.092; P = 0.23), not significant. White-matter hyperintensities, hippocampal volume and total gray- and white-matter volume were similar between groups.
- **Why both improved (news-medical summary of investigators; not tested in the trial):** both groups lost about 5 kg (roughly 5.5% of initial body weight); dietitian support in both arms; the control group also improved its diet; possible practice effects on the tests. Barnes: "it could have been weight loss that benefited cognition in this trial." Ventrelle: the shared support may have "hindered the ability to detect significant differences".
- **Limitations:** three years is short for dementia; participants were cognitively healthy at the start; the control diet was not a typical diet and received counseling; the trial can't separate the diet from weight loss and coaching; modest sample.

## 6. US POINTER (multidomain lifestyle trial)
- **Citation:** Baker LD, Espeland MA, Whitmer RA, et al. Structured vs self-guided multidomain lifestyle interventions for global cognitive function: the US POINTER randomized clinical trial. *JAMA.* 2025;334(8):681-691. doi:10.1001/jama.2025.12923. PMID 40720610.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40720610/
- **Design/population:** randomized, 2 years, n = 2,111 adults aged 60–79 (mean 68.2, SD 5.2) at risk of cognitive decline: 1,056 structured, 1,055 self-guided. Both programs encouraged physical and cognitive activity, a healthy diet, social engagement and cardiovascular monitoring; they differed in structure, intensity and accountability.
- **Numbers:** global cognition rate of change 0.243 SD/year (95% CI 0.227–0.258) structured vs 0.213 SD/year (0.198–0.229) self-guided; difference 0.029 SD/year (0.008–0.050; P = .008).
- **Limitations:** no no-intervention control; both groups improved; the diet component can't be separated from exercise and other parts; authors call for longer follow-up to judge clinical relevance. (A search result stated that the structured arm's nutrition part was the MIND diet; I did not see this on the abstract page, so the script says only "a healthy diet".)

## 7. Meta-analysis
- **Citation:** Huang L, Tao Y, Chen H, et al. Mediterranean-Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet and cognitive function and its decline: a prospective study and meta-analysis of cohort studies. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2023;118(1):174-182. doi:10.1016/j.ajcnut.2023.04.025. PMID 37105521.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37105521/
- **Design/population:** new cohort from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (n = 4,066, median follow-up 3 years) plus a meta-analysis with 7 other cohort studies, 26,103 participants in total. PROSPERO CRD42022330417.
- **Numbers:** meta-analysis: each 1-SD higher MIND score was associated with a 0.042 higher global cognitive z-score (95% CI 0.020–0.065). For annual decline: 0.010 slower per year (95% CI −0.001 to 0.021), which includes zero, so not conclusive. In the Chinese cohort, each 3-point higher score = 0.110 higher z-score (about one year younger).
- **Limitations:** observational cohorts; mean age not given in the abstract.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- In several Rush cohorts of older adults, higher MIND scores went with fewer Alzheimer's cases and slower measured decline (HR 0.47 in the top third; 7.5 years "younger" in decline rate). These are associations.
- A 3-year randomized trial in 604 adults aged 65+ found no significant difference between the MIND diet and a control diet with mild calorie restriction (0.035, 95% CI −0.022 to 0.092). Both groups improved.
- Leafy greens and berries show consistent but observational signals (Rush leafy greens, Nurses' Health Study berries).

**Uncertain**
- Whether the MIND diet itself protects memory over decades: the trial was only 3 years long, in people with healthy cognition.
- Why both groups improved: practice effects, weight loss (about 5 kg each) and counseling are plausible but untested explanations.
- Whether single foods (greens, berries) cause benefit; the effect sizes expressed as "years younger" are statistical translations.
- The pooled link of MIND score with slower decline is small and its range crosses zero.
- Whole-lifestyle programs (US POINTER) improved cognition slightly more with structure, but the diet part is not separable.

**False or overstated**
- "The MIND diet cuts Alzheimer's risk by 53%": that is an observational comparison of top vs bottom thirds, not a trial result.
- "The MIND diet makes your brain 7.5 (or 11) years younger": it is a statistical translation of decline rates.
- "Berries, greens or any superfood prevent or reverse dementia": not shown; no food is a treatment.
