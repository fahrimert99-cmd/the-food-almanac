# Research notes: Does skipping breakfast hurt your blood sugar or muscles after 60?

Slug: `breakfast-most-important-meal`. All abstracts below were opened (PubMed itself blocked the fetcher, so abstracts were read through the Europe PMC record of each paper, or the publisher page). Numbers are copied from those abstracts.

## 1. Where the slogan came from

- Claim: "Breakfast is the most important meal of the day" is a marketing-era slogan more than a research finding.
- Evidence level: weak, popular-history sources only. Accounts differ on who said it first (some credit a 1944 General Foods / Grape-Nuts campaign, some Kellogg, some the 1920s Edward Bernays bacon campaign in which about 4,500 doctors signed a statement favoring a hearty breakfast). No primary document was seen. **In the script, say only that the phrase is often linked to early cereal and bacon marketing and that accounts of who first said it differ. Do not name a single inventor.**
- Source: The Takeout, "Why Is Breakfast Called 'The Most Important Meal Of The Day?'" https://www.thetakeout.com/1805611/breakfast-most-important-meal/ (names no single coiner; cites Bernays bacon campaign, Kellogg cereal marketing; speculation about both).
- Scholarly check on the belief itself: Brown AW, Bohan Brown MM, Allison DB. Belief beyond the evidence: using the proposed effect of breakfast on obesity to show 2 practices that distort scientific evidence. Am J Clin Nutr 2013;98(5):1298-1308. doi:10.3945/ajcn.113.064410. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24004890/ . Finding: the idea that skipping breakfast causes weight gain is supported by observational associations; causal support is lacking; belief exceeded the strength of the evidence. Limitation: an analysis of the literature, not a new trial.

## 2. Randomized trials on weight and energy intake

### Sievert et al., BMJ 2019 (meta-analysis)
- Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, Wang Y, Hughes HJ, Malek M, Cicuttini FM. Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ 2019;364:l42. doi:10.1136/bmj.l42. https://www.bmj.com/content/364/bmj.l42 (PMID 30700403)
- Design: 13 RCTs in adults in high-income countries, breakfast vs no breakfast. 7 trials measured weight, 10 measured energy intake.
- Weight: people assigned to breakfast ended up slightly heavier than skippers: mean difference 0.44 kg (95% CI 0.07 to 0.82; I² = 43%), skippers lost slightly more.
- Energy intake: breakfast eaters took in 259.79 kcal/day more (95% CI 78.87 to 440.71; I² = 80%).
- Limitations: every trial at high or unclear risk of bias in at least one domain; short follow-up (about 7 weeks for weight, about 2 weeks for intake); mostly low quality. Adults in general, not specifically older adults. Authors: caution needed when recommending breakfast for weight loss.

### Chowdhury et al., AJCN 2016 (Bath Breakfast Project, obese adults)
- Chowdhury EA, Richardson JD, Holman GD, Tsintzas K, Thompson D, Betts JA. The causal role of breakfast in energy balance and health: a randomized controlled trial in obese adults. Am J Clin Nutr 2016;103(3):747-756. doi:10.3945/ajcn.115.122044. (PMID 26864365)
- Design: 23 obese adults (15 women, 8 men), ages 21–60, randomized for 6 weeks to daily breakfast (at least 700 kcal before 11:00) or fasting until noon (0 kcal).
- Results: body mass rose in both groups with no treatment effect on body composition; resting metabolic rate stable; the insulin response to an oral glucose tolerance test fell with breakfast and rose with fasting (P = 0.05); the authors concluded insulin sensitivity improved with breakfast relative to fasting.
- Limitations: small; adults 21–60 only; 6 weeks.

### Betts et al., AJCN 2014 (lean adults)
- Betts JA, Richardson JD, Chowdhury EA, Holman GD, Tsintzas K, Thompson D. The causal role of breakfast in energy balance and health: a randomized controlled trial in lean adults. Am J Clin Nutr 2014;100(2):539-547. doi:10.3945/ajcn.114.083402. (PMID 24898233; PMC4095658)
- 33 lean adults, ages 21–60, 6 weeks. Body mass and adiposity did not differ; resting metabolic rate stable (within 11 kcal/day); intake 539 kcal/day higher with breakfast (95% CI 157 to 920); afternoon/evening glucose more variable with fasting.

## 3. Blood sugar: the second-meal effect

### Jakubowicz et al., Diabetes Care 2015 (key study)
- Jakubowicz D, Wainstein J, Ahren B, Landau Z, Bar-Dayan Y, Froy O. Fasting until noon triggers increased postprandial hyperglycemia and impaired insulin response after lunch and dinner in individuals with type 2 diabetes: a randomized clinical trial. Diabetes Care 2015;38(10):1820-1826. doi:10.2337/dc15-0761. (PMID 26220945)
- Design: randomized crossover; 22 adults with type 2 diabetes (mean age 56.9, mean diabetes duration 8.4 years, BMI 28.2, HbA1c 7.7%). Two test days: breakfast + lunch + dinner, or lunch + dinner only. Lunch and dinner were each 700 kcal, same content on both days.
- Lunch (area under the curve 0–180 min, no-breakfast day vs breakfast day): glucose 36.8% higher, free fatty acids 41.1% higher, glucagon 14.8% higher, insulin 17% lower, intact GLP-1 19% lower (P < 0.0001).
- Dinner: glucose 26.6% higher, free fatty acids 29.6% higher, glucagon 11.5% higher, insulin 7.9% lower, intact GLP-1 16.5% lower. Insulin peak delayed 30 minutes after lunch and dinner on the no-breakfast day.
- Limitations: 22 people, mean age under 60, single test days (not weeks of habit), meals were set high-carbohydrate test meals in a lab. Did not test prediabetes. Does not show A1C changes.

### Ogata et al., Br J Nutr 2019 (healthy young men)
- Ogata H, Hatamoto Y, Goto Y, Tajiri E, Yoshimura E, Kiyono K, Uehara Y, Kawanaka K, Omi N, Tanaka H. Association between breakfast skipping and postprandial hyperglycaemia after lunch in healthy young individuals. Br J Nutr 2019;122(4):431-440. doi:10.1017/S0007114519001235. (PMID 31486356)
- 9 healthy young men (19–24): higher glycemic response after lunch when breakfast was skipped (P < 0.001), higher NEFA after lunch; 24-hour average glucose did NOT differ (P = 0.179).
- Limitation: tiny and young. Shows the second-meal effect is not only a diabetes phenomenon but says nothing about older adults. The script may use "the effect has been seen in healthy young men too, but their 24-hour average glucose did not differ".

## 4. Long-term outcomes (observational)

- Rong S, Snetselaar LG, Xu G, Sun Y, Liu B, Wallace RB, Bao W. Association of skipping breakfast with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. J Am Coll Cardiol 2019;73(16):2025-2032. doi:10.1016/j.jacc.2019.01.065. (PMID 31023424)
- NHANES III cohort, 6,550 adults aged 40–75; 112,148 person-years of follow-up (about 17 years per person on average, derived); 2,318 deaths, 619 cardiovascular. "Never" eating breakfast vs eating it daily: cardiovascular mortality HR 1.87 (95% CI 1.14 to 3.04); all-cause mortality HR 1.19 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.42, not statistically significant).
- Limitations: observational; breakfast reported at one time point; people who skip breakfast differ in smoking, diet, income and health, and statistical adjustment cannot remove all of this; association not cause. Authors do not claim causation.
- Review: Santos HO, Genario R, Macedo RCO, Pareek M, Tinsley GM. Association of breakfast skipping with cardiovascular outcomes and cardiometabolic risk factors: an updated review of clinical evidence. Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr 2022;62(2):466-474. doi:10.1080/10408398.2020.1819768. (PMID 32935557) Skipping associated with higher risk but unmeasured confounding may explain part; long-term randomized trials with clinical outcomes are sparse.

## 5. Muscle after 60: protein and the morning

- Bauer J, Biolo G, Cederholm T, et al. Evidence-based recommendations for optimal dietary protein intake in older people: a position paper from the PROT-AGE Study Group. J Am Med Dir Assoc 2013;14(8):542-559. doi:10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021. (PMID 23867520) Healthy older adults (over 65) should take in at least 1.0 to 1.2 g protein per kg body weight per day; people exercising at least 1.2 g/kg; people with illness 1.2–1.5 g/kg; severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30) may need to limit protein. (Seen via Europe PMC record summary; the per-meal figure was NOT verified, so do not quote it for PROT-AGE.)
- Mamerow MM, Mettler JA, English KL, et al. Dietary protein distribution positively influences 24-h muscle protein synthesis in healthy adults. J Nutr 2014;144(6):876-880. doi:10.3945/jn.113.185280. (PMID 24477298) Randomized crossover, **8 healthy adults, mean age 36.9** (young). Even distribution (about 31.5, 29.9, 32.7 g at breakfast, lunch, dinner) vs skewed to dinner (10.7, 16.0, 63.4 g): 24-h muscle protein synthesis 25% higher with even distribution (0.075 vs 0.056 %/h, P = 0.003). Limitation: young, tiny, 7-day feeding.
- Kim IY, Schutzler S, Schrader AM, Spencer HJ, Azhar G, Wolfe RR, Ferrando AA. Protein intake distribution pattern does not affect anabolic response, lean body mass, muscle strength or function over 8 weeks in older adults: a randomized controlled trial. Clin Nutr 2018;37(2):488-493. doi:10.1016/j.clnu.2017.02.020. (PMID 28318687) **14 older adults**, 8 weeks, 1.1 g/kg/day either evenly spread or mostly at dinner (15/20/65%): no differences in lean mass, strength, or muscle protein synthesis. Limitation: tiny. This contradicts Mamerow; both must be presented.
- Norton C, Toomey C, McCormack WG, Francis P, Saunders J, Kerin E, Jakeman P. Protein supplementation at breakfast and lunch for 24 weeks beyond habitual intakes increases whole-body lean tissue mass in healthy older adults. J Nutr 2016;146(1):65-69. doi:10.3945/jn.115.219022. (PMID 26581685) Single-blind RCT, **60 adults, mean age 61 ± 5** (range targeted 50–70), 24 weeks. Milk protein drink 0.165 g/kg at breakfast and midday vs isoenergetic maltodextrin. Whole-body lean tissue +0.45 kg (95% CI 0.06 to 0.83) in the protein group vs −0.16 kg (95% CI −0.49 to 0.17) in the control group (P = 0.006); appendicular lean mass +0.27 kg vs no change. Limitations: supplement drink, not food; breakfast and lunch together, so cannot isolate breakfast; lean mass, not function.
- Kinoshita K, Otsuka R, Nishita Y, et al. Breakfast protein quality and muscle strength in Japanese older adults: a community-based longitudinal study. J Am Med Dir Assoc 2022;23(5):729-735. doi:10.1016/j.jamda.2021.11.037. (PMID 35007507) 701 adults aged 60–83, mean follow-up 6.9 years, 282 developed weakness. Compared with the lowest breakfast protein quality group, odds of incident weakness 0.71 (95% CI 0.43–1.18) in the middle group and 0.50 (95% CI 0.29–0.86) in the highest group; independent of total protein intake. Observational, Japanese diet, "protein quality" is an amino acid score, not grams.
- Kim HK, Chijiki H, Fukazawa M, et al. Supplementation of protein at breakfast rather than at dinner and lunch is effective on skeletal muscle mass in older adults. Front Nutr 2021;8:797004. doi:10.3389/fnut.2021.797004. People 65+, cross-sectional studies plus a 12-week placebo-controlled RCT in 40 women; authors conclude breakfast protein "might have relatively stronger effects" on muscle mass. (Only the abstract conclusion was seen; no numbers — not used in the narration beyond the notes.)
- Loenneke JP, Loprinzi PD, Murphy CH, Phillips SM. Per meal dose and frequency of protein consumption is associated with lean mass and muscle performance. Clin Nutr 2016;35(6):1506-1511. doi:10.1016/j.clnu.2016.04.002. (PMID 27086196) Cross-sectional NHANES 1999–2002, **1,081 adults aged 50–85**. More meals with at least 30 g of protein linked with greater leg lean mass and knee strength; the link leveled off at about 30 g per meal for one such meal and about 45 g for two. Association, not cause. Authors suggest 1–2 daily meals with 30–45 g may help maintain lean mass and strength with aging.

## 6. Morning medicines and skipped meals

- Levothyroxine label (DailyMed, Accord Healthcare label): "Administer levothyroxine sodium tablets as a single daily dose, on an empty stomach, one-half to one hour before breakfast" with a full glass of water; take at least 4 hours before or after drugs known to interfere with absorption; iron and calcium supplements and antacids decrease absorption. https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=a6233381-3043-4e9a-aaa6-a6b105e5142b
- Metformin (Glucophage prescribing information): starting dose "given with meals"; common reactions include diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, indigestion, abdominal discomfort. Seen through third-party copies of the label (Drugs.com). https://drugs.com/pro/glucophage.html
- Low blood sugar and skipped meals: NIDDK (archived and current pages): taking insulin or insulin-stimulating pills (sulfonylureas) and then skipping or delaying a meal can cause hypoglycemia. Archived copy: https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20090113181208/http:/diabetes.niddk.nih.gov/dm/pubs/hypoglycemia/hypoglycemia.pdf ; current page https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/preventing-problems/low-blood-glucose-hypoglycemia (the current page text was not retrieved).
- No stop/change advice: the script says to ask a doctor or pharmacist before changing the timing of any dose.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- "Most important meal of the day" is a slogan, not a finding. Randomized trials of breakfast for weight (Sievert 2019, 13 trials) found no weight benefit from eating it, and breakfast eaters took in more calories, though trials were short and low quality.
- In people with type 2 diabetes, skipping breakfast made blood sugar after a fixed 700-calorie lunch about 37% higher and after dinner about 27% higher in one 22-person crossover trial (Jakubowicz 2015), with lower insulin and GLP-1 responses.
- Observational studies link regular breakfast skipping with higher cardiovascular death (NHANES, HR 1.87), but this cannot show cause.

**Uncertain**
- Whether the second-meal effect holds up over weeks, in people over 60, in prediabetes, or with ordinary meals; whether it changes A1C. Not tested in the trials found. Healthy young men also showed higher post-lunch glucose but no difference in 24-hour average glucose.
- Whether protein timing at breakfast matters for muscle. Short crossover work in young adults found benefit from even spread; an 8-week trial in 14 older adults did not. A 24-week trial in 60 adults (mean 61) found about 0.6 kg more lean mass with protein drinks at breakfast and lunch. Total daily protein (at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg for older adults) has the stronger case.
- Whether skipping breakfast itself harms muscle: no trial was found that randomized people over 60 to eat or skip breakfast and measured muscle. This is the main gap.

**False or misleading**
- "Skipping breakfast slows your metabolism": 6-week trials found resting metabolic rate unchanged (within about 8–11 kcal/day).
- "Breakfast is necessary for weight loss": meta-analysis does not support it.
- "Breakfast is dangerous" or "skipping is a cure": neither is supported; people with diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas have a real low-blood-sugar risk when skipping meals.
