# Research notes: Does cranberry juice really prevent urinary tract infections?

Slug: `cranberry-uti` · Researched 10 November 2026.
Note: PubMed pages returned a cookie wall, and the Cochrane Library page returned HTTP 403. The Cochrane figures below were read from the PMC full text (PMC10108827) and the cochrane.org plain-language summary. I did not see the JAMA 2016 and AJCN 2016 abstracts on PubMed itself; their figures come from the EurekAlert release (JAMA) and the Cranberry Institute abstract page (AJCN, which reproduces the journal abstract). These are flagged where relevant.

## 1. Cochrane review (main source)
- **Citation:** Williams G, Hahn D, Stephens JH, Craig JC, Hodson EM. Cranberries for preventing urinary tract infections. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2023;(4):CD001321. DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD001321.pub6. PMID 37068952. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108827/ . Plain-language summary (Issue 11, 10 Nov 2023 version, search to 13 March 2023): https://www.cochrane.org/CD001321/UTI_cranberry-for-preventing-urinary-tract-infections
- **Design:** systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized and quasi-randomized trials; **50 studies, 8,857 participants**; 45 compared cranberry with placebo or no treatment; others vs antibiotics or probiotics. Trials had to last at least four weeks. Juice or concentrate: 19 trials (3,936 participants); tablets, capsules or powder: 29 trials (4,682).
- **Overall (any cranberry product vs placebo/no treatment, symptomatic culture-verified UTI):** 26 studies, 6,211 participants, **RR 0.70 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.84)**, I² = 69%, moderate certainty (about 30% lower risk).
- **Subgroups (RR, 95% CI):**
  - Women with recurrent UTIs: 8 studies, 1,555 women, **0.74 (0.55 to 0.99)**, I² 54%, moderate certainty (about 26% lower; CI = 1% to 45% lower).
  - Children: 5 studies, 504 children, **0.46 (0.32 to 0.68)**, moderate certainty. (The summary-of-findings table in the same document shows 4 studies/428 children, RR 0.53; both are "roughly half". The script says "roughly half" and the chart uses the abstract figure.)
  - People susceptible to UTI after an intervention (e.g. bladder radiotherapy): 6 studies, 1,434 participants, **0.47 (0.37 to 0.61)**, I² 0% (certainty low in the April abstract; the script does not state a certainty grade for this group).
  - Elderly institutionalized: 3 studies, 1,489 participants, **0.93 (0.67 to 1.30)**: little or no benefit. (Certainty reported as low in one place and moderate in another; the script gives no grade.)
  - Pregnant women: 3 studies, 765, **1.06 (0.75 to 1.50)**: little or no benefit.
  - Adults with neuromuscular bladder dysfunction and incomplete emptying: 3 studies, 464, **0.97 (0.78 to 1.19)**: little or no benefit, low certainty.
- **Juice vs capsules:** unclear whether efficacy differs between juice and tablets/capsules; certainty very low. No clear difference between low, moderate and high PAC doses.
- **vs antibiotics:** 2 studies, 385 participants, RR 1.03 (0.80 to 1.33): little or no difference; review could not determine whether cranberry is better or worse.
- **vs probiotics:** 3 studies, 215 participants, RR 0.39 (0.27 to 0.56) (low certainty).
- **Adverse events:** GI events, 10 studies, 2,166 participants, RR 1.33 (1.00 to 1.77), I² 0%. Most common: stomach pain. Adherence was poor with juice in some trials; palatability caused withdrawals; one pregnancy trial had many withdrawals because of the volume of juice required.
- **Mechanism (as stated by Cochrane):** proanthocyanidins (PACs) are thought to stop bacteria sticking to the bladder wall. This is a hypothesis; trials measure outcomes.
- **Limitations (from the review):** high heterogeneity (one trial, Barbosa-Cesnik 2011, pointed the other way; removing it lowered I² to 32% for the recurrent-UTI group); many small trials, wide CIs; unclear/high risk of bias for allocation concealment and blinding in many; inconsistent UTI definitions; PAC dose not standardized or often not measured; commercial involvement in 23 studies (5 with Ocean Spray); attrition above 10% in 13 studies; short treatment duration.

## 2. Maki et al. 2016 (juice trial)
- **Citation:** Maki KC, Kaspar KL, Khoo C, Derrig LH, Schild AL, Gupta K. Consumption of a cranberry juice beverage lowered the number of clinical urinary tract infection episodes in women with a recent history of urinary tract infection. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2016;103(6):1434-1442. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01776021. Abstract: https://www.cranberryinstitute.org/cranberry-health-research/abstract/consumption-of-cranberry-juice-beverage-lowered-number-of-clinical-urinary-tract-infection-episodes-in-women-with-recent-history-of-urinary-tract
- **Design:** randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter; 24 weeks; **373 women** with a recent UTI history (185 cranberry, 188 placebo); mean age 40.9; one 240 mL (about 8 oz) serving daily; adherence 98%; 86% completed.
- **Results:** clinical UTI episodes 39 vs 67; antibiotic-use-adjusted incidence rate ratio **0.61 (0.41 to 0.91), P = 0.016** (about 39% lower rate); one clinical UTI prevented per **3.2 woman-years** (95% CI 2.0 to 13.1); time to culture-positive UTI **not** different (HR 0.97, 0.56 to 1.67).
- **Limitations:** women averaging 41, not older adults; clinical (investigator-diagnosed) endpoint, culture-confirmed endpoint negative; beverage composition not seen by me; industry-linked research area (Cochrane notes Ocean Spray involvement in 5 trials; I did not verify the funder of this specific trial from the journal itself, so the script does not state it).

## 3. Juthani-Mehta et al. 2016 (capsules, older women in nursing homes)
- **Citation:** Juthani-Mehta M, et al. Effect of cranberry capsules on bacteriuria plus pyuria among older women in nursing homes: a randomized clinical trial. *JAMA.* 2016. doi:10.1001/jama.2016.16141. Release: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/670868 (full author list and pages not seen; the script credits only Juthani-Mehta and Yale).
- **Design:** randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; **185 women** in 21 Connecticut nursing homes, mean age 86; capsules with 36 mg PAC, two capsules daily (72 mg PAC per day; about 20 oz of juice according to the release) vs placebo for up to 360 days; 147 completed; adherence 80%.
- **Results:** bacteriuria plus pyuria **29.1% vs 29.0%** (no significant difference after adjustment); symptomatic UTIs 10 (cranberry) vs 12 (placebo); deaths 17 vs 16; no difference in hospitalizations or antibiotic use.
- **Limitations:** one capsule product; enrolled women with and without baseline bacteriuria plus pyuria; the primary outcome was bacteriuria plus pyuria, not symptomatic UTI; small number of symptomatic episodes; nursing home residents only.

## 4. NIH NCCIH page (agency source)
- **Source:** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health. Cranberry. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cranberry (last updated November 2024).
- States: "in general, cranberry products may decrease the overall risk of symptomatic, recurrent UTIs in women by 25 percent"; not recommended as a treatment for an existing UTI in any population; people who think they have a UTI should see a provider; generally thought safe by mouth; very large amounts can cause stomach upset and diarrhea; "there is conflicting evidence about whether cranberry interacts with the anticoagulant (blood thinner) warfarin"; talk with a provider before using cranberry if you take warfarin or other medicine.

## 5. Sugar in cranberry juice cocktail
- **Source:** retailer Nutrition Facts panel for Ocean Spray Cranberry Juice Cocktail (64 oz bottle), Stop & Shop: https://stopandshop.com/groceries/product/ocean-spray-cranberry-juice-cocktail-64-oz-btl/3025 . Serving 8 fl oz: 110 calories, 28 g total carbohydrate, **25 g total sugars, 23 g added sugars (46% DV)**. Ingredients: filtered water, cranberry juice (water and concentrate), sugar, ascorbic acid, vegetable concentrate for color. Other retailer pages show the same 25 g.
- "About six teaspoons" is arithmetic (25 g ÷ about 4.2 g per teaspoon). The label page lists no juice percentage, so the script makes no juice-percentage claim.
- Limitation: one brand's label; other products (unsweetened, "light", 100% juice) differ and should be checked on the label.

## Honest verdict
- **Well supported:** In women with recurrent UTIs, cranberry products lower the risk of symptomatic, culture-verified UTI by roughly a quarter (RR 0.74, CI wide, borderline); a similar or larger relative reduction is seen in children and in people prone to infection after bladder procedures. NIH NCCIH says about 25 percent. Cranberry is not a treatment for an existing infection. Side effects are mostly mild stomach upset. Cranberry juice cocktail contains substantial added sugar (about 25 g per 8 oz on one popular label).
- **Uncertain:** whether juice or capsules work better (very low certainty); the best dose of PACs; benefit in older adults living independently; long-term use; whether cranberry matches antibiotics; the strength of the effect given heterogeneity, small trials and industry involvement; whether the warfarin interaction is real at ordinary intakes (conflicting evidence).
- **Not supported / false:** that cranberry juice treats an active UTI; that it helps older adults in care homes, pregnant women or adults with incomplete bladder emptying (little or no benefit in the pooled trials); that it can replace medical care or prescribed antibiotics.
