## 1. The Toronto meta-analysis (main source) — opened (PMC full text)

**Citation.** Ahmed A, Tul-Noor Z, Lee D, Bajwah S, Ahmed Z, Zafar S, Syeda M, Jamil F, Qureshi F, Zia F, Baig R, Ahmed S, Tayyiba M, Ahmad S, Ramdath D, Tsao R, Cui S, Kendall CWC, de Souza RJ, Khan TA, Sievenpiper JL. *Effect of honey on cardiometabolic risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Nutrition Reviews. 2023;81(7):758–774 (online 16 Nov 2022). DOI 10.1093/nutrit/nuac086. PMID 36379223. PMC10251304. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10251304/ · PROSPERO CRD42015023580.
University news summary: https://temertymedicine.utoronto.ca/news/sweet-honey-reduces-cardiometabolic-risks-u-t-study-shows

**Design.** Systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled feeding trials (≥ 7 days), GRADE used for certainty. 18 trials, 33 comparisons, **n = 1,105**. Trial size 8–72 (median 43). Median age 41.2 years (IQR 10.55–60.7); about 90% adults; 54% female. Health status: 42% healthy, 12% overweight/obesity, **21% type 1 or type 2 diabetes**, 10% glucose-intolerant, 15% mixed. Median baseline BMI 26.
**Dose / duration.** Median **40 g/day** (IQR 25–70; range 5–125 g) ≈ 2 tablespoons; median **8 weeks** (range 1–24).
**Comparators.** Usual diet 23 comparisons; sucrose 5; high-fructose corn syrup 2; mixed 3.
**Context stated by authors/news summary.** Trials were in people eating healthy dietary patterns with added sugars ≤ 10% of calories. Honey is about 80% sugar; honey should mainly *replace* other sweeteners rather than be added. Khan: "We're not saying you should start having honey if you currently avoid sugar." Authors call for more studies on floral source and processing. News summary says pasteurization (about 65 °C for 10 min) may reduce honey's benefits (stated in the news summary, not verified in the paper's results).

**Pooled results (random effects, mean difference, 95% CI), oral honey:**

| Outcome | MD (mmol/L) | 95% CI | Certainty | mg/dL (my conversion) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fasting glucose (20 comparisons) | −0.20 | −0.37 to −0.04 | low | **−3.6** (×18.016) |
| Total cholesterol | −0.18 | −0.33 to −0.04 | low | −7.0 (×38.67) |
| LDL-C | −0.16 | −0.30 to −0.02 | low | −6.2 (×38.67) |
| HDL-C | +0.07 | 0.04 to 0.10 | **high** | +2.7 (×38.67) |
| Fasting triglycerides | −0.13 | −0.20 to −0.07 | low | −11.5 (×88.57) |

Other: ALT −9.75 U/L (1 trial, low). IL-6 and TNF-α *increased* (moderate certainty).

**Subgroups (each rests on few trials, all low certainty):**
- Raw honey (5 trials): fasting glucose −1.05 mmol/L (−1.90 to −0.20) = **−18.9 mg/dL**; total cholesterol −0.61 (−1.07 to −0.14); triglycerides −0.27; HDL +0.11.
- Clover honey (2 trials for glucose): fasting glucose −1.82 mmol/L (−2.35 to −1.30) = **−32.8 mg/dL**; TC −0.44; LDL −0.38; TG −0.31.
- Robinia (acacia) honey (1 trial): TC −0.69, LDL −0.52, TG −0.20. (No robinia glucose estimate in the results, although the abstract lists it.)

**Limitations (from the paper):**
- Poor reporting; most trials unclear risk of bias; substantial unexplained heterogeneity.
- **Publication bias suspected for fasting glucose** (Egger P = 0.033); trim-and-fill adjusted effect **−0.10 mmol/L (−0.30 to 0.09) ≈ −1.8 mg/dL, not significant**.
- Removing 6 trials eliminated the LDL-C effect. Authors note the total cholesterol and LDL benefits appear clinically trivial.
- Most subgroup claims rest on 1–5 trials and need confirmation.
- Only 7 of 33 comparisons used sucrose or HFCS as the comparator; most compared honey with usual diet.
- **Conflicts:** T.A. Khan reports research support from the National Honey Board; J.L. Sievenpiper also reports National Honey Board support and many other industry ties. Review funding was government/institutional. Trial funding: 64% agency, 3% industry, 33% not reported.

## 2. Yaghoobi 2008 — original trial — abstract opened (PMC)

Yaghoobi N, Al-Waili N, Ghayour-Mobarhan M, et al. *Natural honey and cardiovascular risk factors; effects on blood glucose, cholesterol, triacylglycerole, CRP, and body weight compared with sucrose.* ScientificWorldJournal. 2008;8:463–469. DOI 10.1100/tsw.2008.64. PMID 18454257. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5848643
- Randomized, 55 overweight/obese adults: **17 sucrose, 38 honey**, 70 g/day for up to 30 days, measured at baseline and day 31. Honey type and ages not given in the abstract.
- Honey group (p < 0.05): body weight −1.3%, body fat −1.1%. In subjects with normal values: fasting glucose −4.2%, triglycerides −11%, HDL +3.3%. In subjects with elevated values: triglycerides −19%.
- The abstract reports changes in the honey group; sucrose-group changes and between-group tests are not in the abstract.
- Limitations: small, short (30 days), unequal groups, abstract only seen.

## 3. Bahrami 2009 — original trial in type 2 diabetes — **secondary** (search summaries + Erejuwa commentary opened)

Bahrami M, Ataie-Jafari A, Hosseini S, Foruzanfar MH, Rahmani M, Pajouhi M. *Effects of natural honey consumption in diabetic patients: an 8-week randomized clinical trial.* Int J Food Sci Nutr. 2009;60(7):618–626. DOI 10.3109/09637480801990389.
- 48 people with type 2 diabetes, 8 weeks, honey group vs control group that took no honey. HbA1c **rose** significantly in the honey group (P < 0.01); fasting glucose not significantly different between groups after adjustment. Weight, total/LDL cholesterol and triglycerides decreased, HDL rose in the honey group.
- Erejuwa OO. *Effect of honey in diabetes mellitus: matters arising.* J Diabetes Metab Disord. 2014;13:23. PMID 24476150 (opened, PMC3909917): calls the HbA1c rise the only reported potential harm to glycemic control; says high doses may have contributed; calls for better long-term RCTs. Also notes the proposed antioxidant explanation and that much mechanism work is in rats.
- Limitation: abstract-level only; script uses just n = 48, 8 weeks, HbA1c rose, the "high doses may have contributed" comment.

## 4. Glycemic index — opened (PMC)

Atkinson FS, Foster-Powell K, Brand-Miller JC. *International tables of glycemic index and glycemic load values: 2008.* Diabetes Care. 2008;31(12):2281–2283. PMID 18835944. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2584181/
- Summary Table 1 (mean ± SEM): **honey 61 ± 3; sucrose 65 ± 4; glucose 103 ± 3; fructose 15 ± 4.**
- University of Sydney GI service (search summary, **secondary**): honeys tested range from GI about 32 to 87; some Australian single-floral honeys are lower (35–53). https://glycemicindex.com/2018/10/food-for-thought-24/
- Limitation: GI is measured on 50 g carbohydrate portions in small groups of healthy volunteers; it says nothing about portion sugar content or long-term control.

## 5. Composition and nutrition

- Typical honey ≈ 38% fructose, 30–31% glucose, ≈ 17% water, ≈ 1% sucrose; varies with nectar source (**secondary**: Royal Society of Chemistry chapter "Sugars in Honey" https://books.rsc.org/books/edited-volume/1813/chapter/2125161/Sugars-in-Honey and reference tables). Script says "roughly" and "typical".
- Table sugar is sucrose, a disaccharide of one glucose and one fructose (textbook chemistry).
- USDA data (via MyFoodData printout of FDC 169640, **secondary**): 1 tablespoon (21 g) = **64 kcal, about 17 g sugars**; ~304 kcal/100 g. https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/ (official page did not load).
- Toronto summary: honey "about 80 percent sugar".

## 6. Added-sugar limits and labels

- AHA scientific statement: Johnson RK, et al. *Dietary sugars intake and cardiovascular health: a scientific statement from the American Heart Association.* Circulation. 2009;120:1011–1020. DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.109.192627. Most women ≤ **100 kcal/day** (about 25 g, 6 teaspoons) and most men ≤ **150 kcal/day** (about 36 g, 9 teaspoons) of added sugars. Seen via **secondary** summaries (Healio, AHA-hosted fact sheet). The guidance applies to sugars *added* to foods; honey you add counts (Mayo Clinic Minute script, secondary). https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20120331/new-aha-statement-adds-upper-limit-to-added-sugar-intake-cites-links-with-cv-risk-factors
- FDA 2019 final guidance: single-ingredient honey need not show the "Includes Xg Added Sugars" grams line but must show the percent Daily Value for added sugars. https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-issues-final-guidance-regarding-declaration-added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label-honey-maple-syrup (search summary, **secondary**; page not opened).
- Mayo Clinic: "Diabetes foods: Can I substitute honey for sugar?" Generally no advantage; both raise blood sugar; honey has slightly more carbohydrate and calories per teaspoon than granulated sugar; count honey's carbohydrate in your plan. https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/diabetes/expert-answers/diabetes/faq-20058487 (search summary).
- Infants: CDC/AAP advise no honey under 12 months (infant botulism). Seen via MSU Extension and UF/IFAS fact sheets (**secondary**). https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/AA142

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Honey is mostly sugar (about 80%), mainly fructose and glucose. A tablespoon is about 64 calories and 17 g of sugar. Sucrose is the same two sugars joined together.
- Honey's glycemic index (about 61) is only slightly below table sugar's (about 65); no health body treats honey as a free pass. Mayo Clinic: generally no advantage for people with diabetes. Honey you add counts toward AHA added-sugar limits.
- A 2022 Toronto meta-analysis found small average improvements (fasting glucose about −4 mg/dL, HDL about +3 mg/dL) in mostly healthy-diet trials of about 8 weeks at about 2 tablespoons a day.

**Uncertain**
- Almost all outcomes are low certainty. The glucose benefit shrinks to non-significance after adjusting for publication bias. The cholesterol changes are clinically trivial per the authors.
- The large raw-honey and clover-honey effects rest on 2–5 small trials; "raw" vs pasteurized and floral source have not been tested cleanly.
- Median participant age was 41; only one in five had diabetes. Almost nothing is known for adults over 60 or for long-term use. In the one diabetes trial found (n = 48), HbA1c rose.
- Some authors have National Honey Board funding (disclosed).

**False or misleading**
- "Honey is a free pass because it is natural / raw honey doesn't spike blood sugar." It does raise blood sugar; it is sugar.
- "Honey is much lower in calories than sugar" (not established here; a spoon is 64 calories).
- "Raw honey can replace diabetes treatment." No evidence, and nothing here should discourage treatment.
