# Research notes: Does coffee actually dehydrate you?

Slug: `coffee-and-hydration`. Angle: caffeine's mild diuretic effect versus the water in the cup; the controlled trial in habitual drinkers; the beverage hydration index; tolerance; how much caffeine is considered safe.

Access note: PubMed pages returned only a cookie notice, so abstracts were read through Europe PMC records and the agency pages. Only abstract-level details are recorded below. Nothing here comes from full texts.

## 1. Killer, Blannin & Jeukendrup 2014: coffee versus water in habitual drinkers

- **Citation:** Killer SC, Blannin AK, Jeukendrup AE. No evidence of dehydration with moderate daily coffee intake: a counterbalanced cross-over study in a free-living population. PLoS One. 2014;9(1):e84154. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0084154. PMID 24416202. PMCID PMC3886980.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084154 (Europe PMC: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24416202)
- **Design:** randomized, counterbalanced crossover. Two 3-day trials per participant.
- **Population:** n = 50 men, habitual coffee drinkers (usually 3 to 6 cups a day).
- **Intervention:** coffee (4 × 200 mL a day, providing 4 mg caffeine per kg body weight) or water. Activity, food and fluid intake were controlled.
- **Measures:** total body water (deuterium oxide), daily urine and blood hydration markers, nude body mass.
- **Results:**
  - Total body water: 51.5 ± 1.4 kg with coffee, 51.4 ± 1.3 kg with water. No meaningful change in either trial and no difference between them.
  - No blood markers differed between trials.
  - 24-hour urine volume: 2409 ± 660 mL with coffee, 2428 ± 669 mL with water. Urine specific gravity, osmolality and creatinine were similar.
  - Urinary sodium excretion was higher with coffee (p = 0.02).
  - Body mass did not differ between conditions. Both trials showed a small decline of 0.4 ± 0.5 kg (p < 0.05).
- **Authors' conclusion:** in moderation, coffee provides hydration similar to water in caffeine-habituated men.
- **Limitations:** men only. Habitual drinkers only. 3 days. Measured moderate dose. The abstract does not say whether the water volume matched the coffee volume, so the script says only "they drank water". Cause: randomized, so a causal comparison.

## 2. Maughan et al. 2016: beverage hydration index

- **Citation:** Maughan RJ, Watson P, Cordery PA, Walsh NP, Oliver SJ, Dolci A, Rodriguez-Sanchez N, Galloway SD. A randomized trial to assess the potential of different beverages to affect hydration status: development of a beverage hydration index. Am J Clin Nutr. 2016;103(3):717-723. doi:10.3945/ajcn.115.114769. PMID 26702122.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.114769
- **Design:** randomized crossover. Fasted, euhydrated men drank 1 L of a test drink over 30 minutes. All urine was collected for 4 hours. BHI = water retained at 2 h relative to still water (corrected for the drink's water content).
- **Population:** n = 72 men (as read from the Europe PMC record).
- **Results (abstract):**
  - Cumulative 4-hour urine mass: still water 1337 ± 330 g. Oral rehydration solution 1038 ± 333 g (P < 0.001). Full-fat milk 1052 ± 267 g (P < 0.001). Skimmed milk 1049 ± 334 g (P < 0.001).
  - Not different from water at 4 h: cola, diet cola, hot tea, iced tea, coffee, lager, orange juice, sparkling water, sports drink.
  - BHI at 2 h: ORS 1.54 ± 0.74, full-fat milk 1.50 ± 0.58, skimmed milk 1.58 ± 0.60.
  - The abstract gives no numeric BHI for coffee, so none is used in the script.
- **Limitations:** one liter, one sitting, fasted men, short term. Shows that coffee did not differ from water. It does not show that coffee is better than water.
- **Note on alcohol:** lager also appeared in the "no difference" list. The script does not mention it, to avoid implying anything about alcohol.

## 3. Armstrong et al. 2005: 11 days of controlled caffeine

- **Citation:** Armstrong LE, Pumerantz AC, Roti MW, Judelson DA, Watson G, Dias JC, Sokmen B, Casa DJ, Maresh CM, Lieberman H, Kellogg M. Fluid, electrolyte, and renal indices of hydration during 11 days of controlled caffeine consumption. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab. 2005;15(3):252-265. doi:10.1123/ijsnem.15.3.252. PMID 16131696.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.15.3.252
- **Design:** parallel groups (three dose groups; blinding is not stated in the abstract). Days 1-6 equilibration at 3 mg/kg/day caffeine in capsules. Days 7-11 treatment at 0 (C0, n = 20), 3 (C3, n = 20) or 6 (C6, n = 19) mg/kg/day, with no other dietary caffeine.
- **Population:** 59 healthy men (the sum of the group sizes in the abstract), mean age 21.6 ± 3.3 years.
- **Results:** 24-hour urine volume, urine osmolality, specific gravity and color were not affected by dose (P > 0.05) on days 1, 3, 6, 9 and 11. Body mass, 24-hour sodium and potassium excretion, creatinine, BUN, serum sodium, potassium and osmolality, hematocrit and total plasma protein were also unaffected. No evidence of hypohydration in any group. The abstract gives no numeric urine volumes.
- **Authors' conclusion:** the findings question the widely accepted notion that caffeine consumption acts chronically as a diuretic.
- **Limitations:** young men only. Caffeine given in capsules, not coffee. Short (11 days).

## 4. Maughan & Griffin 2003: review

- **Citation:** Maughan RJ, Griffin J. Caffeine ingestion and fluid balance: a review. J Hum Nutr Diet. 2003;16(6):411-420. doi:10.1046/j.1365-277x.2003.00477.x. PMID 19774754.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-277x.2003.00477.x
- **Design:** narrative review of Medline literature, January 1966 to March 2002.
- **Findings (abstract):**
  - Large acute doses of at least 250-300 mg caffeine (about 2-3 cups of coffee or 5-8 cups of tea) briefly increased urine output in people who had abstained from caffeine for days or weeks.
  - Tolerance develops. Regular consumers show much smaller effects.
  - Doses equal to standard servings of tea, coffee or soft drinks appeared to have no diuretic action.
  - Conclusion: regular caffeinated drinks do not cause fluid loss beyond the volume ingested or poor hydration.
- **Limitations:** narrative review, older studies, mostly small.

## 5. FDA consumer update

- **Citation:** U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Spilling the Beans: How Much Caffeine Is Too Much? Content current as of 28 Aug 2024.
- **URL:** https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much
- **Key points:**
  - "400 milligrams a day, about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee", cited as an amount not generally associated with negative effects, for most healthy adults.
  - Regular brewed non-specialty coffee: 113 to 247 mg per 12 fl oz. Decaf: 2 to 15 mg per 8 fl oz.
  - Sensitivity and elimination rate vary widely between people.
  - If pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding, ask your provider whether to limit caffeine. Some conditions and medications raise sensitivity.

## 6. EFSA caffeine opinion (2015)

- **Citation:** European Food Safety Authority. Scientific opinion on the safety of caffeine (2015), via the EFSA caffeine topic page.
- **URL:** https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine
- **Key points (from the topic page):**
  - Single doses up to 200 mg (about 3 mg/kg) from all sources: no safety concern for healthy adults.
  - A 100 mg single dose may affect sleep in some adults, especially near bedtime.
  - Daily intakes up to 400 mg (about 5.7 mg/kg) spread through the day: no concern for healthy adults except pregnant women.
  - Pregnant or lactating women: up to 200 mg a day from all sources, no concern for the foetus.
  - Children and adolescents: 3 mg/kg a day considered safe for habitual consumption.
- **Limitation:** only the topic page was read, not the full opinion. The opinion's article number is not cited.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Caffeine has a real, mild diuretic effect. It shows up mainly after large single doses (about 250-300 mg) in people who do not normally consume caffeine (Maughan & Griffin 2003).
- In habitual coffee drinkers, moderate coffee intake gave about the same total body water and 24-hour urine volume as water over 3 days (Killer 2014, n = 50, randomized crossover).
- Regular intake builds tolerance. Controlled daily doses of 3 or 6 mg/kg did not change urine volume or hydration markers over 5 days (Armstrong 2005, 59 men).
- In a one-liter test, coffee's 4-hour urine output did not differ from water's (Maughan 2016, n = 72).
- About 400 mg of caffeine a day is the figure both FDA and EFSA treat as not a concern for most healthy adults. EFSA's pregnancy figure is 200 mg.

**Uncertain**
- Whether the results hold in women, in older adults and over months or years. All three trials enrolled only men, and the Armstrong participants were young.
- Whether very large intakes or energy drinks behave the same way.
- Individual sensitivity.

**False or unsupported**
- "Coffee dehydrates you" or "coffee doesn't count toward your fluids". Not supported for habitual drinkers at moderate intake.
- "Coffee hydrates better than water". Also unsupported. The evidence says only that it does about as well.

## Claims used in the script that are general knowledge and not from a recorded source
- Kidneys regulate how much water is kept or excreted as urine (basic physiology; describes what a diuretic is).
- Persistent thirst, very dark urine or dizziness can have causes other than coffee and are worth mentioning to a doctor (general safety advice, no figure).
