# Research notes: Should you cut out spinach and dairy if you get kidney stones?

Slug: `kidney-stones-oxalate-calcium`. Written for US viewers, mostly 60 and older.

How the sources were read: PubMed and PMC pages were blocked for the fetch tool, so abstracts were read through the Europe PMC record for each paper. Where a figure is only from a search-result summary of the abstract, it is marked **(secondary)**. The full text of the 2026 AUA guideline is paywalled, so its statements are taken from the AUA/Guideline Central/Urology Times summaries and flagged as such.

---

## 1. Borghi 2002: low-calcium diet vs normal-calcium diet (RCT)

- **Citation:** Borghi L, Schianchi T, Meschi T, Guerra A, Allegri F, Maggiore U, Novarini A. Comparison of two diets for the prevention of recurrent stones in idiopathic hypercalciuria. *N Engl J Med.* 2002;346(2):77-84. DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa010369. PMID 11784873. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11784873/
- **Design:** randomized, 5 years. **n = 120 men** with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and idiopathic hypercalciuria (urine calcium above 300 mg/day on an unrestricted diet).
- **Arms (60 each):**
  - Normal-calcium diet: 30 mmol/day calcium (about 1,200 mg; 30 x 40.08 = 1,202 mg, my conversion), **plus** reduced animal protein (52 g/day) and reduced salt (50 mmol NaCl/day).
  - Traditional low-calcium diet: 10 mmol/day calcium (about 400 mg; 10 x 40.08 = 401 mg, my conversion).
- **Results:** stone relapse at 5 years in **12 of 60** (normal-calcium diet) vs **23 of 60** (low-calcium diet). Relative risk **0.49 (95% CI 0.24 to 0.98; P = 0.04)**. Percentages computed from counts: 20% vs 38%. Urinary calcium fell about 170 mg/day in both groups; urinary oxalate **rose** in the low-calcium group and **fell** in the normal-calcium group.
- **Limitations:**
  - The comparison is of two whole diets. The winning diet also cut animal protein and salt, so the trial cannot say how much of the benefit is from calcium itself.
  - Men only; an accompanying editorial (Bushinsky) noted it is unknown whether results hold for women **(secondary)**.
  - Men with hypercalciuria and calcium oxalate stones only; borderline P value; no blinding possible with diet.
  - Mean age is not stated in the abstract, so do not claim anything about age.

## 2. Curhan 1993: dietary calcium and stones in men (cohort)

- **Citation:** Curhan GC, Willett WC, Rimm EB, Stampfer MJ. A prospective study of dietary calcium and other nutrients and the risk of symptomatic kidney stones. *N Engl J Med.* 1993;328(12):833-838. DOI 10.1056/NEJM199303253281203. PMID 8441427. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8441427/
- **Design:** prospective cohort, **n = 45,619 men aged 40 to 75**, no prior stone; food-frequency questionnaire in 1986; **505 stone cases** over 4 years.
- **Results (highest vs lowest fifth):** calcium RR 0.56 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.73) age-adjusted; **RR 0.66 (0.49 to 0.90)** after further adjustment (alcohol, animal protein, potassium, fluid). Animal protein **RR 1.33 (1.00 to 1.77)**. Potassium RR 0.49 (0.35 to 0.68). Fluid RR 0.71 (0.52 to 0.97).
- **Limitations:** observational, self-reported diet, mostly health professionals; association not proof; animal-protein result is borderline (CI touches 1.00).

## 3. Curhan 1997: dietary vs supplemental calcium in women (cohort)

- **Citation:** Curhan GC, Willett WC, Speizer FE, Spiegelman D, Stampfer MJ. Comparison of dietary calcium with supplemental calcium and other nutrients as factors affecting the risk for kidney stones in women. *Ann Intern Med.* 1997;126(7):497-504. DOI 10.7326/0003-4819-126-7-199704010-00001. PMID 9092314. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9092314/
- **Design:** prospective cohort, **n = 91,731 women** (Nurses' Health Study I), aged 34 to 59 in 1980, no prior stones; 12 years, 903,849 person-years; **864 cases**.
- **Results (highest vs lowest fifth unless noted):**
  - Dietary calcium **RR 0.65 (0.50 to 0.83)**
  - Supplemental calcium users vs non-users **RR 1.20 (1.02 to 1.41)**
  - Sucrose RR 1.52 (1.18 to 1.96)
  - Sodium RR 1.30 (1.05 to 1.62)
  - Fluid RR 0.61 (0.48 to 0.78)
  - Potassium RR 0.65 (0.51 to 0.84)
  - In **67% of supplement users** the calcium was taken without a meal or with meals probably low in oxalate. Authors suggest timing relative to oxalate may explain the food vs pill difference (other dairy components could also contribute).
- **Limitations:** observational; women only, mostly nurses; ages at start 34 to 59 (few over 65 at baseline); supplement-timing explanation is a hypothesis, not tested here.

## 4. Taylor and Curhan 2007: oxalate intake and stones (cohorts)

- **Citation:** Taylor EN, Curhan GC. Oxalate intake and the risk for nephrolithiasis. *J Am Soc Nephrol.* 2007;18(7):2198-2204. DOI 10.1681/ASN.2007020219. PMID 17538185. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17538185/
- **Design:** three prospective cohorts: **45,985 men** (HPFS), **92,872 older women** (NHS I), **101,824 younger women** (NHS II); food questionnaires every 4 years; **4,605 incident stones**. Mean oxalate intake 214 mg/day (men), 185 (older women), 183 (younger women).
- **Results:** spinach supplied **more than 40%** of dietary oxalate. Highest vs lowest fifth of oxalate: RR **1.22 (1.03 to 1.45)** in men, **1.21 (1.01 to 1.44)** in older women; **no association in younger women**. **Spinach 8 or more servings per month vs less than 1:** RR **1.30 (1.08 to 1.58)** men, **1.34 (1.10 to 1.64)** older women (not in younger women). Men with lower dietary calcium had higher risk (P for interaction = 0.08, not firm).
- **Authors' conclusion:** dietary oxalate is **not a major risk factor** for kidney stones.
- **Limitations:** observational; food-questionnaire oxalate values are imprecise; modest relative risks; mostly health professionals.

## 5. Liebman and Chai 1997: calcium reduces oxalate absorption (human tracer study)

- **Citation:** Liebman M, Chai W. Effect of dietary calcium on urinary oxalate excretion after oxalate loads. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 1997;65(5):1453-1459. DOI 10.1093/ajcn/65.5.1453. PMID 9129476. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9129476/
- **Design:** oxalate-load tests using a labeled oxalate tracer to separate dietary from body-made oxalate; 24-hour urine. **Number of participants is not given in the abstract.** Each load had 180 mg unlabeled + 18 mg labeled oxalate (about 198 mg).
- **Results:** replication 1, oxalate absorption **18.3%** alone, **8.1%** with 300 mg calcium (carbonate), **7.2%** with 300 mg calcium (citrate malate); exogenous urinary oxalate 36.2 mg to 16.1 and 14.3 mg. Replication 2: 11.3% alone, 9.1% (100 mg Ca), 5.9% (200 mg), 7.6% (300 mg); 200 mg and 300 mg were significantly lower than 100 mg or none. A meal with the load lowered absorption when no calcium was given, but not when 300 mg calcium was given.
- **Limitations:** small, short, single-dose laboratory study; not a stone outcome; did not test calcium timing; population details not in the abstract.

## 6. Fluids: Borghi 1996 RCT, PUSH 2026 RCT, ACP 2014, AUA 2026

- **Borghi 1996 (secondary):** Borghi L, Meschi T, Amato F, Briganti A, Novarini A, Giannini A. Urinary volume, water and recurrences in idiopathic calcium nephrolithiasis: a 5-year randomized prospective study. *J Urol.* 1996;155(3):839-843. Randomized 199 stone formers after a first idiopathic calcium stone. Recurrence within 5 years: **12 of 99** (high water intake) vs **27 of 100** (usual care), P = 0.008; time to recurrence 38.7 vs 25.1 months. Seen only as abstract text in search summaries (Cochrane lists group 1 as n = 110, so group sizes differ between sources). Script uses the abstract's 12/99 vs 27/100 and says only "drink more water".
- **PUSH trial 2026:** Desai AC, Maalouf NM, Harper JD, et al. (Urinary Stone Disease Network). Prevention of urinary stones with hydration: a randomised clinical trial of an adherence intervention. *Lancet.* 2026;407(10534):1171-1181. DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(25)02637-6. PMID 41864748. n = 1,658 (826 intervention, 832 control), people with stones and low 24-hour urine volume; median age **44**; 57% female; median follow-up 738 days. Symptomatic stone events **154 (19%) vs 165 (20%)**, HR **0.96 (0.77 to 1.20)**. Urine volume was higher in the intervention group. Asymptomatic hyponatremia 12 (1%) vs 2 (under 1%). Authors: did not reduce recurrent stone events but modestly increased urine volume. Limits: adherence-support intervention, not a pure water vs no water test; younger population.
- **ACP 2014 guideline:** Qaseem A, Dallas P, Forciea MA, Starkey M, Denberg TD; Clinical Guidelines Committee of the ACP. Dietary and pharmacologic management to prevent recurrent nephrolithiasis in adults. *Ann Intern Med.* 2014;161(9):659-667. DOI 10.7326/M13-2908. PMID 25364887. Recommends increasing fluids spread through the day to reach at least **2 L of urine per day** (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence).
- **AUA 2026 guideline (Part I):** Pearle MS, Matlaga BR, Antonelli JA, et al. Medical management of kidney stones: AUA guideline (2026) Part I. *J Urol.* 2026;216(5):613-622. DOI 10.1097/JU.0000000000005227. https://doi.org/10.1097/JU.0000000000005227 . It replaces the 2014 guideline (39 recommendations in total). Statements as reported by summaries (Guideline Central; AUA press page; Urology Times; Renal & Urology News; full text not accessible):
  - Adults with a history of calcium stones "should be encouraged" to reach a urine volume of **2.5 L (85 oz)** a day, mainly from water. AUA commentary notes intake must modestly exceed the urine target because not all fluid is excreted as urine (version of that page not confirmed).
  - Adults with recurrent calcium stones "may be offered" directed dietary advice based on their current diet and metabolic testing.
  - Adults with kidney stones "may be recommended" to limit soft drink consumption.
  - People with higher urinary calcium and excess salt intake should be advised to limit salt; adults on thiazides should limit sodium and keep calcium intake normal.
  - Evidence for lowering animal protein is limited (one RCT).
  - Fruit and vegetable emphasis for calcium stones; plant-forward pattern for uric acid stones; higher fluid for cystine and struvite stones (Urology Times).
  - **Not verified:** the guideline's wording on citrus/lemon and on oxalate restriction. The script does not attribute any citrus statement to the guideline.

## 7. Lemonade and urinary citrate (weak evidence)

- Retrospective study, *Urology* 2007 (PMID 17919696): lemonade therapy alone (n = 63) vs potassium citrate plus lemonade (n = 37) in recurrent calcium oxalate stone formers; urine citrate rose by up to 203 and 346 mg/day, urine volume by 763 and 860 mL/day. Not randomized, no stone outcomes.
- Small crossover trial (13 participants, 2 L/day of regular vs diet lemonade for 5 days): diet lemonade raised urine citrate (777 vs 610 mg/day). Abstract only, via search summary **(secondary)**.
- Takeaway: small, short, surrogate-marker studies. Say "small studies" and "a maybe", no stone-recurrence claim.

---

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Blanket calcium restriction is not a good idea for most people who form calcium stones. In one 5-year trial in 120 men, relapses were 12/60 on normal calcium (with less salt and animal protein) vs 23/60 on low calcium. Large cohorts link higher food calcium with fewer stones (RR about 0.65 to 0.66).
- Calcium in a meal lowers the oxalate absorbed from that meal (Liebman and Chai); this is the plausible reason low-calcium diets backfired (urine oxalate rose).
- Drinking more fluid lowers recurrence in older trials, and guidelines (ACP 2 L urine; AUA 2026 2.5 L urine, mostly water) recommend it.
- Higher sodium (RR 1.30 in women) and sugar/soft drinks (RR 1.52 for sucrose; AUA says may limit soft drinks) go with more stones in cohorts.

**Uncertain**
- How much of the 2002 trial's benefit came from calcium vs salt vs animal protein (confounded design; men only).
- Whether calcium pills raise risk because of timing relative to meals; Curhan 1997 only suggests it.
- Spinach: linked with about 30% higher risk at 8 or more servings a month in men and older women, but the authors concluded oxalate is not a major risk factor, and effects were absent in younger women. Whether eating spinach together with a calcium food removes the risk has not been tested for stone outcomes.
- Animal protein (borderline RR 1.33; guideline calls evidence limited).
- Lemon/citrus: only small surrogate-marker studies; could not verify the guideline's citrus wording.
- Adherence-based hydration programs may not reduce stones (PUSH 2026, HR 0.96), although urine volume rose. PUSH enrolled a younger population (median 44).
- Almost no data specific to adults over 65.

**False or misleading**
- "If you get kidney stones, cut out all dairy and spinach." Cutting calcium did worse in the trial; spinach only has a modest association, and pairing with calcium is a reasonable approach.
- "Oxalate is the main cause of stones, so avoid every oxalate food." The authors of the largest oxalate study concluded oxalate intake is not a major risk factor.
- "Calcium supplements are as protective as calcium from food." In women, supplement users had about 20% higher risk.

**Safety to keep in the script:** ask your doctor or pharmacist about calcium supplements and any medicine (thiazide water pills); heart or kidney failure and fluid limits; advanced kidney disease follows the kidney team's plan; the best diet depends on stone type and testing. Do not tell anyone to stop or change medicine.
