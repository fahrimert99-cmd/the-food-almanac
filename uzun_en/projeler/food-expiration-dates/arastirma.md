# Research notes: What do "best by" and "use by" dates on food really mean?

Slug: `food-expiration-dates` · Audience: US adults, mostly 60+ · Notes written 10 November 2026.

Pages I actually opened are marked **[opened]**. Pages that blocked me (HTTP 403/404) are marked **[blocked]** and are NOT relied on for any number or quote in the script. Where a claim comes only from a search-result excerpt, it is marked **[search excerpt only]** and is not used in the script.

## 1. What federal rules require (and do not require)

**Claim 1.1: Except for infant formula, product dating is not required by federal regulation.**
- Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS), "Food Product Dating" notice of updated fact sheet, *Federal Register* 84 FR 14083, 9 April 2019. **[opened]** https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2019-04-09/html/2019-06988.htm
- Exact wording used: "except for infant formula, product dating is not required by Federal regulation." The notice cites 21 CFR 107.20(c) for infant formula.
- Type: agency notice (regulatory text and agency response to comments). Not a study.
- Limits: this is the federal picture only. The notice does not describe state rules, and I did not verify them, so the script says "no federal rule" and never says "no rule anywhere".

**Claim 1.2: "Best If Used By" is the phrase FSIS recommends because it is understood as quality, not safety.**
- Same source. FSIS says research shows it is "easily understood by consumers as an indicator of quality rather than safety." About 70 percent of adults surveyed understood it as a quality indicator (survey cited in the notice; the notice excerpt I read does not give the sample).
- Limits: the 70 percent figure is the agency's summary of a survey it cites; I did not see the original survey.

**Claim 1.3: "Sell By" and similar phrases are voluntary quality dates.**
- Same source: manufacturers use phrases such as "Sell By" "on a voluntary basis to describe quality dates."
- The notice does not define "Sell By". I do not state who the date is "for".

**Claim 1.4: "Freeze By" was added to the FSIS fact sheet to explain what it means; some meat and poultry carry "Use or Freeze By" labels.**
- Same source. A trade association said such labels "give consumers clear direction and offer an alternative to disposal." (Trade-association comment quoted in the notice, so it is an industry view, not an FSIS finding.)

**Claim 1.5: "Use By" is not defined by FSIS. For certain refrigerated ready-to-eat products FSIS "recommends but does not require 'Use By' dating on labels."**
- Same source. A commenter asked that "Use By" be defined as "do not eat after this date even without spoilage." FSIS did not adopt that definition.

**Claim 1.6: Food safety after production "largely depends on the environmental, storage, and distribution conditions of the food." Food that is "otherwise wholesome and safe" should not be discarded only because it is past the printed date.**
- Same source (agency statements). 

**Claim 1.7: A "use by" date cannot guarantee safety.**
- Newsome R, Balestrini CG, Baum MD, Corby J, Fisher W, Goodburn K, Labuza TP, Prince G, Thesmar HS, Yiannas F. Applications and perceptions of date labeling of food. *Comprehensive Reviews in Food Science and Food Safety*. 2014;13(4):745–769. DOI 10.1111/1541-4337.12086. **[opened: abstract page]** https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/applications-and-perceptions-of-date-labeling-of-food/
- Finding: terms are inconsistent, so businesses and consumers misread them, causing avoidable waste. The abstract states that a "use by" or similar date "cannot be relied on to indicate or guarantee food safety" because temperature cannot be perfectly controlled across the supply chain.
- Design: expert review by an IFT-convened working group (academia, industry, regulators). Not a trial. Some authors work in industry.

**Not used:** the FSIS "Food Product Dating" web page and PDF returned 403 to me. A search excerpt said FSIS states that infant formula carries a "Use-By" date and that foods without signs of spoilage may be eaten past a "Best if Used By" date. I did not open it, so the script says only "a date" for infant formula and relies on the Federal Register wording ("otherwise wholesome and safe").

## 2. How confused are Americans, and how much do they throw out?

**Claim 2.1: 2016 national survey.**
- Neff RA, Spiker M, Rice C, Schklair A, Greenberg S, Leib EB. Misunderstood food date labels and reported food discards: a survey of U.S. consumer attitudes and behaviors. *Waste Management*. 2019;86:123–132. DOI 10.1016/j.wasman.2019.01.023, PMID 30770169. **[opened via Europe PMC record]** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30770169/
- Design: cross-sectional online survey, 1,029 US adults, nationally representative sample, April 2016.
- Figures: 84 percent discard food near its package date at least occasionally. "Best if used by" was most often seen as signaling quality; "expires on" and "use by" were most often seen as signaling safety. More than one-third wrongly believed date labeling is federally regulated, and another 26 percent were unsure. Adults aged 18–34 and people who misunderstood the labels discarded food based on dates significantly more often.
- Limits: self-reported behavior, online panel. Cross-sectional, so association, not cause.

**Claim 2.2: 2025 national survey (trend vs. 2016).**
- ReFED article summarizing the 2025 national survey on consumer perceptions of food date labels. **[opened]** https://refed.org/articles/confusion-over-food-date-labels-has-grown-according-to-new-national-survey (the policy-brief PDF could not be read as text).
- Design: online survey of 2,069 US adults aged 18+, fielded by The Harris Poll on behalf of Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, 10–14 January 2025; margin of error ±2.5 percentage points. Compared with the 2016 survey (ORC International, 1,029 adults; some wording differed, so comparisons are approximate).
- Figures: always or usually discard food near or past the date: 43 percent (2025) vs 37 percent (2016). Believe the federal government regulates date label phrases: 44 percent (2025) vs 36 percent (2016); in fact only infant formula date labels are federally regulated. On average, 87 percent said they knew the meaning of eight labels, but only 53 percent answered correctly; across labels, 39 percent thought they knew the meaning but were wrong.
- Limits: self-report; ReFED is an advocacy and research organization that publishes the summary; the 2016-to-2025 changes may be partly due to question wording.

**Claim 2.3: Waste estimate.**
- ReFED estimates that label confusion alone leads US consumers to discard about 3 billion pounds of food a year, worth about 7 billion dollars (same ReFED page **[opened]**).
- Limits: the page does not explain how the number was calculated. Other, older estimates differ (search excerpts mentioned 3.5 and 4 million tons), so the script gives it as "ReFED estimates" with the caveat that the method is not shown.

## 3. Listeria: where dates are not the whole story

**Claim 3.1: Who is at higher risk.**
- CDC, "Listeria: risk factors." **[opened]** https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/risk-factors/index.html
- Higher risk: pregnant women, newborns, people 65 and older, people with a weakened immune system. Others "can get infected but usually don't become seriously ill." For older adults, infection almost always needs hospitalization and 1 in 6 die (CDC figure as stated on that page).

**Claim 3.2: Burden.**
- CDC, "About Listeria." **[opened]** https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/about/index.html — each year about 1,250 people in the US are infected and 172 die.

**Claim 3.3: Surveillance study of risk by age.**
- Silk BJ, et al. Invasive listeriosis in the Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network (FoodNet), 2004–2009: further targeted prevention needed for higher-risk groups. *Clinical Infectious Diseases*. 2012;54(Suppl 5):S396–S404. DOI 10.1093/cid/cis268, PMID 22572660. **[opened via Europe PMC record]** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22572660/
- Design: population-based surveillance, 762 invasive cases, 2004–2009. Overall incidence 0.25 to 0.32 cases per 100,000 per year, not changing significantly. 18 percent of cases were fatal. Incidence in adults 65 and older (cases not associated with pregnancy) was 4–5 times the overall annual rates.
- Limits: surveillance counts diagnosed invasive cases only (undercounts milder illness); data from 2004–2009, so rates today may differ. It does not tell us about date labels. The script credits "Silk and colleagues".

**Claim 3.4: Listeria grows in the refrigerator.**
- FDA, "Listeria (Listeriosis)." **[opened]** https://www.fda.gov/food/foodborne-pathogens/listeria-listeriosis — "can survive and grow even under refrigeration"; unlike most bacteria it "can grow at refrigeration temperatures and freezing will not eliminate or reduce the pathogen." Advice: refrigerator at 40°F (4°C) or below, freezer at 0°F (−18°C). Adults over 65 and people with weakened immune systems are at greater risk of serious complications, including death.

**Claim 3.5: Food advice for higher-risk people.**
- CDC, "Listeria prevention." **[opened]** https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/prevention/index.html
- Avoid unheated deli meat, cold cuts, hot dogs, fermented or dry sausages; if eaten, heat to 165°F or until steaming hot. Avoid premade deli salads such as coleslaw or tuna salad (homemade is a safer choice). Avoid unheated queso fresco-type cheeses whether made with pasteurized or raw milk. Avoid any cheese made with raw (unpasteurized) milk unless heated to 165°F or steaming hot. Safer: hard cheeses made with pasteurized milk (Cheddar, Parmesan, Swiss), and cottage cheese, cream cheese, string cheese, feta or mozzarella made with pasteurized milk.
- Note: this corrects the common idea that "soft cheese" is always the problem. The CDC's concern is raw-milk cheeses and unheated queso fresco-style cheeses.

**Claim 3.6: 2024 deli-meat outbreak.**
- CDC outbreak notice for the 2024 deli-meat Listeria outbreak. **[opened]** https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/outbreaks/delimeats-7-24/index.html (page last updated 31 January 2025; investigation closed).
- Final counts: 61 illnesses, 60 hospitalizations, 10 deaths, 19 states; product: meats sliced at delis. (A news summary I opened gave 59 illnesses, so I use the CDC number.) The CDC page I read did not name a company, so the script does not.
- Limits: an outbreak is one event; it does not show typical risk.

**Not used:** the FDA/USDA 2003 risk assessment (only the introduction opened) and Pouillot et al. 2015, J Food Prot (PMID 25581188; abstract summary only) are background reading. I did not find a source that quantifies how Listeria risk changes with days past a printed date, so the script makes no such claim.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Apart from infant formula, no federal regulation requires a date on food (FSIS, *Federal Register* 2019).
- "Best if used by" is the phrase FSIS recommends, and it is meant as a quality date. "Sell by" is also described as a voluntary quality date. "Use by" is not defined by FSIS.
- Many Americans misread these labels and discard food because of them: 84 percent at least occasionally (2016, n = 1,029); 43 percent always or usually (2025, n = 2,069). 44 percent wrongly think the federal government regulates date phrases.
- Listeria can grow in the refrigerator; adults 65 and older are a higher-risk group; CDC advises heating deli meats and choosing certain cheeses.
- A date cannot guarantee safety, because safety depends on how food was handled and stored (FSIS; Newsome et al. 2014).

**Uncertain**
- How much food is wasted because of date confusion. ReFED's figure (3 billion pounds, 7 billion dollars a year) is an estimate with no method shown in what I read.
- Whether surveys of what people say match what they do.
- Whether a given deli meat or soft food is riskier on day 1 past its date than on day 0. I found no study that answers this, so I make no claim.
- Risk figures are from 2004–2009 surveillance and CDC summaries; they describe populations, not any one person.

**False or misleading**
- "A date on food is regulated and checked by the federal government" (except infant formula). 44 percent of surveyed adults believed it.
- "'Use by' always means a legal deadline for safety." It is not defined by FSIS and is not required except on infant formula.
- "Food becomes unsafe at midnight on its date," and its opposite, "a date means nothing for any food." Neither is supported; safety depends on the food and how it was kept.
- "All soft cheese is dangerous for older adults." The CDC's advice is narrower (raw-milk cheeses, unheated queso fresco-style cheeses).
- Topic brief said "after 60": the sources define the higher-risk group as 65 and older, so the script says 65.
