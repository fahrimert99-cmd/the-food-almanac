# Research notes: Can chamomile tea really help you sleep? (slug: chamomile-sleep)

Research date: 23 November 2026. Sources were opened through search results, Europe PMC / NCCIH page fetches and PubMed
search summaries. PubMed pages themselves blocked direct fetching (cookie wall), so figures marked **(summary)** come from
the abstract as relayed by the search tool and should be re-checked against the abstract if anything seems off.

## 1. Older adults, chamomile extract (the "better sleep quality" trial)

- **Claim:** Chamomile extract improved sleep quality in older adults over 28 days.
- **Figures:** 60 adults aged 60 or older; chamomile extract capsules 200 mg twice a day for 28 days vs capsules of wheat
  flour (placebo). Outcome: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Baseline sleep quality poor and similar in both groups;
  after the intervention sleep quality was significantly better in the chamomile group (P < 0.05). Summary: the authors
  concluded the extract "can improve sleep quality in older people and may be a safe option".
- **Design:** single-blind randomized clinical trial, day care nursing home, Karaj, Iran. Capsule extract, **not tea**.
- **Source:** Adib-Hajbaghery M, Mousavi SN. The effects of chamomile extract on sleep quality among elderly people:
  A clinical trial. Complement Ther Med. 2017;35:109-114. doi:10.1016/j.ctim.2017.09.010. PMID 29154054.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154054/
- **Limitations:** n = 60, one site, single-blind (participants but not assessors blinded), self-reported questionnaire,
  4 weeks, extract not tea. A trade-press summary says total sleep time did not increase; I did not rely on it.

## 2. Postpartum women, chamomile tea (the tea trial)

- **Claim:** Drinking chamomile tea for two weeks improved some sleep and mood scores right afterwards, but the effect was
  gone by four weeks.
- **Figures:** 80 Taiwanese postnatal women with poor sleep quality (Postpartum Sleep Quality Scale score of 16 or more),
  randomized to chamomile tea for 2 weeks or routine postpartum care only. Measures: sleep quality scale, Edinburgh
  Postnatal Depression Scale, Postpartum Fatigue Scale. Immediately after the 2 weeks the tea group had significantly lower
  scores for physical-symptom-related sleep inefficiency and for depression than controls. At the 4-week follow-up the two
  groups were similar, so benefits appeared short-term.
- **Source:** Chang SM, Chen CH. Effects of an intervention with drinking chamomile tea on sleep quality and depression in
  sleep disturbed postnatal women: a randomized controlled trial. J Adv Nurs. 2016;72(2):306-315. doi:10.1111/jan.12836.
  PMID 26483209. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26483209/
- **Limitations:** the control group received **no drink at all** (no placebo tea), so herb and ritual/expectation cannot be
  separated; not blinded; self-rated scales; young postpartum women, not older adults; short follow-up.

## 3. Chronic primary insomnia, chamomile extract (the "no difference" trial)

- **Claim:** In adults with diagnosed chronic insomnia, chamomile extract did not clearly beat placebo.
- **Figures:** 34 adults aged 18 to 65 with DSM-IV primary insomnia for at least 6 months (17 per group); 270 mg chamomile
  extract twice daily vs placebo for 28 days; double-blind, randomized, pilot. No significant between-group differences in
  sleep diary measures (total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, sleep quality,
  awakenings). Moderate effect sizes favoured chamomile for sleep latency, night awakenings and fatigue; a moderate effect
  size favoured placebo for total sleep time. Adverse events: no difference.
- **Source:** Zick SM, Wright BD, Sen A, Arnedt JT. Preliminary examination of the efficacy and safety of a standardized
  chamomile extract for chronic primary insomnia: a randomized placebo-controlled pilot study. BMC Complement Altern Med.
  2011;11:78. doi:10.1186/1472-6882-11-78. PMID 21939549. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198755
- **Limitations (stated by authors):** underpowered pilot; people with mood or anxiety disorders excluded; subjective
  measures only; 4 weeks; no multiplicity correction; improvements could reflect natural course or regression to the mean.

## 4. Meta-analysis 2019

- **Claim:** Pooled trials show better sleep quality but no clear effect on insomnia severity; evidence is limited.
- **Figures (summary):** systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 randomized and quasi-randomized trials of chamomile for
  state anxiety, generalized anxiety disorder, insomnia and sleep quality. Sleep quality improved significantly. For
  insomnia severity only one randomized trial was available and it showed no significant change. Three trials reported only
  mild adverse events. Authors: chamomile "appears to be efficacious and safe for sleep quality and GAD", evidence for
  anxiety and insomnia is little, larger trials needed. (A pooled standardized mean difference of about -0.73 for sleep
  quality was relayed by one search result; I did **not** use that number.)
- **Source:** Hieu TH, Dibas M, Surya Dila KA, et al. Therapeutic efficacy and safety of chamomile for state anxiety,
  generalized anxiety disorder, insomnia, and sleep quality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
  and quasi-randomized trials. Phytother Res. 2019;33(6):1604-1615. doi:10.1002/ptr.6349. PMID 31006899.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31006899/
- **Limitations:** small, heterogeneous trials; many used extract rather than tea; only one insomnia trial.

## 5. Meta-analysis 2024

- **Claim:** Chamomile lowers PSQI scores, mostly via fewer awakenings; no clear effect on duration or efficiency.
- **Figures:** 10 studies, 772 participants. Significant reduction in PSQI score; improvement mainly in awakenings after
  sleep onset / staying asleep; no clear improvement in sleep duration, sleep efficiency or daytime functioning; no adverse
  events reported in included studies; authors call for objective sleep measures. (A weighted mean difference of about
  -1.88 PSQI points with high heterogeneity appeared in a search result only; not used.)
- **Source:** Effects of chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla L.) on sleep: a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical
  trials. Complement Ther Med. 2024;84:103071. doi:10.1016/j.ctim.2024.103071. PMID 39106912.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39106912/ (first author "Kazemi" per search result; author list not verified)
- **Limitations:** high heterogeneity; mostly subjective questionnaires; mix of tea and extract.

## 6. Agency page: NIH NCCIH

- **Claims:** "Very little information" on chamomile for insomnia; one study in a 2019 review found no benefit for this
  sleep disorder. Likely safe orally in amounts commonly found in teas and foods; uncommon side effects include nausea,
  dizziness and allergic reactions including anaphylaxis. People allergic to related plants (ragweed, chrysanthemums,
  marigolds, daisies) are more likely to react. Interactions with some liver-metabolized drugs and with warfarin have been
  reported; might interact with sedatives. Little is known about safety in pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- **Source:** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health. Chamomile. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/chamomile

## 7. Mechanism: apigenin (animal / lab only)

- **Claim:** Apigenin from chamomile binds the central benzodiazepine receptor and was anxiolytic in mice.
- **Figures:** fractionation of an aqueous chamomile flower-head extract; apigenin displaced flunitrazepam (Ki about 4 µM);
  no activity at muscarinic receptors or alpha-1 adrenoceptors; did not change muscimol binding at GABA-A receptors.
  In mice (elevated plus-maze) anxiolytic without obvious sedation or muscle relaxation at classical-benzodiazepine-like
  doses; a tenfold higher dose caused mild sedation (26 percent less ambulatory activity). No anticonvulsant action.
- **Source:** Viola H, Wasowski C, Levi de Stein M, et al. Apigenin, a component of Matricaria recutita flowers, is a central
  benzodiazepine receptors-ligand with anxiolytic effects. Planta Med. 1995;61(3):213-216. doi:10.1055/s-2006-958058.
  https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/all/doi/10.1055/s-2006-958058
- **Limitations:** mice and receptor binding only. A 1997 rat study (PMID 9408191, as surfaced by search; not opened) reportedly
  did not reproduce the anxiolytic effect. I found no human data showing that the apigenin in a cup of tea reaches the
  brain at effective levels, so the video says only that the mechanism is unproven.

## 8. Safety: warfarin case report

- **Claim:** A single patient on warfarin who drank chamomile tea heavily had a very high INR and bleeding.
- **Figures:** 70-year-old woman with a mechanical mitral valve on warfarin; chamomile tea several times a day plus a
  chamomile lotion; INR 3.6 at an earlier emergency visit and 7.9 on hospital arrival; retroperitoneal and rectus sheath
  hematomas; transfused. Authors proposed a coumarin-related synergy; pharmacokinetic interaction not ruled out.
- **Source:** Segal R, Pilote L. Warfarin interaction with Matricaria chamomilla. CMAJ. 2006;174(9):1281-1282.
  https://www.cmaj.ca/content/174/9/1281 (PMC1435958)
- **Limitations:** one case; causation is a hypothesis.

## 9. Safety: ragweed-family cross-reactivity

- **Claim:** Severe allergic reactions to chamomile tea are uncommon but documented, mainly in people sensitized to
  ragweed/mugwort pollen.
- **Figures:** 1989 case report of an 8-year-old with hay fever who had anaphylaxis after a first cup of chamomile tea;
  ELISA-inhibition showed cross-reactivity between chamomile tea extract and pollens of Matricaria chamomilla, giant ragweed
  and mugwort. A 2000 study (PMID 10998021) also described chamomile anaphylaxis and cross-reacting allergens.
- **Source:** Anaphylactic reaction after the ingestion of chamomile tea: a study of cross-reactivity with other composite
  pollens. J Allergy Clin Immunol. 1989. PMID 2674263. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2674263/ (author list not verified,
  so the video does not name authors)
- **Limitations:** case-level evidence.

## 10. Guideline: what to do about persistent insomnia

- **Claim:** For chronic insomnia disorder in adults, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as
  the initial treatment (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence); medicine is considered only if CBT-I alone
  fails (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence).
- **Source:** Qaseem A, Kansagara D, Forciea MA, Cooke M, Denberg TD; Clinical Guidelines Committee of the American College
  of Physicians. Management of chronic insomnia disorder in adults: a clinical practice guideline from the American College
  of Physicians. Ann Intern Med. 2016;165(2):125-133. doi:10.7326/M15-2175. PMID 27136449.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136449/

## Not found / deliberately not claimed

- No trial of chamomile **tea** in older adults was found; the older-adult trial used capsules.
- No source on how many cups of tea equal a capsule dose; the video says none of the trials found answers it.
- No study isolating the "warm-drink ritual". The video says only that the trials were not designed to separate herb from
  ritual (true for the tea trial: its control received no drink).

## Honest verdict

- **Reasonably supported:** in small, short trials, chamomile (tea or extract) improved how people rated their sleep
  quality; pooled analyses agree, with fewer awakenings the clearest signal. Side effects in trials were mild.
- **Uncertain:** size and durability of the effect (the tea benefit in new mothers faded by 4 weeks); whether tea works like
  capsules; whether apigenin explains anything in people; how much is ritual or expectation; effects in older adults drinking tea.
- **Not supported:** chamomile as a treatment for diagnosed chronic insomnia (one small trial found no clear benefit; NIH
  calls the evidence very limited); the idea that it is a mild sleeping pill.
- **Cautions that are real:** allergy in people sensitized to ragweed/daisy-family plants; a documented warfarin case;
  possible additive effects with sedatives; unknown safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
