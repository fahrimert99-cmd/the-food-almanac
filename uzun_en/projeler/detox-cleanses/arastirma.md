# Research notes: Do detox juice cleanses really remove toxins?

Slug: `detox-cleanses`. Angle: what "toxins" means in marketing; how the liver and kidneys actually clear waste; the 2015 critical review of detox diets; real risks for older adults (blood sugar, potassium, oxalate, kidney injury cases); what helps the liver instead.

## 1. Detox diets: the review of the evidence

**Klein AV, Kiat H. Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. 2015;28(6):675-686.** (online 18 Dec 2014)
- PMID 25522674. DOI 10.1111/jhn.12286. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25522674/ (read via Europe PMC record)
- Design: critical (narrative) review of the evidence on detox diets. Asked whether detox diets are necessary, what they involve, whether they work, and whether they are risky.
- Findings (abstract): "very little clinical evidence" supports detox diets. A few clinical studies suggested commercial detox diets increase liver detoxification and reduce persistent organic pollutants, but these had methodological flaws and small samples. Some foods (coriander, nori, olestra) show early signs of detox effects, mostly in animals. The authors knew of no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets in humans. They called for more research on benefits and risks.
- Limitations: a critical review, not a meta-analysis; it is from 2015 and the abstract does not give a count of included studies; no new trials are reviewed here. The "no RCTs" statement is "to the authors' knowledge".

**Obert J, Pearlman M, Obert L, Chapin S. Popular weight loss strategies: a review of four weight loss techniques. Curr Gastroenterol Rep. 2017;19(12):61.**
- PMID 29124370. DOI 10.1007/s11894-017-0603-8. (read via Europe PMC record)
- Review of juicing/detox diets, intermittent fasting, paleo and HIIT. Few studies show long-term weight loss from these regimens. Juice cleansing/detox diets produce weight loss because calories are sharply cut for a short time; weight tends to return once normal eating resumes.
- Limitations: narrative review; weight, not toxins, is the outcome.

**NCCIH (NIH). Detoxes and Cleanses: What You Need to Know.** https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know (page opened)
- Detox programs are "suggested as ways to remove toxins from your body, lose weight, or promote health" and include fasting, juices, specific foods, supplements, herbs, colon cleansing, reducing exposures, saunas. Chelation therapy (recommended by CDC for certain serious metal toxicity cases) is excluded from the discussion.
- Few human studies; some report positive effects on weight, fat loss, insulin resistance, blood pressure, but studies are "of low quality". Cites the 2015 review (no compelling research) and a 2017 review (initial weight loss from low calorie intake; weight often returns). "There are no studies on long-term effects."
- Safety: FDA and FTC have acted against companies over hidden ingredients, false claims to treat serious disease, and unapproved colon-cleansing devices. Colon cleansing risk higher with GI disease, colon surgery, severe hemorrhoids, kidney disease, heart disease. Unpasteurized juices can cause serious illness, especially in children, older adults, people with weakened immune systems. Juices from high-oxalate foods such as leafy greens and beets may be a risk for people prone to kidney stones. People with diabetes should follow their care team's plan and consult before major diet changes. Laxatives can cause diarrhea, dehydration, malabsorption. Large amounts of water or herbal tea with no food for days can cause dangerous electrolyte imbalances. Fasting can cause headaches, fainting, weakness, dehydration.
- Limitation: a consumer fact sheet, not primary research.

## 2. The only juice-cleanse trial found (small, uncontrolled)

**Henning SM, Yang J, Shao P, Lee RP, Huang J, Ly A, Hsu M, Lu QY, Thames G, Heber D, Li Z. Health benefit of vegetable/fruit juice-based diet: Role of microbiome. Sci Rep. 2017;7:2167.**
- PMID 28526852. DOI 10.1038/s41598-017-02200-6. (abstract read via Europe PMC record)
- Design: 20 healthy adults drank only vegetable and fruit juices for 3 days, then resumed their usual diet for 14 days. Single group (no control arm, as described in secondary summaries; the abstract describes one group). Ages not stated in the abstract.
- Results: body weight and BMI dropped significantly on day 4 (p = 2.0E-05), still significant on day 17 (p = 3.0E-04). Stool Firmicutes and Proteobacteria fell, Bacteroidetes and Cyanobacteria rose on day 4; shifts partly reversed by day 17. Plasma nitric oxide +244 ± 89%, urinary nitric oxide +450 ± 360%; urinary malondialdehyde (lipid peroxidation marker) −32 ± 21% on day 4. General well-being score improved by end of study.
- Limitations: n = 20, healthy adults, no comparison group, short. Outcomes were weight, microbiota and some markers; no toxin elimination measured. Authors' own wording: "little scientific evidence" for juice-diet benefits.
- Script uses only: n = 20, 3 days juice, 14 days usual diet, weight/BMI down day 4 and still down day 17, microbiota shift partly reversed, urinary marker of fat oxidation fell, well-being improved by end. The "no comparison group" statement is flagged as from secondary summaries; the script says "had no comparison group" — keep, as the design described is a single group.

## 3. Real harms: case reports and series (humans, causal evidence weak but consistent)

**Getting JE, Gregoire JR, Phul A, Kasten MJ. Oxalate nephropathy due to 'juicing': case report and review. Am J Med. 2013;126(9):768-772.**
- DOI 10.1016/j.amjmed.2013.03.019. (Europe PMC record of PMID 23830537)
- Index patient: acute renal failure from oxalate in juiced oxalate-rich fruits and vegetables. Reviewed 65 Mayo Clinic (Rochester) patients, 1985-2010, with renal failure and biopsy calcium oxalate crystals; a cause identified in all (36 single cause, 29 at least two). 3 patients including index case had presumed diet-induced oxalate nephropathy with chronic kidney disease. Conclusion: heavy consumption of oxalate-rich juices after juicing appears a potential cause of oxalate nephropathy and acute renal failure.
- Limitations: case report plus retrospective single-center series; cannot give incidence.

**Makkapati S, D'Agati VD, Balsam L. "Green Smoothie Cleanse" causing acute oxalate nephropathy. Am J Kidney Dis. 2018;71(2):281-286.**
- DOI 10.1053/j.ajkd.2017.08.002. PMID 29203127. (Europe PMC record)
- One 65-year-old woman, normal kidney function before a green smoothie juice cleanse of oxalate-rich leafy greens and fruits; remote gastric bypass and recent prolonged antibiotics; acute kidney injury progressing to end-stage renal disease. Authors describe oxalate nephropathy as an uncommon cause of AKI.
- Limitation: single case.

**Raja N, Radhakrishnan H, Masilamani S. Oxalate nephropathy: a case report of acute kidney injury due to juice diet. Cureus. 2023;15(12):e51226.**
- DOI 10.7759/cureus.51226. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10821746/ (page opened)
- 85-year-old man, diabetes for 3 years (metformin, glipizide), admitted for upper GI bleed (gastric ulcer). Advised semi-solid diet 48 h after endoscopy; chose a liquid diet for about a week. Drank unsweetened pomegranate juice 3-4 times a day, about 600-900 mL/day, plus occasional sweet lime juice. Estimated about 450 mg vitamin C/day (recommended 90 mg men, 75 mg women). Creatinine rose from 1.12 mg/dL (admission) to peak 5.43 mg/dL on day 11; presented with edema, oliguria; dialysis started. Biopsy: calcium oxalate crystals in about 20-25% of tubules. Lost to follow-up after missing dialysis; authors expected slow recovery or lasting dialysis dependence. Reported risk factors: older age, diabetes, dehydration, underlying CKD.
- Limitation: single case, juice diet after a GI bleed rather than a commercial "cleanse"; the juice was pomegranate.

**Mueller BA, Scott MK, Sowinski KM, Prag KA. Noni juice (Morinda citrifolia): hidden potential for hyperkalemia? Am J Kidney Dis. 2000;35(2):310-312.**
- DOI 10.1016/s0272-6386(00)70342-8. PMID 10676732. (Europe PMC record)
- A man with chronic renal insufficiency taking noni juice had hyperkalemia despite reporting a low-potassium diet. Noni juice samples contained 56.3 mEq/L potassium, similar to orange juice and tomato juice. Authors: herbal/alternative products may be hidden potassium sources in renal disease.
- Limitation: single case; noni juice, not a juice "cleanse"; I found no case report of a cleanse causing potassium disorders in an older adult, so the script does not claim one.

**NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Potassium (consumer fact sheet).** https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-Consumer/ (page returned 403; statements taken from a search-result excerpt: FLAGGED)
- Potassium from food and beverages has not been shown to cause harm in healthy people with normal kidney function; people with chronic kidney disease and those who use certain medications (ACE inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics) can develop abnormally high blood potassium. Talk to your provider.

## 4. Juice and blood sugar

**Muraki I, Imamura F, Manson JE, Hu FB, Willett WC, van Dam RM, Sun Q. Fruit consumption and risk of type 2 diabetes: results from three prospective cohort studies. BMJ. 2013;347:f5001.**
- PMID 23990623. DOI 10.1136/bmj.f5001. (Europe PMC record)
- Nurses' Health Study (66,105 women), NHS II (85,104 women), Health Professionals Follow-up Study (36,173 men): 187,382 US health professionals free of major chronic disease at baseline; 3,464,641 person-years; 12,198 developed type 2 diabetes. Per three additional servings a week: fruit juice pooled HR 1.08 (1.05-1.11); blueberries 0.74 (0.66-0.83); grapes/raisins 0.88 (0.83-0.93); apples/pears 0.93 (0.90-0.96); total whole fruit 0.98 (0.97-0.99).
- Limitations: observational, self-reported diet, health professionals (not specifically older adults), about everyday juice not cleanses; association not cause. Total 187,382 is my sum of the three cohort sizes.
- Mechanism "juice usually has less fiber than whole fruit" is general nutrition knowledge, stated in the script without a number.

I found no trial of blood sugar during a juice cleanse in older adults; the script says nothing more than the above plus NCCIH's advice for people with diabetes.

## 5. How the organs actually clear waste, and what helps the liver

**NIDDK. Your Kidneys & How They Work.** https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/kidneys-how-they-work (page opened)
- "Healthy kidneys filter about a half cup of blood every minute." "In a single day, your kidneys filter about 150 quarts of blood." "Only 1 to 2 quarts become urine." Kidneys remove wastes, extra water and acid; they maintain a balance of water, salts and minerals (potassium, sodium, calcium, phosphorus); without it nerves, muscles and other tissues may not work normally.

**MedlinePlus (National Library of Medicine). Liver Diseases.** https://medlineplus.gov/liverdiseases.html (page opened)
- The liver "helps your body digest food, store energy, and remove poisons."

**NIDDK. Eating, Diet, & Nutrition for NAFLD & NASH** (reviewed April 2021). https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/nafld-nash/eating-diet-nutrition (page opened)
- Eating healthfully and controlling portion sizes may help prevent NAFLD. Doctors may suggest limiting fats, swapping saturated and trans fats for unsaturated fats, and eating more low-glycemic foods such as fruits, vegetables and whole grains. Avoid sugary drinks and juices, especially those with fructose. "You should minimize alcohol use, which can further damage your liver." If overweight or obese, a doctor may recommend "gradually losing weight." (No numeric targets; no mention of detox or supplements.)

**NIDDK. Definition & Facts for NAFLD & NASH.** https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/liver-disease/nafld-nash/definition-facts (page opened)
- "Experts estimate about 24% of U.S. adults have NAFLD and about 1.5% to 6.5% of U.S. adults have NASH."

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Commercial detox diets and juice cleanses have no good evidence for removing toxins; the 2015 critical review found very little clinical evidence and knew of no randomized trials; NCCIH calls the human studies few and low quality, with no long-term studies.
- Short-term weight loss on juice-only plans is explained by sharply fewer calories and tends to come back (2017 review, NCCIH).
- The liver and kidneys already clear waste continuously (MedlinePlus, NIDDK).
- Oxalate-rich juice diets have caused acute kidney injury in documented case reports and a Mayo series, including in older adults, some leading to dialysis or end-stage kidney disease. It is uncommon, and risk factors include older age, diabetes, dehydration, kidney disease, gastric bypass, recent antibiotics.
- People with kidney disease or on ACE inhibitors/potassium-sparing diuretics can develop high potassium (ODS, flagged), and juices can be potassium-rich (noni case).

**Uncertain**
- Henning 2017: 20 healthy adults, 3 days of juice: weight and gut-microbiota changes, but tiny, uncontrolled; no toxin measurement; the microbiota changes partly reversed.
- How often juice cleanses injure the kidneys (only case reports; incidence unknown).
- Effects of juice cleanses on blood sugar in older adults (no trial found); juice-and-diabetes link is observational and about everyday juice.
- Whether any "detox" food has human benefit (animal data mostly).

**False or unsupported**
- That a juice cleanse measurably removes "toxins" or boosts the liver and kidneys beyond what they do anyway.
- That "gentle" or "natural" juice-only plans are risk-free for older adults.
