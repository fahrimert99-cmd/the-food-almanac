# Research notes: cinnamon and blood sugar

Topic: What does cinnamon really do to your blood sugar? (Cassia vs. Ceylon, coumarin, never a substitute for diabetes medication.)
Notes were compiled from abstracts and agency pages opened via PubMed, Europe PMC and the agency sites. Full texts were not read; where only a search summary was seen, it is stated.

## 1. Pooled trials: fasting glucose and A1C

### Allen 2013 (main meta-analysis used in the script)
- **Figures:** 10 RCTs, 543 patients with type 2 diabetes; cinnamon 120 mg/day to 6 g/day for 4 to 18 weeks. Fasting plasma glucose −24.59 mg/dL (95% CI −40.52 to −8.67). HbA1c −0.16 percentage points (95% CI −0.39 to 0.02), not significant. Also lower total cholesterol (−15.60 mg/dL), LDL-C (−9.42 mg/dL), triglycerides (−29.59 mg/dL), higher HDL-C (+1.66 mg/dL). Heterogeneity I² 66.5% to 94.72% (high for all but HDL-C).
- **Design/population:** Systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of RCTs (search through Feb 2012) in adults with type 2 diabetes.
- **Source:** Allen RW, Schwartzman E, Baker WL, Coleman CI, Phung OJ. Cinnamon use in type 2 diabetes: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis. Ann Fam Med. 2013;11(5):452-459. doi:10.1370/afm.1517. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24019277/
- **Limits:** High heterogeneity; authors state optimal dose and duration are unclear, which limits clinical application. Small trials. Cinnamon type often unreported.

### Baker 2008 (earlier, null meta-analysis)
- **Figures:** 5 placebo-controlled trials, n = 282; cinnamon did not significantly change A1C, fasting blood glucose or lipid parameters; subgroup and sensitivity analyses agreed. (Abstract summarized via Europe PMC; included people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.)
- **Source:** Baker WL, Gutierrez-Williams G, White CM, Kluger J, Coleman CI. Effect of cinnamon on glucose control and lipid parameters. Diabetes Care. 2008;31(1):41-43. doi:10.2337/dc07-1711. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17909085/
- **Limits:** Only 5 small trials; superseded by larger pooled analyses.

### Moridpour 2024 (positive 2023 meta-analysis)
- **Figures:** 24 RCTs in type 2 diabetes (search to Dec 2022). Fasting blood sugar SMD −1.32 (95% CI −1.77 to −0.87); HbA1c SMD −0.67 (−1.18 to −0.15, p = 0.011); insulin not significantly changed (SMD −0.17, −0.34 to 0.01). Authors conclude cinnamon "may have potential as an adjunct therapy". Standardized mean differences are not directly convertible to mg/dL, so the script uses no number from this paper, only the direction. (The abstract lists identical values for fasting glucose and HOMA-IR, which looks like a possible transcription error; not used.)
- **Source:** Moridpour AH, Kavyani Z, Khosravi S, et al. Phytother Res. 2024;38(1):117-130 (online 11 Oct 2023). doi:10.1002/ptr.8026. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37818728/
- **Limits:** Abstract only; heterogeneity and certainty of evidence not checked (the PMC umbrella-review page was blocked by a bot check, so it was not used).

### Yu 2023 (null 2023 meta-analysis)
- **Figures:** Dose-response meta-analysis of RCTs. Overall: no significant effect on fasting blood glucose or HbA1c. Subgroup of doses ≤1,200 mg/day showed a hypoglycemic effect (MD −11.1, 95% CI −14.64 to −7.58). TG and LDL-C decreased, HDL-C increased. Abstract gives no trial counts or units for the MD.
- **Source:** Yu T, Lu K, Cao X, et al. Nutrients. 2023;15(13):2983. doi:10.3390/nu15132983, PMID 37447309. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37447309/
- **Limits:** Abstract only. The contrast between Moridpour (positive) and Yu (null overall) is the core example of why trials "disagree".

### Zhou 2022
- **Figures:** 16 RCTs, 1,020 patients with type 2 diabetes, 40 days to 4 months; glycolipid levels improved vs. placebo overall; larger effect in patients with HbA1c around 8%; effect with doses ≤1,200 mg (subgroup). (Seen via Europe PMC search listing summary.)
- **Source:** Zhou Q, et al. Front Physiol. 2022. PMID 36505061. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36505061/
- **Limits:** Summary level only. Used only for the statement that effects were larger when A1C started near 8 percent, with hedged wording ("suggested").

### Costello 2016 (narrative review)
- **Figures:** 11 RCTs, 694 adults with type 2 diabetes; 120 to 6,000 mg/day; 4 to 16 weeks. Studies that measured HbA1c found "very modest" decreases; no meta-analysis because of heterogeneity.
- **Source:** Costello RB, Dwyer JT, Saldanha L, Bailey RL, Merkel J, Wambogo E. J Acad Nutr Diet. 2016;116(11):1794-1802. doi:10.1016/j.jand.2016.07.015, PMID 27618575. (Seen via Europe PMC.)
- **Limits:** Narrative, not quantitative.

## 2. Individual trials

### Khan 2003
- **Figures:** 60 adults with type 2 diabetes randomized to 1, 3 or 6 g cinnamon/day or placebo capsules for 40 days (then 20-day washout). Fasting serum glucose fell 18% to 29% in cinnamon groups; placebo groups showed no significant change. Triglycerides, LDL and total cholesterol fell; HDL change not significant.
- **Source:** Khan A, Safdar M, Ali Khan MM, Khattak KN, Anderson RA. Diabetes Care. 2003;26(12):3215-3218. doi:10.2337/diacare.26.12.3215. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14633804/
- **IMPORTANT:** The Europe PMC record lists an **expression of concern** (Diabetes Care 2025;48(9):1646, doi:10.2337/dc25-ec09). The script says the journal added one and advises caution. The content of the concern was not read.
- **Limits:** Small, short, single trial; cinnamon type not checked.

### Crawford 2009
- **Figures:** RCT, 109 adults with type 2 diabetes and HbA1c above 7.0; cinnamon 1 g/day in capsules plus usual care vs. usual care alone, 90 days. HbA1c fell 0.83 (95% CI 0.46 to 1.20) with cinnamon vs. 0.37 (0.15 to 0.59) with usual care. Difference about 0.46 points (our arithmetic).
- **Source:** Crawford P. J Am Board Fam Med. 2009;22(5):507-512. doi:10.3122/jabfm.2009.05.080093, PMID 19734396.
- **Limits:** Comparison was usual care alone, not placebo (so no blinding is evident from the abstract summary); single trial; type of cinnamon not checked.

### Wainstein 2011
- 59 adults ≥30 with type 2 diabetes, 1,200 mg/day cinnamon vs. placebo, 12 weeks; main outcome systolic BP. J Med Food 2011;14(12):1505-1510, PMID 21861719. Only used to note that trials were not limited to older adults (minimum age 30).

## 3. NIH position (NCCIH)
- "It's unclear whether cinnamon supplementation is helpful for diabetes or weight loss." "Research doesn't clearly support using cinnamon for any health condition." Results hard to interpret partly because species or plant part tested is often unclear.
- Ceylon = "true" cinnamon, mainly Sri Lanka; cassia is the more common type sold in North America; types differ in chemical composition; labels may not say which.
- Coumarin in cassia: interactions with the liver reported; typical food amounts usually not enough to cause major problems but some cassia products contain high coumarin; prolonged use could be an issue for sensitive people such as those with liver disease; Ceylon may contain only a trace.
- Food amounts likely safe; check with a health care provider before using cinnamon if you take any medicine.
- **Source:** NCCIH, Cinnamon. https://nccih.nih.gov/health/cinnamon (page fetched; date of last update not recorded).

## 4. Coumarin: cassia vs. Ceylon
- **AGES (Austria):** coumarin in cassia 1,880 to 3,260 mg/kg (in the cited studies); Ceylon highest measured 185 mg/kg. AGES also describes cassia as containing about a hundred times more than Ceylon (typical-vs-typical). The script uses the more conservative ratio computed from the numbers given: 1,880/185 ≈ 10 and 3,260/185 ≈ 18 (our arithmetic). Tolerable daily intake (TDI) 0.1 mg/kg body weight (BfR and EFSA); 6 mg/day for 60 kg. High intake over a long time can affect the liver in sensitive individuals (raised liver values, up to jaundice, seen with a coumarin drug over weeks; reversible after stopping). https://www.ages.at/en/human/nutrition-food/residues-contaminants-from-a-to-z/coumarin
- **BfR (Germany), press release 27 Sept 2012:** TDI 0.1 mg/kg/day still applies; applies to cinnamon (bioavailability study). A 60 kg adult reaches TDI with about 2 g of cassia with average coumarin per day. Slight exceedance for one to two weeks considered safe; risk is long-term high intake. Recommends frequent heavy users choose low-coumarin Ceylon. "Even relatively small doses [of the drug] can lead to liver damage in sensitive persons." (Search snippet, not the page text: BfR says consumers can hardly tell cassia from Ceylon in powder form.) https://www.bfr.bund.de/en/press-release/cassia-cinnamon-with-high-coumarin-contents-to-be-consumed-in-moderation/
- **Our arithmetic (labelled "our math" in narration):** 1 level teaspoon ground cinnamon ≈ 2.5 g (common kitchen conversion; ConsumerLab also uses about 2.5 g, seen only in a search snippet). Quarter teaspoon ≈ 0.6 g × 1.88–3.26 mg/g = 1.1–2.0 mg coumarin. Full teaspoon 2.5 g × 1.88–3.26 = 4.7–8.2 mg vs. a 6 mg limit for 60 kg. 6 g/day × 1.88–3.26 mg/g = 11–20 mg, about 2 to 3 times the limit. These assume average cassia; individual products vary widely (AGES reported a range up to 4,380 mg/kg in the broader sample).
- **Limits:** Values are European, from a handful of samples; US products not tested in these sources. The limit is per kilogram of body weight, so a heavier person's limit is higher (e.g., 80 kg: 8 mg) and a child's lower.

### Case report
- 34-year-old woman, probable cinnamon-induced mixed hepatocellular-cholestatic liver injury after prolonged cinnamon use for weight loss; cinnamon stopped; liver biochemistry returned to normal. Dose and duration not in abstract. Yesukumar SJ, et al. Cureus 2025;17(9):e93328. doi:10.7759/cureus.93328, PMID 41158894.
- **Limits:** Single case, causality "probable" only; does not show how common this is.

## Honest verdict
- **Reasonably supported:** In several meta-analyses of small trials in type 2 diabetes, cinnamon was associated with lower fasting glucose (about −25 mg/dL in Allen 2013, with a wide interval of −41 to −9). Effects on A1C are smaller and less certain (−0.16 points, not significant, in Allen; positive in Moridpour; null in Yu). Cassia contains far more coumarin than Ceylon; the daily limit for coumarin is about 6 mg for a 60 kg adult, which about 2 g of average cassia reaches.
- **Uncertain:** Whether any glucose effect is clinically meaningful or lasts; the best dose and duration; whether Ceylon works as well as cassia (type is often not reported); whether results apply to people over 60; the 2003 Khan trial, which carries an expression of concern; the Crawford trial's lack of placebo.
- **False or unsupported:** That cinnamon can replace diabetes medication or treat diabetes (NIH: research doesn't clearly support it for any health condition). That "natural" means harmless: high long-term cassia intake can burden the liver in sensitive people. That a label saying "cinnamon" tells you which type you have.
