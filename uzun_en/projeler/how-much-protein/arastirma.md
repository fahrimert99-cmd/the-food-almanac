# Research notes: How much protein do you need after 60?

Prepared for the video `how-much-protein`. Evidence was read through search-result abstracts and records (several PubMed/PMC pages blocked direct fetching with cookie or captcha pages). Where a figure comes only from a secondary summary, it is marked **[secondary]**. Nothing below is cited from memory alone.

## 1. The official number: the US RDA

- **Claim.** The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for protein is **0.8 g per kg of body weight per day** (Estimated Average Requirement 0.66 g/kg/day). It applies to all adults, including those over 65, and was derived from nitrogen-balance studies: the smallest intake that avoids progressive loss of lean body mass in apparently healthy adults.
- **Source.** Institute of Medicine (National Academies). *Dietary Reference Intakes for Energy, Carbohydrate, Fiber, Fat, Fatty Acids, Cholesterol, Protein, and Amino Acids.* National Academies Press, 2005. Figures confirmed via review articles that restate the DRI, e.g. "The Link between Dietary Protein Intake, Skeletal Muscle Function and Health in Older Adults", Healthcare (Basel) 2015, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4939566/ **[secondary]**.
- **Limitations.** The DRI is built for healthy adults and to prevent deficiency, not to optimize muscle in later life; several reviews argue it may be too low for older adults.
- **Unit conversions (our arithmetic).** 0.8 g/kg = 0.36 g/lb. A 170 lb person is 77 kg: RDA about 62 g/day; 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg is about 77 to 93 g/day; 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg = 0.45 to 0.55 g/lb.

## 2. PROT-AGE recommendations (guideline / expert consensus)

- **Figures.** For healthy adults over 65: average intake of **at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day**. Older adults who exercise: **at least 1.2 g/kg/day** or more. Older adults with acute or chronic disease: **1.2 to 1.5 g/kg/day**. Per-meal dose: about **25 to 30 g of high-quality protein** per meal (about 2.5 to 2.8 g leucine) **[secondary: trade-press coverage of the paper]**. Exception: people with **severe kidney disease (eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², not on dialysis)** may need to limit protein **[from search summary of the abstract; exact wording should be checked in the full text]**. The group acknowledged that evidence on protein quality, timing and supplements was still limited.
- **Design.** Position paper of the PROT-AGE Study Group (international group of geriatric/nutrition experts convened with the European Union Geriatric Medicine Society and other societies). Expert consensus based on the literature, not a trial.
- **Source.** Bauer J, Biolo G, Cederholm T, et al. Evidence-based recommendations for optimal dietary protein intake in older people: a position paper from the PROT-AGE Study Group. *J Am Med Dir Assoc.* 2013;14(8):542-559. DOI 10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021. Record: https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/80029
- **Limitations.** Consensus statement; the "1.0 to 1.2" figure is an average minimum aimed at healthy older people, not a proven optimum.

## 3. Observational data: protein intake and lean-mass loss

- **Figures.** Highest quintile of energy-adjusted protein intake lost about **40% less** lean mass over 3 years than the lowest quintile: total lean mass **−0.501 kg vs −0.883 kg**; appendicular lean mass −0.400 vs −0.661 kg (trend P < 0.01). Slightly attenuated after adjusting for fat-mass change but still significant.
- **Design / population.** Prospective cohort, Health ABC study; **n = 2,066** community-dwelling men and women aged **70 to 79**; 108-item food-frequency questionnaire; lean mass by DXA; 3 years.
- **Source.** Houston DK, Nicklas BJ, Ding J, et al. Dietary protein intake is associated with lean mass change in older, community-dwelling adults: the Health, Aging, and Body Composition (Health ABC) Study. *Am J Clin Nutr.* 2008;87(1):150-155. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18175749/
- **Limitations.** Observational (association, not cause); food-frequency questionnaire; residual confounding. A later Health ABC analysis (Verreijen et al., 2019) did not find a protein association with thigh muscle area by CT, so results are not uniform.

## 4. Randomized trials in frail older adults

**4a. Protein + resistance training**
- **Figures.** 24 weeks, supervised progressive resistance training twice a week. Protein group (15 g protein supplement twice daily) vs placebo. Lean body mass rose from about 47.2 to 48.5 kg in the protein group (about **+1.3 kg**) while the placebo group went from about 45.7 to 45.4 kg (treatment × time P = 0.006). **[the +1.3 kg and −0.3 kg are our arithmetic from the reported means; the means were read via a search summary]**. Strength and physical performance improved in both groups with no protein effect.
- **Design.** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; **n = 62** frail older adults, mean age 78.
- **Source.** Tieland M, Dirks ML, van der Zwaluw N, et al. Protein supplementation increases muscle mass gain during prolonged resistance-type exercise training in frail elderly people: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. *J Am Med Dir Assoc.* 2012;13(8):713-719. DOI 10.1016/j.jamda.2012.05.020. https://read.qxmd.com/read/22770932/
- **Limitations.** Small, frail Dutch population; supplement rather than food.

**4b. Protein alone (no exercise program)**
- **Figures.** 15 g protein at breakfast and lunch for 24 weeks vs placebo; Short Physical Performance Battery rose from 8.9 to 10.0 points in the protein group and did not change in placebo (P = 0.02 interaction); skeletal muscle mass did **not** increase.
- **Design.** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; **n = 65** frail older adults.
- **Source.** Tieland M, van de Rest O, Dirks ML, et al. Protein supplementation improves physical performance in frail elderly people: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. *J Am Med Dir Assoc.* 2012;13(8):720-726. DOI 10.1016/j.jamda.2012.07.005. https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/protein-supplementation-improves-physical-performance-in-frail-el/
- **Limitations.** Small; frail adults; supplement.

## 5. Meta-analysis: protein supplements and resistance training

- **Figures.** 49 randomized trials, **1,863** participants, resistance training at least 6 weeks. Protein supplementation added: 1-repetition-maximum strength **+2.49 kg** (95% CI 0.64 to 4.33); fat-free mass **+0.30 kg** (0.09 to 0.52); muscle fiber area +310 µm²; mid-femur area +7.2 mm². The FFM effect **decreased with age** (−0.01 kg per year, p = 0.002). Above about **1.62 g/kg/day** total intake, no further FFM gain.
- **Design.** Systematic review, meta-analysis, meta-regression of RCTs in healthy adults (all ages, not only older).
- **Source.** Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, et al. A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults. *Br J Sports Med.* 2018;52(6):376-384. PMID 28698222. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/ (abstract read via https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/280995/). A correction was published in 2020; not checked.
- **Limitations.** Healthy adults, many younger; supplement trials; the 1.6 g/kg ceiling applies to training gains, not to older adults' minimum needs.

## 6. Spreading protein across meals

**6a. Even vs dinner-heavy**
- **Figures.** 24-hour muscle protein fractional synthesis rate **0.075 vs 0.056 %/h, about 25% higher** with the even pattern (P = 0.003); still different after 7 days (0.077 vs 0.056, P = 0.001). Even diet about 31.5/29.9/32.7 g at breakfast/lunch/dinner; skewed about 10.7/16.0/63.4 g; same daily total (about 90 g).
- **Design.** Randomized crossover feeding study, **n = 8** healthy adults, mean age **36.9** (young/middle-aged), 7-day diets, 30-day washout.
- **Source.** Mamerow MM, Mettler JA, English KL, et al. Dietary protein distribution positively influences 24-h muscle protein synthesis in healthy adults. *J Nutr.* 2014;144(6):876-880. PMC4018950, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4018950 (results read through a search summary of the abstract). Daily total from ScienceDaily coverage https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/05/140520133218.htm **[secondary]**.
- **Limitations.** Eight adults who were not older; short-term metabolic outcome (synthesis rate), not muscle size or strength.

**6b. How much per meal**
- **Figures.** 30 g of protein (113 g of lean beef) raised muscle protein synthesis by roughly **50%** in both young and older adults; 90 g (340 g beef) added nothing further.
- **Design.** Controlled feeding with stable-isotope infusion and biopsies; **n = 17 young (mean 34 y) and 17 older (mean 68 y)**.
- **Source.** Symons TB, Sheffield-Moore M, Wolfe RR, Paddon-Jones D. A moderate serving of high-quality protein maximally stimulates skeletal muscle protein synthesis in young and elderly subjects. *J Am Diet Assoc.* 2009;109(9):1582-1586. PMID 19699838. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3197704/ (details via search summary)
- **Limitations.** Single meal, single food (beef), healthy participants. Note it found a similar response at 30 g in older and young adults, so "anabolic resistance" is mainly argued from other work (higher thresholds at lower doses), as in the PROT-AGE paper.

## 7. Kidney disease exception

- **Figures.** KDIGO 2024 suggests that adults with CKD stages G3 to G5 (not on dialysis) keep protein at about **0.8 g/kg/day** and avoid more than 1.3 g/kg/day; graded as a weak recommendation (2C). Very-low-protein diets only under close supervision. **[secondary: summaries from NephJC and a journal review; the guideline itself was not opened]**.
- **Source.** Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) CKD Work Group. KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease. *Kidney Int.* 2024;105(4S):S117-S314. Summary: https://www.nephjc.com/news/kdigo-ckd-part2
- **How the video uses it.** Only the general statement: higher targets are for people without advanced kidney disease; people with advanced CKD may be told to hold near the standard amount; ask a doctor/dietitian.

## 8. Protein content of common foods (approximate, third-party restating USDA data) **[secondary]**
Large egg about 6 g; 3 oz (85 g) cooked skinless chicken breast about 26 g; 1/2 cup 2% cottage cheese about 12 g; 1 cup cooked lentils about 18 g. Check against USDA FoodData Central (fdc.nal.usda.gov) for exact values.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- The US RDA is 0.8 g/kg/day, built to prevent deficiency in healthy adults.
- Expert groups (PROT-AGE) recommend an average of at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for healthy adults over 65, more with exercise or illness.
- Protein plus resistance training improves muscle gains in older and frail adults more than training alone (Tieland; Morton, with smaller benefit at older ages).
- Roughly 25 to 30 g per meal is a reasonable per-meal target; far more at one meal adds nothing in a single-meal test (Symons).

**Uncertain**
- The exact optimum. 1.0 to 1.2 is a consensus minimum, not a proven ideal; observational links (Houston) cannot prove cause.
- Whether even spreading builds more muscle over months in older adults (the Mamerow study was 8 younger adults, 24-hour measure).
- Whether protein alone, without exercise, builds muscle in older adults (frail-adult trial: function improved, mass did not).
- Details of the kidney thresholds (guideline wording secondary).

**False or unsupported**
- That the RDA of 0.8 is an ideal for older adults, or that older adults are fine on the RDA by default.
- That one huge protein dinner makes up for light breakfast and lunch (not proven, and the single-meal data show a ceiling near 30 g).
- That more is always better (no extra lean-mass gain above about 1.6 g/kg in the meta-analysis; may be harmful in advanced kidney disease).
- That protein alone will rebuild muscle without movement.
