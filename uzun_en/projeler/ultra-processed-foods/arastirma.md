# Research notes: ultra-processed foods

Topic: Are ultra-processed foods really that bad for you? Slug: `ultra-processed-foods`.
Notes compiled 8 November 2026. Where a page could not be opened (PubMed, bmj.com blocked), the bibliographic record and abstract were read through Europe PMC or PMC. Anything seen only in press or law-firm summaries is marked **[secondary]** and is not used for any number in the script.

## 1. What "ultra-processed" means (NOVA)

- NOVA sorts foods by the nature, extent and purpose of industrial processing, not by nutrients. Four groups: (1) unprocessed or minimally processed foods, (2) processed culinary ingredients, (3) processed foods, (4) ultra-processed foods.
- Hall et al. describe group 4 as "formulations mostly of cheap industrial sources of dietary energy and nutrients plus additives, using a series of processes."
- Example categories of ultra-processed food come from the two cohorts below: sugary products, sweetened and artificially sweetened drinks, packaged snacks, processed meat and fish products, refined breads, sauces and spreads, ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat mixed dishes. In the Chen et al. paper the group also contains dark and whole-grain breads, packaged cereals and yogurt or dairy desserts.
- The group 1 to 3 examples in the script (fruit, vegetables, eggs, oil, butter, canned beans, cheese) are the standard NOVA examples. They are not quoted from a single fetched page. Treat as common NOVA knowledge. NOVA was developed by Brazilian researchers (Monteiro and colleagues); the script says only "researchers in Brazil".
- Limitation: NOVA classifies by process, so a sugary soda and a packaged whole-grain bread can sit in the same group. Food questionnaires are not designed for NOVA, which adds misclassification.

## 2. NIH inpatient randomized trial (key study 1)

- **Citation:** Hall KD, Ayuketah A, Brychta R, et al. Ultra-processed diets cause excess calorie intake and weight gain: an inpatient randomized controlled trial of ad libitum food intake. Cell Metabolism. 2019;30(1):67-77.e3. DOI 10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008. PMID 31105044. PMC7946062. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946062/
- **Design:** randomized, crossover, inpatient at the NIH Clinical Center. 14 days ultra-processed, 14 days unprocessed, order randomized, 28 days in total. Meals matched for presented calories, energy density, sugar, sodium, fiber and macronutrients. Participants could eat as much or as little as they wanted.
- **Population:** n = 20 weight-stable adults, 10 men and 10 women, mean age 31.2 years (SE 1.6), mean BMI 27 kg/m2.
- **Results:** energy intake 508 ± 106 kcal/day higher on the ultra-processed diet (p = 0.0001), from carbohydrate (about 280 kcal/day) and fat (about 230 kcal/day), no difference in protein. Weight +0.9 kg on ultra-processed, -0.9 kg on unprocessed (0.9 kg is about 2 lb). Fat mass +0.4 ± 0.1 kg vs -0.3 ± 0.1 kg. Eating rate faster on the ultra-processed diet by 17 ± 1 kcal/min (p < 0.0001). The paper also describes these foods as softer and easier to chew. PYY (appetite-suppressing hormone) 34.3 pg/ml after unprocessed vs 25.1 pg/ml after ultra-processed. Ghrelin fell on the unprocessed diet only.
- **Limitations (authors):** short (2 weeks per diet), hospital setting, free meals with no choice, no washout or run-in, not designed to identify the mechanism, matching of sugar, fat and sodium may have narrowed the intake difference, energy intake estimated without fecal collection. Participants were young adults; no older-adult data.

## 3. UPDATE trial, UK (key study 2)

- **Citation:** Dicken SJ, Jassil FC, Brown A, et al. Ultraprocessed or minimally processed diets following healthy dietary guidelines on weight and cardiometabolic health: a randomized, crossover trial. Nature Medicine. 2025;31(10):3297-3308. DOI 10.1038/s41591-025-03842-0. PMID 40760353. (Read via Europe PMC.)
- **Design:** 2 x 2 randomized crossover feeding trial (NCT05627570), two 8-week ad libitum diets, both following the UK Eatwell Guide. Primary outcome: percent weight change. 55 adults randomized in England, BMI 25 to under 40, habitual ultra-processed intake at least 50 percent of calories; 50 in the intention-to-treat sample.
- **Results:** weight change -2.06 percent (95% CI -2.99 to -1.13) on the minimally processed diet vs -1.05 percent (95% CI -1.98 to -0.13) on the ultra-processed diet. Difference -1.01 percentage points (95% CI -1.87 to -0.14), p = 0.024. Mild gastrointestinal side effects common on both diets.
- **Limitations:** 8 weeks, adults of all ages with overweight, provided food, UK diet and food supply. Co-authors include Kevin Hall. Other secondary outcomes (fat mass, self-reported intake) were seen only in press summaries [secondary] and are not used.

## 4. French cohort and cardiovascular disease (key study 3)

- **Citation:** Srour B, Fezeu LK, Kesse-Guyot E, et al. Ultra-processed food intake and risk of cardiovascular disease: prospective cohort study (NutriNet-Santé). BMJ. 2019;365:l1451. DOI 10.1136/bmj.l1451. PMID 31142457. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6538975/
- **Design/population:** prospective cohort, n = 105,159 adults in France, mean age 42.7, 79.2 percent women, median follow-up 5.2 years, repeated 24-hour records. Ultra-processed food averaged 17.4 percent of grams eaten.
- **Results:** per 10 percentage-point rise in ultra-processed share of diet: cardiovascular disease HR 1.12 (1.05 to 1.20; 1,409 cases), coronary heart disease HR 1.13 (1.02 to 1.24; 665 cases), cerebrovascular disease HR 1.11 (1.01 to 1.21; 829 cases). Highest vs lowest quarter, overall CVD: 277 vs 242 cases per 100,000 person-years (about 3.5 per 10,000 per year). Ultra-processed beverages were linked with overall CVD; fats, sauces and meats with coronary disease; beverages, sugary products and salty snacks with cerebrovascular disease.
- **Limitations (authors):** observational, residual confounding, NOVA misclassification, short follow-up, volunteer cohort younger, more female, more educated and healthier than the French population, about 20 percent excluded as under-reporters. Causality "remains to be established".

## 5. US cohorts and type 2 diabetes (key study 4)

- **Citation:** Chen Z, Khandpur N, Desjardins C, et al. Ultra-processed food consumption and risk of type 2 diabetes: three large prospective U.S. cohort studies. Diabetes Care. 2023;46(7):1335-1344. DOI 10.2337/dc22-1993. PMID 36854188. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10300524/
- **Design/population:** Nurses' Health Study (71,871 women), NHS II (87,918 women) and Health Professionals Follow-up Study (38,847 men): 198,636 people, 19,503 incident type 2 diabetes cases, follow-up about 26 to 32 years.
- **Results:** highest vs lowest fifth of intake, HR 1.46 (1.39 to 1.54) with intake as percent of grams; HR 1.28 (1.21 to 1.36) with servings per day after adjusting for baseline BMI (BMI accounted for roughly two-thirds of the attenuation by the authors' estimate). Subgroups linked with higher risk: refined breads, sauces/spreads/condiments, sugar- and artificially sweetened beverages, animal-based products, ready-to-eat or ready-to-heat mixed dishes. Subgroups linked with lower risk: dark and whole-grain breads, ultra-processed cereals, packaged sweet and savory snacks, fruit-based products, yogurt and dairy-based desserts. Measured nutrients (fiber, added sugar, sodium and others) explained less than 30 percent of the associations.
- **Limitations (authors):** food-frequency questionnaires, mostly White health professionals, observational, possible confounding by health consciousness (especially for the lower-risk fruit and snack findings), not validated for NOVA.

## 6. Umbrella review (review)

- **Citation:** Lane MM, Gamage E, Du S, et al. Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. 2024;384:e077310. DOI 10.1136/bmj-2023-077310. PMID 38418082. (Read via Europe PMC.)
- **Content:** 45 pooled analyses, about 9.9 million participants. Higher ultra-processed intake was associated with 32 health parameters (mortality, cancer, mental, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, metabolic). Evidence graded convincing for cardiovascular disease mortality, type 2 diabetes, anxiety and common mental disorders. Authors call for mechanistic research and population-level measures.
- **Limitations:** nearly all included studies are observational; umbrella reviews give a high-level overview and cannot remove confounding. Press-reported effect sizes were not verified against the paper and are not used.

## 7. US guidance and label reading

- **FDA, How to understand and use the Nutrition Facts label** https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/how-understand-and-use-nutrition-facts-label : 5 percent Daily Value or less per serving is low, 20 percent or more is high. Get less of added sugars, sodium and saturated fat; get more of fiber. Added Sugars line includes syrups, honey and concentrated juices. Sodium Daily Value is 2,300 mg; the page's sample lasagna has 850 mg, 37 percent DV. Fiber Daily Value 28 g. The page does not discuss ingredient lists.
- **Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025-2030** [secondary]: released 7 January 2026 by HHS and USDA. Law-firm and news summaries (Wiley, Hogan Lovells, DLA Piper) report advice to significantly reduce "highly processed" foods high in refined carbohydrates, added sugars and sodium, with no formal definition of ultra-processed or highly processed. dietaryguidelines.gov home page itself did not state this when fetched. The script mentions it in one sentence only, without numbers.
- **Ingredient-list tip** (long list with flavorings, emulsifiers, syrups usually signals ultra-processing) is a practical heuristic drawn from the NOVA definition (additives and industrial ingredients), not a tested rule. The script says "usually" and presents it as a hint.

## 8. Other evidence seen, not used for numbers

- Rico-Campà A, et al. Association between consumption of ultra-processed foods and all cause mortality: SUN prospective cohort study. BMJ. 2019;365:l1949. DOI 10.1136/bmj.l1949. n = 19,899 Spanish graduates, mean age 37.6, median 10.4 years, 335 deaths; most vs least (over 4 servings/day) HR 1.62 (1.13 to 2.33). Young cohort, few deaths.
- NIH-AARP analysis (Loftfield et al., 50 to 71 year olds, about 540,000, median 23 years follow-up) [secondary]: seen only as press coverage of a NUTRITION 2024 poster; roughly 10 percent higher mortality at high intake. Not used because no peer-reviewed paper was opened.

Full text could not be read; press says its review covered 104 long-term studies with 92 reporting higher risk, mostly associations, funded by Bloomberg Philanthropies, with critics saying the category is too broad [secondary]. Not used.

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- In a short controlled hospital trial, a diet made of ultra-processed foods, matched on presented nutrients, led young and middle-aged adults to eat about 500 more calories a day and gain a little weight and fat (Hall 2019, n = 20).
- A second, longer crossover trial found somewhat less weight loss on an ultra-processed diet even when both diets followed national healthy-eating guidance (Dicken 2025, 8 weeks each).
- Large cohorts consistently link higher ultra-processed intake with more cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes (Srour 2019, Chen 2023, Lane 2024).

**Uncertain**
- Whether the cohort links are causal, and how much of them is confounding or body weight. No long-term trial of hard outcomes exists.
- Why: eating speed, softness and fullness hormones are clues only; measured nutrients explained under 30 percent of the diabetes link.
- Whether the effects are the same in adults over 65: none of the key studies was built around older adults.
- Whether NOVA is the right yardstick. Subgroups differ; whole-grain bread and yogurt looked favorable in one cohort, possibly from healthy-user effects.

**False or unsupported**
- That every packaged or processed food is dangerous; the group is very mixed, and per-person absolute differences in the French cohort were small (about 3.5 extra cardiovascular cases per 10,000 people per year between top and bottom quarters).
- That any single serving causes harm, or that "unprocessed" automatically means healthy.
- That convenience foods should be shamed: for people with trouble chewing, cooking or shopping, or low appetite, they can help meet needs; a dietitian can guide choices.
