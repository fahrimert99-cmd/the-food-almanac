# Research notes: eggs and cholesterol (slug: eggs-and-cholesterol)

Question: Do eggs really raise your cholesterol? Pages were opened via Europe PMC records, publisher abstracts and news/agency summaries. PubMed, AHA and ACC pages returned errors or blocks on some fetches; where only a secondary summary was available, that is flagged below and the video avoids relying on those details.

## 1. Dietary cholesterol is not the same as blood cholesterol (basics)
- One large egg contains about 186 mg of cholesterol (USDA FoodData Central values as reproduced on nutrition-database sites; a USDA-ARS review reportedly found about 185 mg). Source page: https://fdc.nal.usda.gov (entry "Egg, whole, raw, fresh"). Limitation: the figure was confirmed on third-party pages quoting USDA, not on the FDC page itself; the video says "about 185 milligrams" and "roughly 190".
- The 2010 Dietary Guidelines for Americans advised under 300 mg of dietary cholesterol per day. The 2015 Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee stated "Cholesterol is not a nutrient of concern for overconsumption", reasoning that evidence showed no appreciable relationship between dietary and serum cholesterol; the 2015-2020 Dietary Guidelines did not carry forward the 300 mg limit, but said this does not mean dietary cholesterol is unimportant when building a healthy eating pattern. Source: secondary news and trade summaries of the DGAC report and DGA (e.g. https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov). Limitation: primary PDF was too large to open; wording is paraphrased in the video.

## 2. Cohort studies and meta-analysis (Harvard, BMJ 2020)
- Drouin-Chartier JP, Chen S, Li Y, Schwab AL, Stampfer MJ, Sacks FM, Rosner B, Willett WC, Hu FB, Bhupathiraju SN. Egg consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease: three large prospective US cohort studies, systematic review, and updated meta-analysis. BMJ 2020;368:m513. doi:10.1136/bmj.m513. Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7190072/
- Design: Nurses' Health Study (83,349 women), NHS II (90,214 women), Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (42,055 men); all free of CVD, diabetes and cancer at baseline; follow-up up to 32 years (1980-2012 / 1991-2013 / 1986-2012); 14,806 incident CVD cases (9,010 coronary heart disease, 5,903 stroke); more than 5.5 million person-years.
- Result: pooled fully adjusted HR for at least 1 egg a day versus less than 1 egg a month = 0.93 (95% CI 0.82-1.05). Age-only model gave 1.10 (0.97-1.23): adjusting for lifestyle and diet moved the estimate. Per 1 egg/day increase HR 0.98 (0.92-1.04).
- Meta-analysis: 28 studies including the new cohorts; per 1 egg/day, 33 risk estimates, 1,720,108 participants, 139,195 events; pooled RR 0.98 (0.93-1.03), I2 = 62.3%. By region: US 1.01 (0.96-1.06); Europe 1.05 (0.92-1.19); Asia 0.92 (0.85-0.99); interaction P = 0.07.
- People with type 2 diabetes: cohort HR 1.06 (0.81-1.39) with diabetes vs 0.93 (0.81-1.06) without; meta-analysis in diabetes, per 1 egg/day RR 1.25 (0.99-1.59), I2 = 64.6% (considerable heterogeneity); high vs low intake RR 1.40 (1.00-1.97).
- Authors' conclusion: moderate egg consumption (up to one egg per day) is not associated with CVD risk overall, with possibly lower risk in Asian populations; evidence in diabetes is suggestive of higher risk but inconsistent.
- Limitations: observational (association, not cause); diet self-reported by questionnaire; cohorts are health professionals (mostly white, relatively health-conscious); residual confounding.

## 3. The conflicting pooled cohort (JAMA 2019)
- Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Cornelis MC, Wilkins JT, Ning H, Carnethon MR, Greenland P, Mentz RJ, Tucker KL, Zhao L, Norwood AF, Lloyd-Jones DM, Allen NB. Associations of dietary cholesterol or egg consumption with incident cardiovascular disease and mortality. JAMA 2019;321(11):1081-1095. doi:10.1001/jama.2019.1572. PMID 30874756. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30874756/
- Design: pooled individual-level data from 6 prospective US cohorts, 29,615 participants, median follow-up 17.5 years.
- Per additional 300 mg/day dietary cholesterol: incident CVD adjusted HR 1.17 (1.09-1.26), adjusted absolute risk difference 3.24% (1.39-5.08); all-cause mortality HR 1.18 (1.10-1.26), ARD 4.43% (2.51-6.36).
- Per additional half an egg a day: incident CVD HR 1.06 (1.03-1.10), ARD 1.11% (0.32-1.89); all-cause mortality HR 1.08 (1.04-1.11), ARD 1.93% (1.10-2.76).
- Egg associations were no longer significant after adjusting for dietary cholesterol intake (i.e. cholesterol content of eggs drove the association); cholesterol associations lost significance after adjusting for eggs and red meat (the latter from an accompanying summary; not used on screen).
- Limitations: observational; single or few diet assessments at baseline in some cohorts (diet can change over 17 years); residual confounding; effect sizes small in absolute terms; cohorts differ from the Harvard ones (more varied, older diet-assessment methods). Cited with care: ARDs are cumulative over follow-up.

## 4. Randomized trials on blood cholesterol
- Rouhani MH, Rashidi-Pourfard N, Salehi-Abargouei A, Karimi M, Haghighatdoost F. Effects of egg consumption on blood lipids: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials. J Am Coll Nutr 2018;37(2):99-110. doi:10.1080/07315724.2017.1366878. PMID 29111915. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29111915/
  - 28 randomized trials; more eggs raised LDL cholesterol by 5.55 mg/dL (95% CI 3.14-7.69) and HDL cholesterol by 2.13 mg/dL (1.10-3.16) versus control diets. No effect on LDL:HDL or total:HDL ratios or on triglycerides (per search summary of the abstract). Participant total not in abstract. Limitation: mostly short trials, varied designs; blood lipids are a risk marker, not heart attacks.
- Vincent MJ, Allen B, Palacios OM, Haber LT, Maki KC. Meta-regression analysis of the effects of dietary cholesterol intake on LDL and HDL cholesterol: an update. Am J Clin Nutr 2019;109(1):7-16. doi:10.1093/ajcn/nqy273. PMID 30596814. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30596814/
  - 55 studies, 2,652 participants; predicted LDL rise per extra 100 mg/day of dietary cholesterol: 1.90 mg/dL (linear model), 4.46 (Michaelis-Menten), 4.58 (Hill). Limitation: CIs not seen; intermediate marker.
  - Derived (not stated in the paper): one large egg is about 185 mg, so about 1.85 x the per-100 mg figure: a few mg/dL per egg. The video says only "a few milligrams per deciliter".

## 5. Responders vary ("hyper-responders")
- Fernandez ML. Dietary cholesterol provided by eggs and plasma lipoproteins in healthy populations. Curr Opin Clin Nutr Metab Care 2006;9(1):8-12. doi:10.1097/01.mco.0000171152.51034.bf. PMID 16340654. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16340654/
  - Review: about 70% of the population shows only a mild increase or no change in plasma cholesterol with high dietary cholesterol ("hypo-responders"); the rest ("hyper-responders") show larger rises. Egg intake promotes larger LDL particles and shifts people from LDL pattern B to pattern A, described as less atherogenic. Limitation: a narrative review from an egg-research group; whether large-particle LDL rise is harmless is not settled.
- Not used: a 2019 Fernandez and DiMarco abstract (12 of 36 adults meeting hyper-responder cutoff on 3 eggs for 4 weeks) was seen only through a search summary.

## 6. People with diabetes
- Fuller NR et al. Effect of a high-egg diet on cardiometabolic risk factors in people with type 2 diabetes: the Diabetes and Egg (DIABEGG) Study, randomized weight-loss and follow-up phase. Am J Clin Nutr 2018;107(6):921-931. PMID 29741558. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29741558/
  - 128 adults with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes; weight-loss diet; high-egg (at least 12 eggs a week) vs low-egg (less than 2 a week) for 3 months of weight loss then follow-up to 12 months. Weight change from month 3 to 12 similar (-3.1 kg both groups). No group differences in blood sugar, HbA1c, standard blood lipids or inflammation markers. Limitation: modest size; both diets low in saturated fat; participants were losing weight, so lipid responses may be smaller.
- The cohort and meta-analysis results in diabetes (section 2) point toward possible higher risk but are inconsistent (CI includes 1 for per-egg estimate).

## 7. Guideline position
- Carson JAS, Lichtenstein AH, Anderson CAM, Appel LJ, Kris-Etherton PM, Meyer KA, Petersen K, Polonsky T, Van Horn L; AHA Nutrition Committee. Dietary cholesterol and cardiovascular risk: a science advisory from the American Heart Association. Circulation 2020;141(3):e39-e53. doi:10.1161/CIR.0000000000000743. https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000743
  - Abstract: observational studies from several countries generally show no significant association with CVD; meta-analyses of intervention studies disagree, but most link intakes above current average levels to higher total or LDL cholesterol; guidance should focus on healthy dietary patterns (Mediterranean-style, DASH-style) that are naturally low in cholesterol; a specific cholesterol target is hard to apply. Published online December 2019 (the video says "the American Heart Association advisory, published in late 2019").
  - Patterns named in the abstract: vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, lean proteins, nuts, seeds and liquid vegetable oils. The video's remark that eggs are often served with bacon, sausage, butter and white toast, adding saturated fat, is general context (a search summary of the advisory notes cholesterol often comes packaged with saturated fat), not a measured result.
  - The advisory's specific per-day egg amounts were only seen in secondary summaries and are not used on screen.
- Context: egg-industry-affiliated pages exist for some of these studies; the video relies on journal and agency sources only.

## Honest verdict
- Well supported: eggs contain cholesterol (about 185 mg each) and, in trials, eating more eggs modestly raises LDL cholesterol (about +5.5 mg/dL in a 28-trial meta-analysis) and HDL too. In large observational studies, up to about one egg a day is not linked with more heart disease or stroke in the general population (Harvard cohorts and meta-analysis).
- Uncertain: pooled cohort data in JAMA found small positive associations per half egg a day, so the question is not closed; observational data cannot separate eggs from what is eaten with them (age-only HR 1.10 vs adjusted 0.93); effect in people with diabetes (possibly higher risk, inconsistent); how much the "hyper-responder" LDL rise matters for heart disease; no large long-term trial with heart attacks as the outcome.
- False or overstated: "eggs are as bad for your arteries as smoking"; "dietary cholesterol has no effect on blood cholesterol at all" (it has a small effect on average); "the 300 mg limit was dropped because eggs were proven harmless" (the limit was dropped because a numeric target was hard to apply and evidence of independent effect was weak; guidelines still stress overall dietary pattern).
