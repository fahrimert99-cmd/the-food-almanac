# Research notes: How long are leftovers really safe to eat?

Working title: "Are 4-Day-Old Leftovers Safe After 60?"

**Access note.** The USDA FSIS leftovers pages (fsis.usda.gov), foodsafety.gov, Mayo Clinic and the CDC Bacillus cereus page returned 403/404 errors, so none of them could be opened. Every number in the script comes from a page that was actually opened (listed below). Figures that appeared only in search-result summaries (FDA Food Code 135°F to 70°F cooling steps, Dutch refrigerator survey, MSU "can't see, smell or taste" page) were **not used** in the script.

## 1. Time out of the fridge: the two-hour rule

- **Claim:** Do not leave food out of the refrigerator for more than 2 hours; if the temperature is above 90°F, limit is 1 hour.
- **Source:** American Heart Association, "Food Safety: How to Store and How Long to Keep Leftovers." https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/cooking-skills/storing/food-safety---how-to-store-and-how-long-to-keep-leftovers (opened).
- **Supporting:** Patton P, Larvick C, Albrecht JA. Leftover Food Safety. University of Nebraska-Lincoln Extension (Food Safety for Families, 2008; page reviewed and updated 2026). https://food.unl.edu/article/leftover-food-safety (opened): refrigerate within 2 hours of cooking or serving; discard food left out longer. Rutgers Cooperative Extension FS588, Elnakib S, Schaffner D. "Handling Leftovers Safely," Dec 2019. https://extension.rutgers.edu/fs588/ (opened): refrigerate or freeze within 2 hours, 1 hour in hot weather.
- **Type:** agency/extension guidance, not a trial. **Limitations:** a rule of thumb built on bacterial growth data; individual foods differ.

## 2. The 3 to 4 day fridge window; day 4 is the outer edge

- **AHA:** leftovers keep "on average three to four days in the refrigerator"; refrigerator 40°F or below; freezer 0°F; reheat to internal temperature of 165°F; frozen leftovers up to six months; divide into smaller portions sized for one sitting; label frozen items with dish and date; "if unsure, throw it away." (same URL as above, opened)
- **UNL Extension:** refrigerated leftovers 3 to 4 days; food thawed in the refrigerator used within 3 to 4 days or refrozen; reheat to 165°F or until hot and steamy; soups, sauces, gravies to a rolling boil; shallow pans no more than 3 inches deep; ice bath for soups and stews; put hot food in the refrigerator, top shelf, covered loosely until cool; frozen leftovers 2 to 6 months for quality, "safe indefinitely."
- **Rutgers FS588:** "As a general rule, never keep leftovers for more than four days"; containers "three inches tall or less" with space around them; reheat to 165°F throughout, boil wet foods; stir in microwave (uneven heating); mark leftovers with date cooked; "Never taste leftovers that are of questionable age or safety."
- **Limitations:** USDA's own page could not be opened; these three sources all state they follow USDA/FSIS guidance, but treat the script's "three to four days" as extension/AHA guidance, with Rutgers' four-day ceiling.

## 3. Listeria grows in the refrigerator

- **FDA, Listeria (Listeriosis).** https://www.fda.gov/food/foodborne-pathogens/listeria-listeriosis (opened). *L. monocytogenes* "can survive and grow even under refrigeration"; "freezing will not eliminate or reduce the pathogen"; adults over 65 "at greater risk of serious health complications ... including death"; keep refrigerator at 40°F (4°C) or below, freezer at 0°F.
- **CDC, Listeria.** https://www.cdc.gov/listeria/about/index.html (opened). Adults 65 and older among those for whom infection "can be especially harmful"; CDC estimates 1,250 people infected and 172 die each year in the US.
- **Original study:** Evans EW, Redmond EC. Laboratory re-enactment of storage practices of older adults to determine potential implications for Listeria monocytogenes growth. *Food Protection Trends* 2019;39(3):225-236. https://www.foodprotection.org/publications/food-protection-trends/archive/2019-05-laboratory-re-enactment-of-storage-practices-of-older-adults-to-determine-potential-implicat/ (opened).
  - Design: laboratory inoculation (about 3.7 log CFU) of soft cheese and ready-to-eat meat, stored at 2.5°C (n = 110), 7.8°C (n = 110) and 19.5°C (n = 55), sampled every 24 h for up to 21 days.
  - Result: growth at every temperature. Average generation time (time to double) 94 h at 2.5°C (about 36°F), 21.5 h at 7.8°C (about 46°F), 11 h at 19.5°C (about 67°F). Growth slowest in the recommended fridge range; longer storage gave higher populations.
  - Limitations: lab study on cheese and deli meat, not cooked leftovers; no people were studied; generation time is not an illness risk.

## 4. Bacillus cereus: rice, toxin, and reheating

- **Outbreak report:** CDC. Bacillus cereus food poisoning associated with fried rice at two child day care centers, Virginia, 1993. *MMWR* (Epidemiologic Notes and Reports). https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00025744.htm (opened).
  - Lunch on July 21, 1993; 82 children (age 6 or younger) and 9 staff were served; 67 of 80 interviewed ate the lunch; 14 (21%) became ill vs 0 of 13 who did not eat it. 12 cases in children aged 2.5 to 5, 2 in staff. Chicken fried rice was the only food significantly associated: 14 of 48 who ate it fell ill vs 0 of 16 who did not. Median incubation 2 h, median duration 4 h. *B. cereus* isolated from leftover rice and from an ill child's vomit.
  - Rice cooked the night before, cooled at room temperature before refrigeration; on the day it was pan-fried, held unrefrigerated and served at noon. The report notes the heat-stable toxin can survive brief heating such as stir-frying. Recommendation: stop room-temperature cooling of rice and other foods; keep food below 41°F (5°C) or above 140°F (60°C).
  - Limitations: small outbreak investigation (a case series with a retrospective cohort), 1993; no direct toxin measurement is described in the summary opened.
- **Review:** Yang S, Wang Y, Liu Y, Jia K, Zhang Z, Dong Q. Cereulide and emetic *Bacillus cereus*: characterizations, impacts and public precautions. *Foods* 2023;12(4):833. doi:10.3390/foods12040833. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9956921/ (opened; first 100,000 of about 201,000 characters read).
  - Cereulide stable at 121°C for 2 h and through roasting, frying and microwaving; stable at pH 2 to 10. Emetic onset 0.5 to 5 h; diarrheal form 8 to 16 h. Rice and starchy foods are important vehicles; contaminated starchy foods "may look and smell normal." Reheating is "inefficient" at destroying the toxins; spores can germinate when cooked food cools slowly. Cool quickly, refrigerate; below 4°C inhibits all strains.
  - Limitations: narrative review; mostly lab and outbreak data.

## 5. Big batches left warm: Clostridium perfringens

- **CDC, C. perfringens.** https://www.cdc.gov/clostridium-perfringens/about/index.html (opened). "Nearly 900,000" foodborne illnesses per year in the US; outbreaks most often involve foods cooked in large batches and held at unsafe temperatures, between 40°F and 140°F. Foods often linked: poultry, meat, gravy.
- Limitation: the page opened did not describe cooling methods; those come from UNL and Rutgers.

## 6. Why risk is higher after 60 to 65

- **FDA, Food Safety for Older Adults and People with Cancer, Diabetes, HIV/AIDS, Organ Transplants, and Autoimmune Disease.** http://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/food-safety-older-adults-and-people-cancer-diabetes-hivaids-organ-transplants-and-autoimmune (opened). People 65 and older are at higher risk for hospitalization and death from foodborne illness; organs and body systems change with age; the stomach may not produce enough acid to limit intestinal bacteria; diabetes may damage cells that make stomach acid; "between 50 and 60 the immune system in most people begins to decline"; 48 million (1 in 6) Americans get foodborne infections yearly, 128,000 hospitalized, 3,000 die (all ages).
- **CDC, People at Increased Risk for Food Poisoning.** https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/risk-factors/index.html (opened). "Nearly half of people aged 65 and older who have a lab-confirmed foodborne illness from Salmonella, Campylobacter, Listeria or E. coli are hospitalized." Limitation: lab-confirmed cases are skewed toward more severe illness, and the figure is for four pathogens combined.
- **Context study:** Scallan E et al. Foodborne illness acquired in the United States: major pathogens. *Emerg Infect Dis* 2011;17(1):7-15 (title and headline figures seen in search results only; not cited in the script).

## Honest verdict

**Well supported**
- Perishable cooked food should be in the fridge within 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F); leftovers are generally kept 3 to 4 days (never beyond 4 per Rutgers); reheat to 165°F; shallow containers (3 inches or less) cool faster; freezing keeps food safe indefinitely though quality fades.
- Listeria can grow at refrigerator temperatures (FDA, lab data), faster in a warmer fridge. Freezing does not eliminate it.
- *B. cereus* emetic toxin survives heating, so reheating cannot rescue rice or pasta that sat out too long (outbreak report plus review).
- People 65 and older are at higher risk of severe outcomes from foodborne illness (FDA, CDC).

**Uncertain**
- The exact day count: 3 to 4 days is a conservative guideline, not a cliff. No study tested "day 4 vs day 5" for cooked leftovers in home fridges. The Listeria lab data are on cheese and deli meat, not stews.
- Whether smell is useless for every food: the sources show it for starchy foods with *B. cereus* and that guidance says never to taste questionable leftovers; spoilage bacteria can produce odors, but the dangerous germs may not.
- How much extra risk age adds for a typical home leftover is not quantified; the data are for lab-confirmed infections.

**False or misleading**
- "If it smells fine, it is safe."
- "Reheating until it is steaming hot makes anything safe."
- "Hot food must cool completely on the counter before it goes in the fridge" (UNL: put hot food in the refrigerator, shallow, loosely covered).
- "Freezing kills the bacteria."
