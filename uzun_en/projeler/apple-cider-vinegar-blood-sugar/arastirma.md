# Research notes: Does apple cider vinegar really lower blood sugar?

Slug: apple-cider-vinegar-blood-sugar. All sources below were opened by me (abstract or full page via Europe PMC / PMC). Where a publisher page blocked me (PubMed, Diabetes Care), I did not use its numbers.

## 1. Single-meal trial: liquid vinegar vs tablets (human, original RCT)
- **Feise NK, Johnston CS. Commercial Vinegar Tablets Do Not Display the Same Physiological Benefits for Managing Postprandial Glucose Concentrations as Liquid Vinegar. J Nutr Metab. 2020;2020:9098739.** DOI 10.1155/2020/9098739. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7803290/
- Design: 4-arm Latin-square crossover, one week between treatments. 15 enrolled, **12 completed** (5 men, 7 women), mean age 22.6 y, mean BMI 21.2 kg/m2, healthy. Arizona State University.
- Arms, each with a **64 g carbohydrate** test meal: water control; **25 g liquid vinegar (1.25 g acetic acid)**; 4 whole vinegar tablets (1.50 g acetic acid); 4 crushed tablets dissolved in water.
- Result: 60-minute glucose incremental AUC = **4.9 (water), 3.4 (liquid vinegar), 4.9 (whole tablets), 4.1 (crushed tablets) mmol·h/L**, p = 0.043. Liquid vinegar reduced the excursion by **31%** versus control and whole tablets (p = 0.040 and 0.049); not significantly different from crushed tablets (p = 0.278). Fasting glucose 5.06 mmol/L, no differences.
- Limitations (authors): small n, only young healthy adults, glucose measured only for the first hour, finger-stick capillary blood, insulin not measured, acetic acid content taken from labels, tablet dissolution unknown. Internal inconsistency ("4-week" in results vs one-week washout in methods).

## 2. Meta-analysis of acute vinegar and SCFA trials (human, systematic review)
- **Cherta-Murillo A, Pugh JE, Alaraj-Alshehhi S, Hajjar D, Chambers ES, Frost GS. The effects of SCFAs on glycemic control in humans: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Am J Clin Nutr. 2022;116(2):335-361.** DOI 10.1093/ajcn/nqac085, PMID 35388874. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9348993/ (Imperial College London)
- RCTs in adults, searched to 7 Dec 2021: 43 papers, 46 studies, **n = 913** (all SCFAs combined); 44 studies in the meta-analysis.
- Acute vinegar, impaired glucose tolerance / type 2 diabetes: postprandial glucose **SMD −0.53 (95% CI −0.92 to −0.14), n = 67**. Postprandial insulin −0.16 (−0.75 to 0.44), not significant.
- Acute vinegar, healthy volunteers: glucose **SMD −0.27 (−0.54 to 0.00), n = 186**, borderline. Insulin −0.29 (−0.66 to 0.08), not significant.
- Chronic vinegar fasting glucose: SMD −1.60 (−4.30 to 1.09), n = 143, **I2 = 99%**, not significant.
- Authors' conclusion: evidence is **very uncertain**. Certainty very low for most outcomes; **47% of studies at high risk of bias**; publication bias detected on funnel plots for several outcomes; few studies drove some results; doses/vehicles/populations varied.

## 3. Meta-analysis in diabetes (human, systematic review)
- **Siddiqui FJ, Assam PN, de Souza NN, Sultana R, Dalan R, Chan ES. Diabetes Control: Is Vinegar a Promising Candidate to Help Achieve Targets? J Evid Based Integr Med. 2018;23:2156587217753004.** DOI 10.1177/2156587217753004. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954571/
- 12 articles / 11 studies / **278 participants** with type 1 or type 2 diabetes (5 parallel RCTs, 5 crossover RCTs, 1 non-randomized, 1 single-arm). Long-term studies 8–12 weeks (one 1 month). Doses: apple cider vinegar about 15–20 g (about 2 tablespoons) in most trials.
- **HbA1c (8–12 weeks): mean difference −0.39% (95% CI −0.59 to −0.18), 3 studies, n = 147.**
- **Fasting plasma glucose: −0.80 mmol/L (−1.47 to −0.14), 3 studies, n = 161** (≈ 14 mg/dL; 1 mmol/L = 18 mg/dL).
- Short-term postprandial: lower at 30 minutes (difference 0.88 mmol/L), not significant at 60, 90, 120 minutes.
- Adverse effects did not appear to favor the control group.
- Limitations (authors): small trials, heterogeneity in populations/meals/doses, some data digitized from graphs/imputed, some lack of blinding. They state the "quantity and quality of evidence is insufficient" for definitive conclusions on effectiveness and safety.

## 4. Earlier meta-analysis (human)
- **Shishehbor F, Mansoori A, Shirani F. Vinegar consumption can attenuate postprandial glucose and insulin responses; a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials. Diabetes Res Clin Pract. 2017;127.** DOI 10.1016/j.diabres.2017.01.021. Abstract (via Europe PMC): pooled glucose and insulin AUC significantly lower with vinegar than control; vinegar "may be an adjunct". The figure SMD −0.60 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.11), 11 trials, is as cited in Feise & Johnston 2020. Not used on screen beyond the general direction.

## 5. Mechanism: gastric emptying (human pilot)
- **Hlebowicz J, Darwiche G, Björgell O, Almér LO. Effect of apple cider vinegar on delayed gastric emptying in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus: a pilot study. BMC Gastroenterol. 2007;7:46.** DOI 10.1186/1471-230X-7-46, PMID 18093343.
- Investigator-blinded crossover, **10 patients with type 1 diabetes and diabetic gastroparesis**. Gastric emptying rate by ultrasound of the antral area after 300 g rice pudding + 200 mL water. One week 200 mL water daily before breakfast, then **two weeks 200 mL water + 30 mL apple cider vinegar** daily before breakfast. Median gastric emptying rate **27% without vinegar, 17% with vinegar** (p < 0.05).
- Authors: vinegar further slowed emptying, which "could be a disadvantage for glycaemic control" in these patients. Background in the abstract: earlier studies in healthy people found vinegar slows gastric emptying and lowers postprandial glucose and insulin.
- Limitations: tiny pilot, a very specific population (gastroparesis), no control for a placebo drink during the vinegar phase, not generalizable to healthy adults. Acetic acid/gastric emptying is the best-supported proposed mechanism but this is not proof that it explains the glucose effect.

## 6. Safety
- **Hill LL, Woodruff LH, Foote JC, Barreto-Alcoba M. Esophageal injury by apple cider vinegar tablets and subsequent evaluation of products. J Am Diet Assoc. 2005;105(7):1141-1144.** DOI 10.1016/j.jada.2005.04.003, PMID 15983536. After a reported adverse event, 8 tablet products were tested; tablet size, pH and acid content varied considerably; the authors could not confirm apple cider vinegar was an ingredient; labeling and dosing were inconsistent.
- **Chang J, Han SE, Paik SS, Kim YJ. Corrosive Esophageal Injury due to a Commercial Vinegar Beverage in an Adolescent. Clin Endosc. 2020;53(3):366-369.** DOI 10.5946/ce.2019.066, PMID 31405264. Case report: 15-year-old drinking an insufficiently diluted vinegar beverage daily; vomiting blood, esophageal ulcers; recovered with treatment. A single case.
- **Maladkar SR, et al. Erosive Effect of Acidic Beverages and Dietary Preservatives on Extracted Human Teeth: An In Vitro Analysis. Eur J Dent. 2022;16(4):919-929.** DOI 10.1055/s-0041-1742131, PMID 35436789. 190 extracted teeth in nine acidic liquids, assessed at 12–96 days; vinegar and apple cider caused the greatest demineralization overall. Lab study, no saliva, no real-world drinking pattern; abstract gives no numbers.
- Medication: RxList's apple cider vinegar page (https://www.rxlist.com/apple_cider_vinegar/supplements.htm; secondary reference, via search summary) says large amounts may lower potassium, warns about combining with water pills (diuretics), and lists insulin and sulfonylureas as drugs whose doses might need adjusting. This is a mechanism-based precaution, not trial evidence; I found no trial showing vinegar causes hypoglycemia. Script wording: "reference sources flag a possible overlap, ask your doctor or pharmacist".

- 2025–2026 umbrella and dose-response reviews (Shahmohammadi 2026; Arjmandfard 2025) exist, with larger pooled effects, but I only saw their abstracts and they mix very different outcomes; not used.

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- In small trials, vinegar taken with a starchy meal tends to lower the after-meal blood sugar rise (about 31% in one 12-person trial; pooled reviews agree on direction, with a larger effect in people with impaired glucose tolerance or type 2 diabetes than in healthy people).
- Vinegar can slow stomach emptying (shown in a small pilot), which is the leading proposed mechanism.
- Undiluted vinegar is corrosive; tablets are inconsistent products and have been linked to esophageal injury; vinegar can erode teeth in lab tests.

**Uncertain**
- How big and how lasting the effect is. Reviewers rate the certainty as very low; trials are small, short, heterogeneous, with high risk of bias and signs of publication bias.
- Daily use over 8–12 weeks: a small pooled HbA1c drop of about 0.4 points (3 studies, n = 147), but huge variation between trials (I2 = 99% for fasting glucose in the 2022 review).
- Whether the gastric-emptying mechanism fully explains the effect; whether it matters for long-term outcomes (heart disease, complications).
- Interactions with medicines (theoretical).

**False or unsupported**
- That vinegar "cancels" carbohydrates or a big dessert, or works as a substitute for diabetes treatment.
- That tablets and gummies work like liquid vinegar (whole tablets did no better than water in the one head-to-head trial).
- That more is better: higher amounts increase risk of throat and tooth harm without proven extra benefit.
