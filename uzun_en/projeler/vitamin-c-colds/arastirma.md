# Research notes: vitamin C, orange juice and the common cold

Topic: Does vitamin C or orange juice help prevent or shorten a cold?

**How sources were checked.** Pages I actually opened are marked OPENED. Where a site blocked automated access (PubMed, PMC, Cochrane Library, NIH ODS returned cookie walls or 403), I used the Cochrane.org evidence summary and search-result extracts instead; these are marked SNIPPET. Nothing below is from memory alone.

## 1. Cochrane review (main source)
- **Citation:** Hemilä H, Chalker E. Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2013;(1):CD000980. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4
- **URL:** https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD000980_vitamin-c-common-cold (OPENED, plain-language summary); full text https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4/full (blocked).
- **Design:** systematic review and meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials with regular vitamin C of at least 0.2 g (200 mg) per day (prevention) and trials of therapeutic vitamin C started after symptom onset.
- **Prevention, general population:** pooled RR for cold incidence 0.97 (95% CI 0.94 to 1.00), 29 comparisons, 11,306 participants.
- **Physical stress subgroup:** 5 trials, 598 marathon runners, skiers and soldiers on subarctic exercises; RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.64).
- **Duration with regular use:** 31 comparisons, 9,745 episodes. Adults about 8% shorter, children about 14% shorter; in children taking 1 to 2 g/day about 18% shorter. Severity also reduced. (An extract of the abstract gives adults 8% (3% to 12%), children 14% (7% to 21%).)
- **Therapeutic (started after symptoms):** 7 comparisons, 3,249 episodes; no consistent effect on duration or severity. One large trial of 8 g at symptom onset reported benefit, as did two trials using five-day supplementation. Few such trials, none in children.
- **Safety:** published trials reported no adverse effects.
- **Authors' conclusion:** routine supplementation not justified for the general population; may help people exposed to brief severe physical exertion; because it is inexpensive and appeared safe, people with colds may reasonably test whether it helps them.
- **Limitations:** pooled adults and children; no focus on adults over 60; trials mostly tested supplements (at least 200 mg/day), not juice or whole fruit; therapeutic trials few and heterogeneous; harms were not studied over years. The stress subgroup was made of athletes and soldiers, not older adults.

## 2. Original trial: ultramarathon runners
- **Citation:** Peters EM, Goetzsche JM, Grobbelaar B, Noakes TD. Vitamin C supplementation reduces the incidence of postrace symptoms of upper-respiratory-tract infection in ultramarathon runners. Am J Clin Nutr. 1993. PMID 8185726.
- **URL:** https://read.qxmd.com/read/8185726/ (OPENED, abstract mirror).
- **Design:** randomized, placebo-controlled; ultramarathon runners and non-running controls; 600 mg vitamin C daily; symptoms tracked for 14 days after the race.
- **Result:** 68% of placebo runners reported upper-respiratory symptoms vs 33% of vitamin C runners (P < 0.01). In non-running controls, vitamin C reduced duration and severity of symptoms (P < 0.05).
- **Limitations:** sample size not stated in the abstract I saw (so not cited in the script); self-reported symptoms; extreme event; athletes. A later marathon trial (Himmelstein 1998) found no difference over 3 months, only fewer colds in the two weeks after the race (search extract, not used in the script).

## 3. 2023 meta-analysis on severity
- **Citation:** Hemilä H, Chalker E. Vitamin C reduces the severity of common colds: a meta-analysis. BMC Public Health. 2023;23:2468. PMID 38082300.
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38082300/ (blocked); content from search extract of the abstract (SNIPPET).
- **Design:** 15 comparisons from 10 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials of oral vitamin C at 1 g/day or more in healthy people, restricted to trials reporting both duration and severity scales.
- **Result:** severity reduced by about 15% (95% CI 9 to 21%).
- **Limitations:** severity is a self-rated symptom score, not infection prevention; high-dose supplement trials; authors have long advocated vitamin C research (potential for enthusiasm bias).

## 4. Kidney stones: Swedish cohort
- **Citation:** Thomas LD, Elinder CG, Tiselius HG, Wolk A, Åkesson A. Ascorbic acid supplements and kidney stone incidence among men: a prospective study. JAMA Intern Med. 2013;173(5):386-388.
- **URL:** https://news.ki.se/vitamin-c-supplements-linked-to-kidney-stones (OPENED, Karolinska Institutet release); JAMA record https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/article-abstract/1719138 (only the letter below was readable).
- **Design:** prospective population cohort, 23,355 men with no history of kidney stones, Västmanland and Örebro, Sweden, 11 years follow-up; 436 developed stones needing medical care. Supplement users were about twice as likely as non-users; risk rose with frequency of use. Typical tablet 1,000 mg (Swedish RDI 75 mg). Search extracts report adjusted RR about 1.9 and no association for regular multivitamin use (SNIPPET).
- **Limitations:** observational (association, not cause); men only; supplement use self-reported. Hoffer LJ, JAMA Intern Med 2013;173(14):1384 (OPENED) argued the outcome (ICD N20) is nonspecific, the risk is "almost certainly overestimated," and that confounding by indication or more testing among supplement users may inflate the count.

## 5. Agency and reference pages
- **NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Vitamin C:** https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/ (blocked, 403; SNIPPET only). Search extracts state: smokers need 35 mg more than non-smokers; adult tolerable upper limit 2,000 mg/day from all sources, with lower limits for children and teens; regular supplements might modestly shorten colds or ease symptoms, but starting after symptoms does not appear helpful.
- **Mayo Clinic, Vitamin C:** https://www.mayoclinic.org/drugs-supplements/vitamin-c/art-20363932 (SNIPPET). RDA 90 mg adult men, 75 mg adult women; oral supplements may cause kidney stones, mainly at high doses.
- **Orange juice content (USDA FoodData Central values via nutrition tables):** raw orange juice 1 cup (248 g) about 124 mg vitamin C; chilled/from concentrate about 82 mg; canned unsweetened about 86 mg (SNIPPET from UH Hospitals nutrition tables, which reproduce USDA data). Script says "roughly 80 to 125 mg".
- General statements in the script (vitamin C is not made by the body; common food sources are citrus, peppers, strawberries, broccoli; severe deficiency is a real problem) are standard nutrition background that I could not re-verify on the blocked ODS page.
- Own arithmetic in the script: 8 g = 8,000 mg = 4 × 2,000 mg; 2,000 mg ÷ 124 mg per cup ≈ 16 cups; 8% of a ten-day cold ≈ 0.8 day. Illustrative, labeled as such.

## Honest verdict
- **Well supported:** In the general population, taking at least 200 mg of vitamin C daily does not meaningfully reduce how many colds people catch (RR 0.97, CI up to 1.00). Daily use is linked with colds about 8% shorter in adults and 14% shorter in children, and somewhat milder symptoms. Starting after symptoms begin shows no consistent benefit across 7 comparisons. People under brief heavy physical stress (marathon runners, skiers, soldiers in subarctic conditions) had roughly half the colds (RR 0.48). Observational data link high-dose supplements with kidney stones in men.
- **Uncertain:** Whether orange juice or whole oranges affect colds (not tested in the review). Whether high-dose therapy (8 g) works; single large trial positive but few trials. How the findings apply to adults over 60, since trials pooled all ages. The size of the kidney stone risk (likely overestimated per critics).
- **False or unsupported:** That vitamin C or orange juice reliably prevents colds or "boosts immunity" enough to stop them in ordinary people; that more is always safer.
