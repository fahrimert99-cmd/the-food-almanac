# Research notes: Grapefruit and medications after 60

Topic: why grapefruit can interfere with common medicines; which US drugs are affected; why spacing the dose does not help; which citrus is safe.

Access note: PubMed and PMC blocked direct page fetches (cookie and captcha walls) during this session. Figures below were read from abstracts, publisher pages, the FDA page and search-result summaries of those abstracts. Where a number came only through a search summary, that is marked "(via summary)". Nothing is cited that was not at least seen in this way.

---

## 1. FDA consumer page: mechanism, drug examples, other citrus
- Source: US Food and Drug Administration. "Grapefruit Juice and Some Drugs Don't Mix." FDA Consumer Update. https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/grapefruit-juice-and-some-drugs-dont-mix (fetched and read).
- Mechanism: many drugs are broken down by the enzyme CYP3A4 in the small intestine. "Grapefruit juice can block the action of intestinal CYP3A4," so more drug enters the blood and stays longer. Enzyme levels vary between people, so effects vary.
- Examples listed by the FDA:
  - statins: Zocor (simvastatin), Lipitor (atorvastatin);
  - high blood pressure: Procardia and Adalat CC (nifedipine);
  - transplant rejection: Neoral and Sandimmune (cyclosporine);
  - anti-anxiety: BuSpar (buspirone);
  - Crohn's/ulcerative colitis steroid: Entocort EC and Uceris (budesonide);
  - abnormal heart rhythms: Pacerone and Cordarone (amiodarone);
  - antihistamine: Allegra (fexofenadine), where grapefruit instead reduces absorption (transporter effect), so the drug may work less well.
- "Grapefruit does not affect every drug in these classes."
- No safe amount is given: the interaction depends on the person, the drug and the amount. Ask a provider.
- Other citrus: Seville oranges, pomelos and tangelos "might have the same effect as grapefruit juice." The FDA page does not name any citrus as safe.
- Advice: ask your doctor or pharmacist; check the medication guide or Drug Facts label; check whether juice blends contain grapefruit.
- Limitation: consumer page; does not give numbers or timing.

## 2. Bailey 1991: the original observation (felodipine, nifedipine)
- Bailey DG, Spence JD, Munoz C, Arnold JMO. Interaction of citrus juices with felodipine and nifedipine. Lancet. 1991;337:268-269. PubMed 1671113, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1671113/ (abstract seen via search summary).
- Design: small crossover experiments. Felodipine: 6 men with borderline hypertension took felodipine 5 mg with water, grapefruit juice or orange juice. Nifedipine: 6 healthy men took nifedipine 10 mg with water or grapefruit juice.
- Felodipine bioavailability with grapefruit juice: mean 284% (range 164-469%) of that with water. Blood pressure was lower, heart rate higher, and vasodilation side effects more frequent. Orange juice had no such effects.
- Nifedipine with grapefruit juice: 134% (range 108-169%) of that with water.
- Limitations: tiny samples, men only, single doses, young-to-middle-aged adults (not older adults), pharmacokinetic study. The orange juice type was not specified in what I saw (so "Valencia" cannot be attributed to it).

## 3. Lown 1997: mechanism in the gut wall
- Lown KS, Bailey DG, Fontana RJ, et al. Grapefruit juice increases felodipine oral availability in humans by decreasing intestinal CYP3A protein expression. J Clin Invest. 1997;99(10):2545-2553. doi:10.1172/JCI119439; PMID 9153299.
- Design: 10 healthy men drank grapefruit juice three times daily for 6 days; small-bowel biopsies and liver CYP3A4 breath test before/after.
- Result: intestinal CYP3A4 protein fell by 62% (mRNA unchanged); liver CYP3A4 activity did not change. The authors conclude grapefruit juice selectively reduces CYP3A4 in the small intestine (via summary of abstract).
- Limitations: healthy men, n=10, repeated heavy juice dosing.

## 4. Duration and why spacing does not help
- Maskalyk J. Grapefruit juice: potential drug interactions. CMAJ. 2002;167(3):279-280. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC117477/ (fetched and read).
  - Intestinal CYP3A4 reduction begins within 4 hours of ingestion and lasts up to 24 hours.
  - "Separating the intake of the drug and the juice does not prevent interference."
  - As little as 250 mL of juice can alter metabolism of some drugs; whole fruit has the effect too.
  - "There is no similar reaction with orange juice."
- AAFP review (via search summary) recommends avoiding grapefruit for up to 72 hours before a relevant drug, citing a study with effects of 24 to 72 hours, and says timing separation is not workable. Sources differ (24 hours vs up to 72), so the script says "a day or more".
- Mechanism of the long duration: furanocoumarins (bergamottin and 6',7'-dihydroxybergamottin) inactivate intestinal CYP3A4 irreversibly; recovery needs new enzyme synthesis (via summaries; the exact wording differs by source).
- Limitation: exact duration varies by person, juice strength and drug.

## 5. Statins
- Lilja JJ, Neuvonen M, Neuvonen PJ. Effects of regular consumption of grapefruit juice on the pharmacokinetics of simvastatin. Br J Clin Pharmacol. 2004;58(1):56-60. PMC1884539 (abstract seen via summary).
  - Randomized crossover, 10 healthy volunteers; 200 mL grapefruit juice or water daily for 3 days; single 40 mg simvastatin dose on day 3.
  - Simvastatin AUC(0-24) rose about 3.6-fold, simvastatin acid 3.3-fold; Cmax about 3.9-fold and 4.3-fold. Authors: even one glass daily considerably increases concentrations.
  - Limitation: healthy volunteers (not older adults), n=10, single dose, drug levels not outcomes.
- Atorvastatin: the effect is smaller. Via summary: a study of patients taking atorvastatin with about 300 mL grapefruit juice daily (Br J Clin Pharmacol 2011;72(3)) found only a modest rise, roughly 1.3-fold; a trial protocol quoting the label says the effect is especially a concern above 1.2 liters per day. Not verified at primary level, so the script says only "the effect is smaller for atorvastatin than for simvastatin, but the FDA still lists both."
- Harvard Health ("Grapefruit juice and statins," read): not all statins are affected equally; experts say a small glass is probably okay for some people, because harmful findings used a quart or more of juice; check with your doctor. Not used for numbers.
- Safety concern: muscle injury (myopathy, rhabdomyolysis) with high statin levels (CMAJ 2013 review lists rhabdomyolysis among serious effects).

## 6. Heart-rhythm drug
- Libersa CC, Brique SA, Motte KB, et al. Dramatic inhibition of amiodarone metabolism induced by grapefruit juice. Br J Clin Pharmacol. 2000;49(4):373-378. PMC2014943 (via summary).
- 11 healthy volunteers, single oral dose of amiodarone with grapefruit juice vs water. Amiodarone AUC rose about 50% and Cmax 84%; production of the active metabolite N-DEA was completely inhibited. Authors note this decreased the drug's effects on PR and QTc intervals.
- Limitation: single dose, n=11, healthy volunteers; the direction of clinical effect is less clear than for statins. The script says only "levels rose by about half".

## 7. Review: scale and older adults; counter-view
- Bailey DG, Dresser G, Arnold JMO. Grapefruit-medication interactions: forbidden fruit or avoidable consequences? CMAJ. 2013;185(4):309-316. doi:10.1503/cmaj.120951; PMID 23184849; PMC3589309 (via summaries; direct fetch blocked).
- The number of interacting drugs with potentially serious effects rose from 17 to 43 between 2008 and 2012; serious effects include torsade de pointes, rhabdomyolysis, myelotoxicity, respiratory depression, GI bleeding, nephrotoxicity. Older adults are described as especially exposed: large groups of grapefruit buyers and prescription users, and they may tolerate high drug levels less well.
- Counter-view: Greenblatt DJ, Derendorf H letter in CMAJ 2013 argued there is no validated evidence that coadministration has caused serious actual harm and that the review's list reflects predicted risk; the letter's authors disclosed ties to the Florida Department of Citrus (via summary).
- Limitation: much of the evidence is pharmacokinetic (drug levels), not hard outcomes; case reports exist but are rare.

## 8. Other citrus
- Malhotra S, Bailey DG, Paine MF, Watkins PB. Seville orange juice-felodipine interaction: comparison with dilute grapefruit juice and involvement of furocoumarins. Clin Pharmacol Ther. 2001;69(1):14-23. (via summary)
  - Randomized three-way crossover, 10 volunteers, felodipine 10 mg ER with 240 mL Seville orange juice, dilute grapefruit juice or common orange juice (control).
  - Felodipine AUC rose 76% with Seville orange juice and 93% with grapefruit juice vs common orange juice.
- Dugrand-Judek A, et al. The distribution of coumarins and furanocoumarins in Citrus species closely matches Citrus phylogeny. PLoS ONE. 2015;10(11):e0142757. (abstract via summary)
  - 61 Citrus species; pummelos, citrons and papedas make high amounts, mandarins are practically devoid. Peel has higher concentrations than pulp.
- Lemons: not tested directly in anything I could open. Lemons descend partly from citron, and peel is richer in these compounds than juice, so the script says lemon juice is generally considered low-risk, but the evidence is thinner than for oranges and zest/peel is not the same as juice. A commercial guide (Evigrade 2026) lists sweet orange, mandarin, lemon and lime as minimal-effect; low reliability, not cited on screen.
- Valencia: "Valencia" specifically was not found in any source. The sources show common/sweet orange juice had no effect (Bailey 1991; Maskalyk 2002; Malhotra 2001 control). The script says "common sweet oranges, such as Valencia" only as an example of a sweet orange; flagged here as an inference. (Valencia is a sweet orange cultivar, Citrus sinensis, the same species as the "common orange juice" used.)

## Honest verdict
**Well supported**
- Grapefruit juice raises blood levels of certain oral drugs by blocking intestinal CYP3A4 (FDA; Lown 1997; Bailey 1991; Lilja 2004).
- The effect lasts many hours, so taking the drug at a different time of day does not avoid it (Maskalyk 2002; secondary sources).
- Specific named examples (simvastatin, atorvastatin, nifedipine, amiodarone, cyclosporine, buspirone, budesonide) are on the FDA's list; fexofenadine is the reverse case.
- Common orange juice did not show the effect in the felodipine studies; Seville orange, pomelo and tangelo may (FDA; Malhotra 2001).

**Uncertain**
- Size of the effect in real older patients: most human studies used a few healthy volunteers and single doses. Atorvastatin looks modest; simvastatin and felodipine larger.
- Exact duration (about 24 hours up to 72 hours).
- How much real-world harm occurs (Bailey and colleagues say serious; Greenblatt and Derendorf dispute documented cases).
- Lemon and lime: probably low risk for juice, but not directly tested in sources I could open.
- Whether any amount of grapefruit is safe: depends on drug and person; FDA gives no safe amount.

**False or misleading**
- "Take your pill in the morning and eat grapefruit at night." Timing does not remove the effect.
- "All medicines are affected." Many are not; the FDA says not every drug in a class interacts.
- "All citrus is dangerous." Common oranges did not show the effect.
- "Stop your medicine if you love grapefruit." Never; ask a pharmacist.
